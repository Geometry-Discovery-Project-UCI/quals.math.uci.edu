::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Can $Q_8$ be expressed as a nontrivial semidirect product of two groups?

#proof
No. In a nontrivial semidirect decomposition $Q_8=N\rtimes H$, the orders of $N$ and $H$ multiply to $8$ and their intersection is trivial. Both factors have even order unless one is trivial, so each contains an element of order $2$. But $Q_8$ has a unique element of order $2$, namely $-1$. Therefore $-1$ would lie in both $N$ and $H$, contradicting $N\cap H=1$. Thus only a trivial decomposition involving the identity group is possible.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $|G|=p^2q$, where $p<q$ are primes. Prove that either $G$ has a normal Sylow $q$-subgroup or $G\cong A_4$.

#proof
Sylow's theorems give

$$
n_q\equiv1\pmod q,
\qquad
n_q\mid p^2.
$$

Thus $n_q=1$ or $p^2$. In the second case, $q$ divides $p^2-1=(p-1)(p+1)$. Since $q>p$, it must divide $p+1$, so $q=p+1$. The only consecutive primes with the smaller one prime are $p=2$, $q=3$. Hence $|G|=12$ and $n_3=4$.

The four Sylow $3$-subgroups contribute eight nonidentity elements, leaving exactly three nonidentity elements. Any Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $4$ and must consist of the identity and those three elements, so it is unique and normal. Call it $P$. It cannot be cyclic: if $P\cong C_4$, then the conjugation action of a subgroup $C_3$ on $P$ would be trivial because $\operatorname{Aut}(C_4)\cong C_2$, making the Sylow $3$-subgroup normal. Therefore $P\cong V_4$. The nontrivial action of $C_3$ cyclically permutes the three nonidentity elements of $V_4$, and the resulting semidirect product is $A_4$. Thus $G\cong A_4$.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $N(R)$ be the set of nilpotent elements of a ring $R$.

**(a)** If $R$ is commutative, prove that $N(R)$ is an ideal and equals the intersection of all prime ideals.

**(b)** Give a noncommutative example in which $N(R)$ is not an ideal.

#proof
**(a)** If $x^m=0$ and $y^n=0$, every term of $(x+y)^{m+n}$ vanishes, so $x+y$ is nilpotent. Also $(rx)^m=r^mx^m=0$, so $N(R)$ is an ideal.

Every prime ideal contains every nilpotent: if $x^m\in P$, primality applied repeatedly gives $x\in P$. Hence $N(R)$ is contained in the intersection of the prime ideals. Conversely, if $x$ is not nilpotent, the multiplicative set $\{1,x,x^2,\ldots\}$ does not contain zero. An ideal maximal among those disjoint from this set is prime, and it does not contain $x$. Thus $x$ is not in the intersection, proving equality.

**(b)** In $M_2(F)$, the matrices

$$
E_{12}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
E_{21}=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

are nilpotent, but

$$
E_{12}+E_{21}=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$

has square $I$. Hence the nilpotent elements are not closed under addition.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Work in $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]$.

**(a)** Is $3+\sqrt2$ prime?

**(b)** Identify $\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]/(3+\sqrt2)$.

#proof
The norm is

$$
N(a+b\sqrt2)=|a^2-2b^2|.
$$

Thus $N(3+\sqrt2)=7$. Since $7$ is prime in $\mathbb Z$, multiplicativity of the norm shows that any factorization of $3+\sqrt2$ has a unit factor. Hence it is irreducible. The ring is Euclidean and therefore a PID, so irreducible elements are prime.

In the quotient, $\sqrt2=-3$. The relation $(\sqrt2)^2=2$ becomes $9=2$, hence $7=0$. Define

$$
\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]\to\mathbb F_7,
\qquad
a+b\sqrt2\mapsto a-3b\pmod7.
$$

This is surjective and its kernel is $(3+\sqrt2)$. Therefore

$$
\mathbb Z[\sqrt2]/(3+\sqrt2)\cong\mathbb F_7.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $I\neq J$ be maximal ideals of a commutative ring $R$.

**(a)** Prove every element of $R/(I\cap J)$ is a unit or a zero divisor.

**(b)** Prove this quotient has a unit and at least two nonzero zero divisors.

**(c)** Prove it has infinitely many units if and only if it has infinitely many zero divisors.

#proof
Distinct maximal ideals are comaximal, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives

$$
R/(I\cap J)\cong R/I\times R/J=F\times K,
$$

where $F,K$ are fields.

An element $(a,b)$ is a unit exactly when both coordinates are nonzero. If at least one coordinate is zero, it is a zero divisor. This proves **(a)**. The element $(1,1)$ is a unit, while $(1,0)$ and $(0,1)$ are distinct nonzero zero divisors, proving **(b)**.

If both fields are finite, the whole product is finite. If either field is infinite, varying a nonzero coordinate gives infinitely many units and varying one coordinate along an axis gives infinitely many zero divisors. This proves **(c)**.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
If $F\subseteq K\subseteq L$, with $K/F$ and $L/K$ algebraic, prove that $L/F$ is algebraic.

#proof
Let $\alpha\in L$. It satisfies a polynomial over $K$ with finitely many coefficients $c_1,\ldots,c_r\in K$. Since each $c_j$ is algebraic over $F$, the extension

$$
E=F(c_1,\ldots,c_r)
$$

is finite over $F$. The element $\alpha$ is algebraic over $E$, so $E(\alpha)/E$ is finite. Therefore $E(\alpha)/F$ is finite by the tower law, and $\alpha$ is algebraic over $F$. Since $\alpha$ was arbitrary, $L/F$ is algebraic.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Calculate the Galois group over $\mathbb Q$ of

$$
x^4-3x^2+4.
$$

#proof
Let

$$
a=\sqrt{\frac{3+\sqrt{-7}}2},
\qquad
b=\sqrt{\frac{3-\sqrt{-7}}2}.
$$

The roots are $\pm a,\pm b$, and $ab=2$. Hence $b=2/a$ and the splitting field is $K=\mathbb Q(a)$. The quartic is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$: a factorization into rational quadratics would force either $(3\pm\sqrt{-7})/2$ or $7$ to be a rational square. Thus $[K:\mathbb Q]=4$.

The automorphisms may independently apply

$$
s:a\mapsto-a, b\mapsto-b
$$

and

$$
t:a\mapsto b, b\mapsto a.
$$

Both have order $2$ and they commute. These four automorphisms account for the full degree, so

$$
\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong C_2\times C_2.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $K\subseteq\mathbb C$ be Galois over $\mathbb Q$ with cyclic Galois group of order $4$. Prove that $i\notin K$.

#proof
If $i\in K$, then $\mathbb Q(i)$ would be a quadratic subfield. A cyclic group of order $4$ has a unique subgroup of order $2$, so $K$ has a unique quadratic subfield.

Complex conjugation restricts to the unique element of order $2$ in $\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)$. Its fixed field is also quadratic and is contained in $\mathbb R$. Uniqueness would force this real field to equal $\mathbb Q(i)$, which is impossible. Hence $i\notin K$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $V$ be finite dimensional over $\mathbb F_2$, and let $T^5=I$ but $T\neq I$.

**(a)** What are the possible degrees of the minimal polynomial of $T$?

**(b)** What is the smallest dimension in which two nonsimilar such transformations can exist?

#proof
Over $\mathbb F_2$,

$$
x^5-1=(x-1)(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1),
$$

and the quartic factor is irreducible because the order of $2$ modulo $5$ is $4$. The product is squarefree. Since $T\neq I$, its minimal polynomial contains the quartic factor. Thus its minimal polynomial is either the quartic, of degree $4$, or all of $x^5-1$, of degree $5$.

The corresponding modules are semisimple. Below dimension $8$, a nontrivial module consists of one four-dimensional irreducible summand together with a uniquely determined number of trivial one-dimensional summands, so its similarity class is unique in each dimension. In dimension $8$, there are two possibilities:

$$
W\oplus W
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
W\oplus\mathbb F_2^4,
$$

where $W$ is the irreducible four-dimensional module. Their minimal polynomials are respectively the quartic and $(x-1)$ times the quartic, so they are not similar. The smallest dimension is therefore $8$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Prove that every complex square matrix is similar to its transpose.

#proof
Put $A$ in Jordan normal form:

$$
A=PJP^{-1}.
$$

For each Jordan block, conjugation by the permutation matrix that reverses the basis order carries the block to its transpose. Therefore $J$ is similar to $J^T$. Transposing the displayed similarity gives

$$
A^T=(P^{-1})^TJ^TP^T.
$$

Thus $A^T$ is similar to $J^T$, which is similar to $J$, which is similar to $A$. Hence $A$ and $A^T$ are similar.
