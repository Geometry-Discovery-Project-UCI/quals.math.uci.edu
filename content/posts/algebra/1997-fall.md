::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ be finite and suppose that for any subgroups $H,K$, either $H\subseteq K$ or $K\subseteq H$. Prove that $G$ is cyclic of order $p^n$ for some prime $p$.

#proof
If two distinct primes $p,q$ divided $|G|$, Cauchy's theorem would give subgroups of orders $p$ and $q$, neither containing the other. Thus $|G|=p^n$.

Choose an element $g$ of maximal order. For any $x\in G$, the cyclic subgroups $\langle x\rangle$ and $\langle g\rangle$ are comparable. Maximality of the order rules out $\langle g\rangle\subsetneq\langle x\rangle$, so $\langle x\rangle\subseteq\langle g\rangle$. Hence every element lies in $\langle g\rangle$, and $G$ is cyclic.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Prove that no group of order $6\cdot5^n$, $n\ge2$, is simple.

#proof
The number of Sylow $5$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_5\equiv1\pmod5,
\qquad
n_5\mid6,
$$
so $n_5=1$ or $6$. If it is $1$, the Sylow subgroup is normal.

If $n_5=6$, conjugation on the six Sylow subgroups gives a nontrivial homomorphism $G\to S_6$. Were $G$ simple, its kernel would be trivial, so $|G|$ would divide $|S_6|=720$. But $5^n$ with $n\ge2$ does not divide $720$, which contains only one factor of $5$. This is impossible.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $H\le GL_2(F)$ stabilize the set of vectors $\{(x,0)^t:x\in F\}$. Prove that $H$ is solvable.

#proof
The stabilizer consists exactly of the invertible upper triangular matrices
$$
H=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{pmatrix}:a,d\in F^*,\ b\in F\right\}.
$$
Its normal subgroup
$$
U=\left\{\begin{pmatrix}1&b\\0&1\end{pmatrix}:b\in F\right\}
$$
is abelian. The quotient by $U$ is isomorphic to the abelian diagonal group $F^*\times F^*$. Hence $[H,H]\subseteq U$ and $[U,U]=1$, so the derived series reaches the identity after at most two steps.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Over $\mathbb R[X]$, find the monic gcd of
$$
f=X^4-X^2+2X-1,
\qquad
g=X^4+2X^3+X^2-1,
$$
and find $u,v$ such that $d=uf+vg$.

#proof
The Euclidean algorithm gives the monic gcd
$$
d(X)=X^2+X-1.
$$
Indeed, direct expansion verifies the Bézout identity
$$
X^2+X-1
=\frac{X+1}{2}f(X)+\frac{1-X}{2}g(X).
$$
Thus one may take
$$
u(X)=\frac{X+1}{2},
\qquad
v(X)=\frac{1-X}{2}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Determine the unit group of $\mathbb F_5[u]$ subject to $u^3=1$.

#proof
The ring is $\mathbb F_5[u]/(u^3-1)$. Since
$$
u^3-1=(u-1)(u^2+u+1)
$$
and the quadratic factor is irreducible over $\mathbb F_5$, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
$$
\mathbb F_5[u]/(u^3-1)\cong\mathbb F_5\times\mathbb F_{25}.
$$
Therefore its group of units is
$$
\mathbb F_5^*\times\mathbb F_{25}^*
\cong C_4\times C_{24}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $D=\mathbb Z[i]$, $M=D^3$, and let $K$ be generated by
$$
(1,2,1),\qquad(0,0,5),\qquad(1,-i,6).
$$
Prove that $M/K$ is finite and find its order.

#proof
Use these generators as columns of
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&1\\2&0&-i\\1&5&6\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its determinant is
$$
\det A=10+5i=5(2+i)\ne0.
$$
Thus $K$ has full rank and the quotient is finite. For a full-rank sublattice over the Gaussian integers, the underlying abelian-group index is the Gaussian norm of the determinant. Hence
$$
|M/K|=N(10+5i)=10^2+5^2=125.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $E$ be the splitting field of $X^{35}-1$ over $\mathbb F_8$. Determine $|E|$ and the number of its subfields.

#proof
Because $35$ is odd, the roots are distinct. The splitting field is $\mathbb F_{8^m}$, where $m$ is the multiplicative order of $8$ modulo $35$. We have
$$
8^2\equiv29,
\qquad
8^4\equiv1\pmod{35},
$$
and no smaller positive exponent gives $1$. Thus $m=4$ and
$$
E=\mathbb F_{8^4}=\mathbb F_{2^{12}},
\qquad
|E|=4096.
$$
The subfields of $\mathbb F_{2^{12}}$ are precisely $\mathbb F_{2^d}$ for $d\mid12$. Since $12$ has six positive divisors, $E$ has six subfields. Of these, the three corresponding to $d=3,6,12$ contain the base field $\mathbb F_8$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Find the degree over $\mathbb Q$ of the splitting field of $X^{10}-5$.

#proof
Let $\alpha=5^{1/10}$ and $\zeta=\zeta_{10}$. The splitting field is
$$
E=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta).
$$
Eisenstein at $5$ gives $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=10$, while $[\mathbb Q(\zeta):\mathbb Q]=4$. Both fields contain
$$
\sqrt5=\alpha^5,
$$
and the unique quadratic subfield of $\mathbb Q(\zeta_{10})=\mathbb Q(\zeta_5)$ is $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$. Hence their intersection is exactly $\mathbb Q(\sqrt5)$ and has degree $2$. Therefore
$$
[E:\mathbb Q]=\frac{10\cdot4}{2}=20.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $f\in F[X]$ be separable and irreducible of degree $4$. Determine the Galois group of its splitting field when that group has order $8$.

#proof
The Galois group acts faithfully and transitively on the four roots, so it is a transitive subgroup of $S_4$ of order $8$. Such a subgroup is a Sylow $2$-subgroup of $S_4$. All Sylow $2$-subgroups are conjugate, and a standard one is the symmetry group of a square, isomorphic to the dihedral group $D_8$. Hence
$$
G\cong D_8.
$$
In its action on the roots, it is conjugate in $S_4$ to the usual action of $D_8$ on the four vertices of a square.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
For
$$
f(X)=X^3+X^2-2X-1,
$$
show that its splitting field over $\mathbb Q$ is obtained by adjoining one root, and find its Galois group.

#proof
The rational-root test shows that $f$ has no rational root, so it is irreducible. A direct computation shows
$$
f(X)\mid f(X^2-2).
$$
If $\alpha$ is a root, then $\alpha^2-2$ is also a root. Iterating this transformation produces all three roots. Hence all roots lie in $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$, so this degree-$3$ field is already the splitting field.

Since the splitting field has degree $3$, its Galois group has order $3$ and therefore
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(E/\mathbb Q)\cong C_3.
$$
The generator cyclically permutes the three roots by $\alpha\mapsto\alpha^2-2$.
::
