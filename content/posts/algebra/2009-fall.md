::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Prove that there are exactly four groups of order $28$ up to isomorphism. How many are nonabelian?

#proof
Let $G$ have order $28=4\cdot7$. Sylow's theorems give
$$
n_7\equiv1\pmod7,
\qquad n_7\mid4,
$$
so the Sylow $7$-subgroup $N\cong C_7$ is normal. By Schur--Zassenhaus,
$$
G\cong C_7\rtimes H,
$$
where $H$ has order $4$ and is therefore $C_4$ or $C_2\times C_2$.

Now $\operatorname{Aut}(C_7)\cong C_6$, so an action of $H$ has image of order at most $2$. For each choice of $H$, there is a trivial action and, up to automorphisms of $H$, one nontrivial action onto $C_2$, whose nonidentity element acts on $C_7$ by inversion. Hence the four groups are
$$
C_{28},
$$
$$
C_7\times C_2\times C_2\cong C_{14}\times C_2,
$$
$$
C_7\rtimes C_4
\quad\text{with a generator acting by inversion},
$$
and
$$
(C_7\rtimes C_2)\times C_2\cong D_{14}\times C_2.
$$
The first two are abelian and the last two are nonabelian. Thus exactly two are nonabelian.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Prove that there is no simple group of order $30$.

#proof
Let $G$ have order $30$. The number of Sylow $5$-subgroups is $1$ or $6$, and the number of Sylow $3$-subgroups is $1$ or $10$. If either number is $1$, $G$ has a nontrivial proper normal subgroup.

Otherwise, the six Sylow $5$-subgroups contribute
$$
6(5-1)=24
$$
nonidentity elements, and the ten Sylow $3$-subgroups contribute
$$
10(3-1)=20
$$
different nonidentity elements. These two sets are disjoint, giving more than $29$ nonidentity elements, which is impossible. Hence one Sylow subgroup is normal and $G$ is not simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
(a) Give an infinite group in which every element has finite order.

(b) How many solutions does
$$
x^n+x^{n-1}+\cdots+x+1=0
$$
have in $\mathbb F_q$?

#proof
(a) The additive group $\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$ is infinite, and every element has finite order.

(b) Let the characteristic be $p$. For $x\ne1$, the equation is equivalent to
$$
x^{n+1}=1.
$$
The cyclic group $\mathbb F_q^*$ contains exactly
$$
d=\gcd(n+1,q-1)
$$
solutions to this latter equation, one of which is $1$. The value $x=1$ solves the original equation exactly when
$$
n+1=0\quad\text{in }\mathbb F_q,
$$
that is, when $p\mid n+1$. Therefore the number of solutions is
$$
\gcd(n+1,q-1)-1+
\begin{cases}
1,&p\mid n+1,\\
0,&p\nmid n+1.
\end{cases}
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $A$ be a commutative ring with identity, let $S$ be a nonempty multiplicative subset with $0\notin S$, and let $P$ be maximal among ideals disjoint from $S$. Prove that $P$ is prime.

#proof
Suppose $ab\in P$ but neither $a$ nor $b$ lies in $P$. By maximality, both larger ideals $P+(a)$ and $P+(b)$ meet $S$. Choose
$$
s_1=p_1+ra\in S,
\qquad
s_2=p_2+tb\in S,
$$
where $p_1,p_2\in P$. Then
$$
s_1s_2=p_1p_2+p_1tb+p_2ra+rtab\in P.
$$
But $S$ is multiplicative, so $s_1s_2\in S$, contradicting $P\cap S=\varnothing$. Thus $a\in P$ or $b\in P$, and $P$ is prime.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity and let $A,B\in M_n(R)$. Show that
$$
\det(I-ABt)=\det(I-BAt).
$$

#proof
Consider the block matrix over $R[t]$
$$
M=\begin{bmatrix}I&At\\B&I\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Using block row elimination with the upper-left identity block gives
$$
\det M=\det(I-BAt).
$$
Using the lower-right identity block instead gives
$$
\det M=\det(I-AtB)=\det(I-ABt),
$$
because $t$ is central. Equating the two expressions proves the identity.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $p>2$ be prime and let $T$ act on a finite-dimensional rational vector space $V$ whose dimension is not divisible by $p-1$. Show that
$$
T^{p-1}+T^{p-2}+\cdots+T+I\ne0.
$$

#proof
Suppose the displayed operator were zero. Then $V$ would be a module over
$$
\mathbb Q[x]/(\Phi_p(x)),
$$
because
$$
\Phi_p(x)=x^{p-1}+\cdots+x+1.
$$
The cyclotomic polynomial $\Phi_p$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ and has degree $p-1$, so this quotient is a field of degree $p-1$ over $\mathbb Q$. Consequently
$$
\dim_{\mathbb Q}V=(p-1)\dim_{\mathbb Q[x]/(\Phi_p)}V,
$$
contradicting the assumption. Hence the operator is nonzero.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $K_1,K_2$ be extensions of $K$, with $K_1/K$ finite and separable. Show that
$$
K_1\otimes_KK_2
$$
is a direct product of fields as a $K$-algebra.

#proof
By the primitive element theorem, write
$$
K_1=K(\alpha)
$$
with separable minimal polynomial $f(x)\in K[x]$. Then
$$
K_1\otimes_KK_2\cong K_2[x]/(f).
$$
Over $K_2$, factor
$$
f=f_1\cdots f_r
$$
into distinct monic irreducibles. The factors remain distinct because $f$ is separable. By the Chinese remainder theorem,
$$
K_2[x]/(f)\cong
\prod_{j=1}^rK_2[x]/(f_j).
$$
Each factor is a field, proving the result.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
For finite-dimensional irreducible complex representations of a finite group $G$:

(a) Show that if $G$ is abelian, every irreducible representation has degree $1$.

(b) Show that the number of degree-$1$ representations is $|G/[G,G]|$.

#proof
(a) If $G$ is abelian, all operators $\rho(g)$ commute. Over $\mathbb C$, they have a common eigenvector. The span of that vector is a nonzero invariant subspace, so irreducibility forces the entire representation to be one-dimensional.

(b) Every one-dimensional representation annihilates the commutator subgroup and therefore corresponds to a homomorphism
$$
G/[G,G]\longrightarrow\mathbb C^*.
$$
Conversely, every such homomorphism gives a one-dimensional representation. A finite abelian group has exactly as many complex characters as elements, so the number is
$$
|G/[G,G]|.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $F$ be algebraically closed. Find all monic separable polynomials $f(x)\in F[x]$ whose zero set is closed under multiplication.

#proof
Because $f$ is monic and separable, it is determined by its finite set $S$ of distinct roots. If $S$ contains a nonzero element, then $S\cap F^*$ is a finite multiplicatively closed subset of a group. Powers of each element eventually repeat, showing that this set contains $1$ and inverses; hence it is a finite subgroup of $F^*$ and therefore cyclic. Thus
$$
S\cap F^*=\mu_n(F)
$$
for some $n$ not divisible by $\operatorname{char}F$.

The root set may either omit or include $0$. Therefore the nonconstant possibilities are
$$
x,
\qquad x^n-1,
\qquad x(x^n-1),
$$
where $n\ge1$ and, in positive characteristic, the characteristic does not divide $n$. These polynomials are monic and separable, and their root sets are closed under multiplication. If constant polynomials are allowed, $f=1$ is the additional empty-root-set case.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Compute the Galois group over $\mathbb Q$ of
$$
f(x)=x^5-4x+2.
$$

#proof
The polynomial is Eisenstein at $2$, so it is irreducible. Hence its Galois group $G$ acts transitively on five roots, and therefore $5$ divides $|G|$. By Cauchy's theorem, $G$ contains a $5$-cycle.

Modulo $7$, one has
$$
f(x)=(x^2+4x+6)(x^3+3x^2+3x+5).
$$
Both factors are irreducible over $\mathbb F_7$ and are distinct, so this is a square-free factorization of type $(2,3)$. The Frobenius cycle-type theorem implies that $G$ contains an element with cycle type $(2)(3)$. Its cube is a transposition.

Conjugating this transposition by powers of the $5$-cycle produces transpositions whose associated graph on the five roots is connected; such transpositions generate $S_5$. Consequently
$$
G\cong S_5.
$$
