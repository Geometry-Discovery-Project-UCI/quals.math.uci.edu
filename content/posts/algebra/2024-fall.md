::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ be a group and let

$$
H=\{(g,g):g\in G\}\leq G\times G.
$$

Prove that $H\triangleleft G\times G$ if and only if $G$ is abelian.

#proof
Suppose first that $G$ is abelian. Then $G\times G$ is abelian, so every subgroup, including $H$, is normal.

Conversely, suppose $H$ is normal. For $a,b,g\in G$,

$$
(a,b)(g,g)(a,b)^{-1}=(aga^{-1},bgb^{-1})
$$

must lie in $H$. Hence $aga^{-1}=bgb^{-1}$. Taking $b=e$ gives $aga^{-1}=g$ for every $a,g\in G$. Thus every two elements commute, and $G$ is abelian.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $p$ be prime and $k\geq2$. Prove that no group of order $p^k(p+1)$ is simple.

#proof
Let $n_p$ be the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups. Then

$$
n_p\equiv1\pmod p,
\qquad
n_p\mid p+1.
$$

Therefore $n_p$ is either $1$ or $p+1$. If $n_p=1$, the unique Sylow $p$-subgroup is a nontrivial proper normal subgroup.

Suppose $n_p=p+1$. Conjugation gives a nontrivial homomorphism

$$
G\longrightarrow S_{p+1}.
$$

If $G$ were simple, its kernel would be trivial, so $G$ would embed in $S_{p+1}$. But the highest power of $p$ dividing $(p+1)!$ is $p^1$, whereas $p^k$ with $k\geq2$ divides $|G|$. Lagrange's theorem makes such an embedding impossible. Hence $G$ is not simple.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity. Assume $R[x]$ is a PID. Prove that $R$ is a field.

#proof
Because a PID is an integral domain, $R[x]$ and therefore $R$ are integral domains. Let $0\neq a\in R$. Since $R[x]$ is a PID,

$$
(a,x)=(f)
$$

for some $f\in R[x]$. The polynomial $f$ divides both the nonzero constant $a$ and the polynomial $x$. Since degrees add in a polynomial ring over a domain, $f$ must be constant, so $f\in R$.

Because $x\in(f)$, we have $x=fh(x)$ for some $h(x)\in R[x]$. Comparing the coefficients of $x$ gives $1=fc$ for the leading coefficient $c$ of $h$. Thus $f$ is a unit. Hence $(a,x)=R[x]$, so there exist $u(x),v(x)\in R[x]$ such that

$$
au(x)+xv(x)=1.
$$

Setting $x=0$ gives $a u(0)=1$, so $a$ is a unit. Every nonzero element of $R$ is a unit, and therefore $R$ is a field.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
**(a)** Let $R$ be commutative and let $a,b\in R$. Prove that

$$
R/(a,b)\cong (R/(a))/(\overline b).
$$

**(b)** Prove that $(5,x^2+1)$ is not maximal in $\mathbb Z[x]$.

#proof
**(a)** Consider the composite quotient map

$$
R\longrightarrow R/(a)\longrightarrow (R/(a))/(\overline b).
$$

It is surjective. Its kernel consists precisely of those $r$ whose class modulo $(a)$ belongs to the ideal generated by $\overline b$, which is equivalent to $r\in(a,b)$. The first isomorphism theorem gives the desired isomorphism.

**(b)** By part **(a)**,

$$
\mathbb Z[x]/(5,x^2+1)
\cong \mathbb F_5[x]/(x^2+1).
$$

But

$$
x^2+1=x^2-4=(x-2)(x+2)
$$

in $\mathbb F_5[x]$. The quotient therefore has zero divisors and is not a field. Consequently $(5,x^2+1)$ is not maximal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Prove that a commutative ring $R$ satisfies the ascending chain condition for ideals if and only if every ideal of $R$ is finitely generated.

#proof
Assume first that every ideal is finitely generated. Given an ascending chain

$$
I_1\subseteq I_2\subseteq\cdots,
$$

the union $I=\bigcup I_n$ is an ideal. Write $I=(a_1,\ldots,a_r)$. All finitely many generators lie in some $I_N$, so

$$
I\subseteq I_N\subseteq I.
$$

Thus $I_n=I_N$ for all $n\geq N$.

Conversely, assume ACC and let $I$ be an ideal. If $I$ were not finitely generated, choose $a_1\in I$, and after choosing $a_1,\ldots,a_n$, choose

$$
a_{n+1}\in I\setminus(a_1,\ldots,a_n).
$$

Then

$$
(a_1)\subsetneq(a_1,a_2)\subsetneq(a_1,a_2,a_3)\subsetneq\cdots
$$

would be an ascending chain that never stabilizes, contradicting ACC. Therefore every ideal is finitely generated.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $R$ be an integral domain, let $M$ be an $R$-module, and let $N\leq M$. If $N$ and $M/N$ are torsion-free, prove that $M$ is torsion-free.

#proof
Suppose $r\in R$ and $m\in M$ satisfy $rm=0$, with $r\neq0$. In the quotient,

$$
r(m+N)=rm+N=N.
$$

Since $M/N$ is torsion-free, $m+N=N$, so $m\in N$. Now the equality $rm=0$ holds inside the torsion-free module $N$. Since $r\neq0$, it follows that $m=0$. Thus $M$ is torsion-free.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $M$ be a complex $n\times n$ matrix whose characteristic polynomial has no repeated roots, and suppose all its roots are roots of unity.

**(a)** Prove that $M^k=I_n$ for some $k>0$.

**(b)** Can one necessarily take $k=n$?

**(c)** Does **(a)** remain true without separability?

#proof
**(a)** A characteristic polynomial with distinct roots splits over $\mathbb C$, so $M$ is diagonalizable. Write

$$
M=P\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n)P^{-1}.
$$

Each $\lambda_j$ is a root of unity. If $k$ is the least common multiple of their orders, then $\lambda_j^k=1$ for every $j$, and hence $M^k=I_n$.

**(b)** No. For $n=1$, the matrix $M=(-1)$ satisfies the hypotheses, but $M^n=M\neq I_1$.

**(c)** No. Consider

$$
M=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Its only eigenvalue is the root of unity $1$, but its characteristic polynomial is not separable. For every $k>0$,

$$
M^k=\begin{pmatrix}1&k\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\neq I_2.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $F$ be a field such that every odd-degree polynomial in $F[x]$ has a root in $F$. If $K/F$ is finite and $[K:F]>1$, prove that $[K:F]$ is even.

#proof
Suppose instead that $[K:F]$ is odd. Choose $\alpha\in K\setminus F$. By the tower law,

$$
[K:F]=[K:F(\alpha)]\,[F(\alpha):F].
$$

Thus the degree of the minimal polynomial of $\alpha$ over $F$, namely $[F(\alpha):F]$, is an odd integer greater than $1$. By hypothesis, this minimal polynomial has a root in $F$. An irreducible polynomial over $F$ having a root in $F$ must be linear, a contradiction. Therefore $[K:F]$ is even.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $K/F$ be Galois of degree $25$, and suppose there are two distinct fields strictly between $F$ and $K$. How many strict intermediate fields are there in total?

#proof
Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$, so $|G|=25$. A group of order $p^2$ is either cyclic or isomorphic to $C_p\times C_p$. If $G$ were cyclic of order $25$, it would have a unique subgroup of order $5$ and hence only one strict intermediate field. The hypothesis therefore forces

$$
G\cong C_5\times C_5.
$$

The proper nontrivial subgroups of this group are exactly the one-dimensional subspaces of the two-dimensional vector space $\mathbb F_5^2$. Their number is

$$
\frac{5^2-1}{5-1}=6.
$$

By the Galois correspondence, there are exactly six strict intermediate fields.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Let $K=F(\alpha)$ be a finite Galois extension, where $\alpha\notin F$ and $\alpha\neq\alpha^{-1}$. Suppose some $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$ satisfies $\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha^{-1}$. Prove that

$$
[K:F(\alpha+\alpha^{-1})]=2.
$$

#proof
Put $t=\alpha+\alpha^{-1}$. The element $\alpha$ satisfies

$$
x^2-tx+1=0
$$

over $F(t)$. Since $K=F(\alpha)$, this shows

$$
[K:F(t)]\leq2.
$$

On the other hand,

$$
\sigma(t)=\sigma(\alpha)+\sigma(\alpha)^{-1}
=\alpha^{-1}+\alpha=t.
$$

Thus $\sigma$ fixes $F(t)$. But it does not fix $\alpha$, because $\alpha\neq\alpha^{-1}$. Hence $\alpha\notin F(t)$, so $[K:F(t)]>1$. Combining both inequalities gives $[K:F(t)]=2$.
