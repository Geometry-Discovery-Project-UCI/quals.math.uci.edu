::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
(a) Define a prime ideal.

(b) Define a Sylow $p$-subgroup.

(c) Give an example of a unique factorization domain that is not a principal ideal domain.

(d) Give an example of a commutative ring $R$ with identity and a prime ideal $M$ of $R$ that is not maximal.

#proof
(a) A proper ideal $P$ of a commutative ring $R$ is prime if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. Equivalently, $R/P$ is an integral domain.

(b) If $|G|=p^nm$ with $p\nmid m$, a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$ is a subgroup of order $p^n$.

(c) The polynomial ring $k[x,y]$ over a field $k$ is a UFD, but it is not a PID: the ideal $(x,y)$ is not principal.

(d) In $R=\mathbb Z$, the zero ideal is prime because $\mathbb Z$ is an integral domain, but it is not maximal because $\mathbb Z/(0)=\mathbb Z$ is not a field.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $L$ be the splitting field of $x^3-2$ over $\mathbb Q$.

(a) Find $[L:\mathbb Q]$.

(b) Describe $\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)$, both as an abstract group and as a set of automorphisms.

#proof
Let $\alpha=\sqrt[3]{2}$ and $\zeta=e^{2\pi i/3}$. The three roots are $\alpha,\zeta\alpha,\zeta^2\alpha$, so
$$
L=\mathbb Q(\alpha,\zeta).
$$
Eisenstein at $2$ shows that $x^3-2$ is irreducible, hence $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=3$. The field $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ is real while $\zeta$ is not, so adjoining $\zeta$ has degree $2$. Therefore
$$
[L:\mathbb Q]=6.
$$

Define
$$
r(\alpha)=\zeta\alpha,\quad r(\zeta)=\zeta,
\qquad
s(\alpha)=\alpha,\quad s(\zeta)=\zeta^{-1}.
$$
Then $r^3=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. The six automorphisms are
$$
1,r,r^2,s,sr,sr^2,
$$
so
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\cong S_3.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Suppose $A,B\in M_n(\mathbb C)$ and $AB=BA$. Prove that $A$ and $B$ have a common eigenvector.

#proof
Choose an eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$ and let
$$
E_\lambda=\ker(A-\lambda I).
$$
This is a nonzero subspace. If $v\in E_\lambda$, then
$$
 A(Bv)=B(Av)=B(\lambda v)=\lambda Bv,
$$
so $Bv\in E_\lambda$. Thus $E_\lambda$ is invariant under $B$. The restriction $B|_{E_\lambda}$ is an operator on a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space, so it has an eigenvector $0\ne w\in E_\lambda$. Then $Aw=\lambda w$ and $Bw=\mu w$ for some $\mu\in\mathbb C$, making $w$ a common eigenvector.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Suppose $G$ is a group and $H$ is a finite normal subgroup of $G$. If $G/H$ has an element of order $n$, prove that $G$ has an element of order $n$.

#proof
Let $gH$ have order $n$. Then $g^n\in H$. Since $H$ is finite, $g^n$ has finite order, so $g$ has finite order, say $d$. The order of the image of $g$ in $G/H$ divides the order of $g$, so $n\mid d$. Therefore the element
$$
g^{d/n}
$$
has order $n$ in $G$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $C$ denote the center of $GL_2(\mathbb F_3)$ and let $PGL_2(\mathbb F_3)=GL_2(\mathbb F_3)/C$.

(a) Prove that $C=\{\pm I\}$.

(b) Prove that $PGL_2(\mathbb F_3)\cong S_4$.

#proof
(a) A matrix commuting with every invertible diagonal and elementary matrix must be scalar. Hence the center of $GL_2(F)$ consists of $\lambda I$ with $\lambda\in F^\times$. Since $\mathbb F_3^\times=\{1,-1\}$,
$$
C=\{I,-I\}.
$$

(b) 
The group $PGL_2(\mathbb F_3)$ acts by fractional linear transformations on the projective line
$$
\mathbb P^1(\mathbb F_3)=\{\infty,0,1,2\},
$$
which has four points. This gives a homomorphism
$$
PGL_2(\mathbb F_3)\longrightarrow S_4.
$$
If a projective transformation fixes all four points, in particular it fixes $\infty,0,1$. A fractional linear transformation fixing these three distinct points is the identity; hence the action is faithful.

Moreover,
$$
|PGL_2(\mathbb F_3)|
=\frac{|GL_2(\mathbb F_3)|}{|\mathbb F_3^\times|}
=\frac{(3^2-1)(3^2-3)}{2}
=24.
$$
Thus its faithful image is a subgroup of $S_4$ of order $24$, so it is all of $S_4$.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Describe the ring
$$
\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+ax+b)
$$
for arbitrary real numbers $a,b$.

#proof
Let $\Delta=a^2-4b$. Completing the square gives
$$
x^2+ax+b=\left(x+\frac a2\right)^2-\frac{\Delta}{4}.
$$
There are three cases.

If $\Delta>0$, the polynomial has two distinct real roots $r_1,r_2$. The corresponding linear ideals are comaximal, and the Chinese remainder theorem gives
$$
\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+ax+b)\cong\mathbb R\times\mathbb R.
$$

If $\Delta=0$, putting $\varepsilon=x+a/2$ gives
$$
\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+ax+b)\cong\mathbb R[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^2),
$$
the ring of dual numbers.

If $\Delta<0$, put $c=\sqrt{-\Delta}/2$. The class
$$
u=\frac{x+a/2}{c}
$$
satisfies $u^2=-1$, and hence
$$
\mathbb R[x]/(x^2+ax+b)\cong\mathbb C.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $G$ be a finite group and suppose that $p^n$ divides $|G|$, where $p$ is prime and $n$ is positive. Prove that $G$ has a subgroup of order $p^n$.

#proof
Let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $G$. Since $p^n\mid|G|$, its order is $p^m$ for some $m\ge n$. It remains to show that a finite $p$-group of order $p^m$ has a subgroup of every order $p^j$, $0\le j\le m$.

Proceed by induction on $m$. A nontrivial finite $p$-group has nontrivial center, so choose a central subgroup $Z$ of order $p$. The quotient $P/Z$ has order $p^{m-1}$. If $1\le j\le m$, induction gives a subgroup $\overline H\le P/Z$ of order $p^{j-1}$. Its inverse image under $P\to P/Z$ is a subgroup of $P$ of order $p^j$. Taking $j=n$ proves the result.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Suppose $H$ and $K$ are subgroups of a group $G$, and both have finite index in $G$. Show that $H\cap K$ also has finite index in $G$.

#proof
Define a map of sets
$$
G/(H\cap K)\longrightarrow G/H\times G/K,
\qquad
g(H\cap K)\longmapsto(gH,gK).
$$
It is well-defined. It is also injective: if $gH=g'H$ and $gK=g'K$, then $g'^{-1}g\in H\cap K$, so $g(H\cap K)=g'(H\cap K)$. The target is finite, and therefore
$$
[G:H\cap K]\le [G:H][G:K]<\infty.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Describe the conjugacy classes of $GL_2(\mathbb C)$.

#proof
Every complex $2\times2$ matrix has a Jordan canonical form. An invertible one has no zero eigenvalue. Therefore every conjugacy class has exactly one representative of one of the following types, up to interchanging $\lambda$ and $\mu$ in the first type:
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&0\\0&\mu\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \lambda,\mu\in\mathbb C^\times,
$$
or
$$
\begin{pmatrix}\lambda&1\\0&\lambda\end{pmatrix},
\qquad \lambda\in\mathbb C^\times.
$$
The first family includes the scalar matrices when $\lambda=\mu$. Two diagonal representatives are conjugate exactly when they have the same unordered pair of eigenvalues; two Jordan-block representatives are conjugate exactly when their eigenvalues agree. No matrix in the second family is conjugate to one in the first because the former is not diagonalizable.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Suppose that $p$ is prime and $M$ is an $\mathbb F_p[X]$-module satisfying
$$
(X-1)^3M=0,
$$
$$
|(X-1)^2M|=p,qquad |(X-1)M|=p^3,qquad |M|=p^7.
$$
Determine $M$ as an $\mathbb F_p[X]$-module, up to isomorphism.

#proof
Put $t=X-1$. Since $t^3M=0$ and $M$ is finite, the structure theorem over the PID $\mathbb F_p[t]$ gives
$$
M\cong
(\mathbb F_p[t]/(t))^{r_1}
\oplus(\mathbb F_p[t]/(t^2))^{r_2}
\oplus(\mathbb F_p[t]/(t^3))^{r_3}.
$$
Taking base-$p$ logarithms of the three cardinalities gives
$$
r_1+2r_2+3r_3=7,
$$
$$
r_2+2r_3=3,
$$
and
$$
r_3=1.
$$
It follows that $r_3=1$, $r_2=1$, and $r_1=2$. Therefore
$$
M\cong
\mathbb F_p[X]/((X-1)^3)
\oplus\mathbb F_p[X]/((X-1)^2)
\oplus\left(\mathbb F_p[X]/(X-1)\right)^2.
$$
