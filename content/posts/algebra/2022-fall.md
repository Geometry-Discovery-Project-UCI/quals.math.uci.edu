::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ be a group, let $X$ be the set of all homomorphisms $h:G\to G$, and let $Y$ be the set of all automorphisms of $G$. Consider the action of $G$ given by composition with the inner automorphism $f_a(x)=axa^{-1}$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** For the action on $X$, let $h\in X$. Determine the relationship between the stabilizer of $h$ and the centralizer $C_G(\operatorname{Im}h)$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** For the action on $Y$, prove that the action is transitive if and only if every automorphism of $G$ is inner.

#proof
Strictly speaking, the formula $a\cdot h=h\circ f_a$ defines a right action if it is written as $h\cdot a=h\circ f_a$. Equivalently, one may obtain a left action by defining $a\cdot h=h\circ f_{a^{-1}}$. The stabilizers and orbits occurring in the problem are the same under either convention, so we use the stated composition formula.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** For $a,x\in G$,

$$
(h\circ f_a)(x)=h(axa^{-1})=h(a)h(x)h(a)^{-1}.
$$

Thus $a$ stabilizes $h$ precisely when

$$
h(a)h(x)h(a)^{-1}=h(x)
$$

for every $x\in G$. This is equivalent to saying that $h(a)$ commutes with every element of $\operatorname{Im}h$. Therefore,

$$
\operatorname{Stab}_G(h)
=\{a\in G:h(a)\in C_G(\operatorname{Im}h)\}
=h^{-1}\bigl(C_G(\operatorname{Im}h)\bigr).
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Suppose first that every automorphism of $G$ is inner. If $h\in Y$, then $h=f_a$ for some $a\in G$. Hence

$$
h=\operatorname{id}_G\circ f_a,
$$

so $h$ lies in the orbit of $\operatorname{id}_G$. Thus every element of $Y$ belongs to the same orbit, and the action is transitive.

Conversely, suppose the action on $Y$ is transitive. Given $h\in Y$, transitivity implies that $h$ lies in the orbit of $\operatorname{id}_G$. Therefore, for some $a\in G$,

$$
h=\operatorname{id}_G\circ f_a=f_a.
$$

Thus $h$ is inner. Since $h$ was arbitrary, every automorphism of $G$ is inner.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $A$ be a finite abelian group. Show that $A$ is cyclic if and only if, for every prime $p$, it has either no elements of order $p$ or exactly $p-1$ elements of order $p$.

#proof
Suppose first that $A$ is cyclic of order $n$. If $p\nmid n$, then Lagrange's theorem shows that $A$ has no elements of order $p$. If $p\mid n$, a cyclic group has a unique subgroup of order $p$. That subgroup is cyclic, and all of its $p-1$ nonidentity elements have order $p$. Therefore $A$ has exactly $p-1$ elements of order $p$.

Conversely, suppose that for every prime $p$, the group $A$ has either no elements of order $p$ or exactly $p-1$ such elements. Decompose $A$ into its primary components:

$$
A=\prod_{p\mid |A|} A_p,
$$

where $A_p$ is the Sylow $p$-subgroup of $A$. By the classification theorem for finite abelian groups,

$$
A_p\cong
\mathbb Z/p^{e_1}\mathbb Z\times\cdots\times
\mathbb Z/p^{e_r}\mathbb Z
$$

for some integers $e_1,\ldots,e_r\geq 1$. The elements annihilated by $p$ form the subgroup

$$
A_p[p]=\{x\in A_p:px=0\}\cong(\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z)^r.
$$

This subgroup has $p^r$ elements. Its nonzero elements are exactly the elements of order $p$, so $A$ has $p^r-1$ elements of order $p$. By hypothesis,

$$
p^r-1=p-1,
$$

which implies $r=1$. Hence every primary component $A_p$ is cyclic.

The orders of the different primary components are relatively prime, so their direct product is cyclic. Therefore $A$ is cyclic.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $\mathbb F_p$ be the field with $p$ elements. Find the order of a Sylow $p$-subgroup of

$$
G=GL_3(\mathbb F_p),
$$

and give an example of such a subgroup.

#proof
An invertible $3\times3$ matrix is determined by an ordered basis of $\mathbb F_p^3$. The first column may be any nonzero vector, the second may be any vector outside the span of the first, and the third may be any vector outside the span of the first two. Hence

$$
|GL_3(\mathbb F_p)|=(p^3-1)(p^3-p)(p^3-p^2).
$$

Factoring out powers of $p$ gives

$$
|GL_3(\mathbb F_p)|
=(p^3-1)\,p(p^2-1)\,p^2(p-1)
=p^3(p^3-1)(p^2-1)(p-1).
$$

None of $p^3-1$, $p^2-1$, and $p-1$ is divisible by $p$. Therefore the largest power of $p$ dividing $|G|$ is $p^3$, so every Sylow $p$-subgroup has order $p^3$.

Consider the subgroup

$$
U=
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}
1&a&b\\
0&1&c\\
0&0&1
\end{pmatrix}
:a,b,c\in\mathbb F_p
\right\}.
$$

The product and inverse of upper unitriangular matrices are again upper unitriangular, so $U$ is a subgroup of $G$. There are $p$ independent choices for each of $a,b,c$, and therefore

$$
|U|=p^3.
$$

Thus $U$ is a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $GL_3(\mathbb F_p)$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity, and let $I\subseteq R$ be an ideal such that

$$
R/I\cong\mathbb Z.
$$

Show that infinitely many maximal ideals of $R$ contain $I$.

#proof
Let

$$
\pi:R\longrightarrow R/I\cong\mathbb Z
$$

be the quotient map followed by a fixed isomorphism. By the correspondence theorem, ideals of $R$ containing $I$ correspond bijectively to ideals of $\mathbb Z$ through inverse image under $\pi$. This correspondence preserves maximal ideals.

For each prime number $p$, the ideal $p\mathbb Z$ is maximal in $\mathbb Z$ because

$$
\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z
$$

is a field. Therefore

$$
\mathfrak m_p=\pi^{-1}(p\mathbb Z)
$$

is a maximal ideal of $R$ containing $I$. If $p\neq q$, then $p\mathbb Z\neq q\mathbb Z$, so the correspondence theorem gives $\mathfrak m_p\neq\mathfrak m_q$. Since there are infinitely many primes, there are infinitely many maximal ideals of $R$ containing $I$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $R$ be a unique factorization domain in which there is, up to associates, exactly one irreducible element.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that $R$ is a principal ideal domain.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Show that $R$ has a unique maximal ideal and a unique nonmaximal prime ideal.

#proof
Let $p$ be a representative of the unique associate class of irreducible elements.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Since $R$ is a UFD, every nonzero nonunit $r\in R$ is a product of irreducibles. Every irreducible is associated to $p$, so every nonzero element of $R$ has the form

$$
r=up^n
$$

for a unit $u\in R$ and an integer $n\geq0$.

Let $J$ be a nonzero ideal of $R$. Consider the set

$$
S=\{n\geq0:up^n\in J\text{ for some unit }u\}.
$$

This is a nonempty set of nonnegative integers, so it has a least element $m$. Multiplying by a unit inverse shows that $p^m\in J$, and hence

$$
(p^m)\subseteq J.
$$

If $r\in J$ is nonzero, write $r=up^n$. The minimality of $m$ implies $n\geq m$. Therefore

$$
r=up^{n-m}p^m\in(p^m).
$$

Thus $J\subseteq(p^m)$, and consequently $J=(p^m)$. The zero ideal is also principal, so every ideal of $R$ is principal. Hence $R$ is a PID.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** In a PID, every nonzero prime ideal is generated by an irreducible element and is maximal. Since every irreducible is associated to $p$, the only nonzero prime ideal is $(p)$. It is therefore the unique maximal ideal.

Because $R$ is a UFD, it is an integral domain, so $(0)$ is prime. It is not maximal because $R$ is not a field: the element $p$ is a nonzero nonunit. Thus $(0)$ is the unique nonmaximal prime ideal.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $K\subseteq F$ be a submodule of a module over a commutative PID $R$. Assume that $F$ is free of finite rank. Show that $K$ is also free of finite rank.

#proof
We prove the result by induction on $n=\operatorname{rank}F$. Since $F\cong R^n$, it is enough to prove that every submodule of $R^n$ is free of rank at most $n$.

If $n=0$, the result is immediate. If $n=1$, then $K$ is an ideal of $R$. Since $R$ is a PID, either $K=(0)$ or $K=(d)$ for some nonzero $d\in R$. Thus $K$ is free of rank $0$ or $1$.

Assume the result holds for submodules of $R^{n-1}$, and let $K\subseteq R^n$. Let

$$
\pi:R^n\longrightarrow R
$$

be projection onto the last coordinate. Its image $\pi(K)$ is an ideal of $R$, so

$$
\pi(K)=(d)
$$

for some $d\in R$.

If $d=0$, then $K\subseteq\ker\pi\cong R^{n-1}$, and the induction hypothesis applies.

Suppose $d\neq0$. Choose $k_0\in K$ such that $\pi(k_0)=d$. If $k\in K$, then $\pi(k)=rd$ for some $r\in R$. Therefore

$$
\pi(k-rk_0)=0,
$$

so $k-rk_0\in K\cap\ker\pi$. Hence

$$
K=(K\cap\ker\pi)+Rk_0.
$$

This sum is direct. Indeed, if $rk_0\in K\cap\ker\pi$, then

$$
0=\pi(rk_0)=rd.
$$

Since a PID is an integral domain and $d\neq0$, we have $r=0$. Thus

$$
K=(K\cap\ker\pi)\oplus Rk_0.
$$

By the induction hypothesis, $K\cap\ker\pi$ is free of rank at most $n-1$, while $Rk_0\cong R$ is free of rank $1$. Therefore $K$ is free of finite rank at most $n$.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $R=\mathbb Z[x]$ and let $I=(2,x)$. Then $R/I\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that the map $\varphi:I\times I\to\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ defined by

$$
\varphi(a_0+a_1x+\cdots+a_nx^n,
b_0+b_1x+\cdots+b_mx^m)
=\frac{a_0}{2}b_1\pmod 2
$$

is $R$-balanced.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Prove that

$$
2\otimes x-x\otimes2\neq0
$$

in $I\otimes_R I$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Prove that $2\otimes x-x\otimes2$ is annihilated by both $2$ and $x$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Prove that the submodule generated by $2\otimes x-x\otimes2$ is isomorphic to $R/I$.

#proof
We regard $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$ as an $R$-module through the quotient map $R\to R/I$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** If $p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots\in I$, then $a_0$ is even, so $a_0/2$ is an integer. Thus $\varphi$ is well-defined. Its additivity in each variable follows immediately from the additivity of the constant coefficient of the first input and the coefficient of $x$ in the second input.

It remains to prove the balancing identity. Let

$$
p(x)=a_0+a_1x+\cdots,\qquad
q(x)=b_0+b_1x+\cdots
$$

belong to $I$, and let

$$
r(x)=c_0+c_1x+\cdots\in R.
$$

The constant coefficient of $p(x)r(x)$ is $a_0c_0$, so

$$
\varphi(pr,q)=\frac{a_0c_0}{2}b_1\pmod2.
$$

The coefficient of $x$ in $r(x)q(x)$ is $c_0b_1+c_1b_0$. Hence

$$
\varphi(p,rq)
=\frac{a_0}{2}(c_0b_1+c_1b_0)\pmod2.
$$

Because $q\in I$, its constant coefficient $b_0$ is even. Therefore

$$
\frac{a_0}{2}c_1b_0\equiv0\pmod2,
$$

and consequently

$$
\varphi(pr,q)=\varphi(p,rq).
$$

Thus $\varphi$ is $R$-balanced.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** By the universal property of the tensor product, $\varphi$ induces an $R$-module homomorphism

$$
\Phi:I\otimes_R I\longrightarrow\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z
$$

satisfying $\Phi(p\otimes q)=\varphi(p,q)$. We have

$$
\Phi(2\otimes x)=\varphi(2,x)=1
$$

and

$$
\Phi(x\otimes2)=\varphi(x,2)=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\Phi(2\otimes x-x\otimes2)=1,
$$

so $2\otimes x-x\otimes2$ is nonzero.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Set

$$
m=2\otimes x-x\otimes2.
$$

Using the balancing relation $(ra)\otimes b=a\otimes(rb)$, we obtain

$$
\begin{aligned}
2m
&=4\otimes x-2x\otimes2\\
&=2\otimes2x-2x\otimes2\\
&=2x\otimes2-2x\otimes2\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Similarly,

$$
\begin{aligned}
xm
&=2x\otimes x-x^2\otimes2\\
&=x\otimes2x-x\otimes2x\\
&=0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus both $2$ and $x$ annihilate $m$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Consider the surjective $R$-module homomorphism

$$
\psi:R\longrightarrow Rm,qquad \psi(r)=rm.
$$

Its kernel is $\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)$. Part (c) gives

$$
I=(2,x)\subseteq\operatorname{Ann}_R(m).
$$

Conversely, suppose $rm=0$. Applying $\Phi$ from part (b), we get

$$
0=\Phi(rm)=r\Phi(m).
$$

Since $\Phi(m)=1$ in the $R$-module $R/I\cong\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, this equality says precisely that the image of $r$ in $R/I$ is zero. Thus $r\in I$. Therefore

$$
\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)=I.
$$

The first isomorphism theorem now gives

$$
Rm\cong R/\operatorname{Ann}_R(m)=R/I.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $F$ be a field, and suppose that $F(\alpha)$ is a finite extension of $F$ of odd degree. Prove that

$$
F(\alpha^2)=F(\alpha).
$$

#proof
Clearly,

$$
F(\alpha^2)\subseteq F(\alpha).
$$

The element $\alpha$ satisfies the polynomial

$$
t^2-\alpha^2\in F(\alpha^2)[t].
$$

Therefore

$$
[F(\alpha):F(\alpha^2)]\leq2.
$$

If $\alpha\notin F(\alpha^2)$, then this degree cannot be $1$, so it must equal $2$. The tower law would then give

$$
[F(\alpha):F]
= [F(\alpha):F(\alpha^2)]\,[F(\alpha^2):F],
$$

where the product on the right is divisible by $2$. This contradicts the assumption that $[F(\alpha):F]$ is odd. Hence $\alpha\in F(\alpha^2)$, and therefore

$$
F(\alpha)=F(\alpha^2).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $F$ be a field with $64$ elements containing $\mathbb F_2$, and let $\alpha\in F^\times$. Suppose that $\alpha$ has order $d\geq8$ in $F^\times$. Show that

$$
\mathbb F_2(\alpha)=F.
$$

#proof
Since $|F|=64=2^6$, we may identify $F$ with $\mathbb F_{2^6}$. Its multiplicative group $F^\times$ is cyclic of order

$$
2^6-1=63.
$$

Thus $d$ divides $63$. In particular, the assumption $d\geq8$ actually implies $d\geq9$, since no divisor of $63$ lies strictly between $7$ and $9$.

Let

$$
E=\mathbb F_2(\alpha).
$$

Every subfield of $\mathbb F_{2^6}$ has order $2^m$ for some divisor $m$ of $6$. Hence a proper subfield has order $2$, $4$, or $8$. Its multiplicative group therefore has order $1$, $3$, or $7$.

If $E$ were a proper subfield of $F$, then the order of $\alpha\in E^\times$ would divide one of $1$, $3$, or $7$. This is impossible because $d\geq8$. Therefore $E$ is not proper, and

$$
\mathbb F_2(\alpha)=F.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Let $K/\mathbb Q$ be a Galois extension whose Galois group is isomorphic to $S_5$. Let

$$
C=\langle(12345)\rangle\subseteq S_5
$$

and let $L=K^C$ be its fixed field. Find the number of subfields $L'\subseteq K$ that are isomorphic to $L$ over $\mathbb Q$, including $L$ itself.

#proof
By the Galois correspondence, the conjugate subgroups

$$
gCg^{-1},\qquad g\in S_5,
$$

have fixed fields

$$
K^{gCg^{-1}}=g(K^C)=g(L),
$$

and these fields are isomorphic to $L$ over $\mathbb Q$.

Conversely, suppose $L'\subseteq K$ is $\mathbb Q$-isomorphic to $L$. A $\mathbb Q$-embedding $L\to K$ extends to an automorphism of an algebraic closure of $\mathbb Q$. Since $K/\mathbb Q$ is normal, the extension sends $K$ to itself and therefore restricts to an element $g\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong S_5$. It follows that

$$
L'=g(L)=K^{gCg^{-1}}.
$$

Thus the desired fields correspond exactly to the conjugates of $C$ in $S_5$.

There are

$$
(5-1)!=24
$$

different $5$-cycles in $S_5$. Every cyclic subgroup of order $5$ contains exactly four $5$-cycles, namely its four nonidentity elements. Moreover, two distinct subgroups of order $5$ have trivial intersection, since any nonidentity element would generate both subgroups. Therefore the number of cyclic subgroups generated by $5$-cycles is

$$
\frac{24}{4}=6.
$$

All such subgroups are conjugate in $S_5$. Hence there are exactly

$$
6
$$

subfields of $K$ that are isomorphic to $L$ over $\mathbb Q$.
::
