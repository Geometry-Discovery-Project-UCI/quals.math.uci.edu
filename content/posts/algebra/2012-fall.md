::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $R$ be a commutative ring. If $r,s\in R$ are nilpotent, prove that $r+s$ is nilpotent. Does this remain true without commutativity?

#proof
Suppose $r^m=0$ and $s^n=0$. By the binomial theorem, every term in
$$
(r+s)^{m+n-1}
$$
contains either at least $m$ factors of $r$ or at least $n$ factors of $s$. Hence every term is zero, so $r+s$ is nilpotent.

The assertion fails in a noncommutative ring. In $M_2(F)$, let
$$
r=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
s=\begin{bmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Both square to zero, but
$$
r+s=\begin{bmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{bmatrix}
$$
has square $I$ and is not nilpotent.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
(a) How many Sylow $5$-subgroups does $S_5$ have?

(b) Find a Sylow $2$-subgroup of $S_5$ and describe it abstractly and explicitly.

#proof
(a) There are $(5-1)!=24$ different $5$-cycles. Each subgroup of order $5$ has four nonidentity elements, all $5$-cycles. Thus the number of Sylow $5$-subgroups is
$$
24/4=6.
$$

(b) Since $|S_5|=120=2^3\cdot15$, a Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $8$. Let
$$
r=(1234),
\qquad s=(13).
$$
Then $r^4=s^2=1$ and $srs=r^{-1}$. Hence
$$
P=\langle r,s\rangle
=\{1,r,r^2,r^3,s,sr,sr^2,sr^3\}
$$
is a subgroup of order $8$, fixing the letter $5$. Abstractly, $P\cong D_8$, and it is a Sylow $2$-subgroup.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
For a $\mathbb Z[x]$-module $M$, consider:

$(*)$ every ascending chain of submodules eventually stabilizes;

$(**)$ every submodule of $M$ is finitely generated.

Does either property imply the other?

#proof
The two properties are equivalent, for modules over any ring.

Assume $(*)$, and let $N\subseteq M$ be a submodule. If $N$ were not finitely generated, choose $x_1\in N$, then $x_2\notin\langle x_1\rangle$, and inductively
$$
x_{k+1}\notin\langle x_1,\ldots,x_k\rangle.
$$
This produces a strictly increasing chain of submodules, contradicting $(*)$. Thus $(*)$ implies $(**)$.

Conversely, assume $(**)$ and let
$$
M_1\subseteq M_2\subseteq\cdots
$$
be an ascending chain. Its union $N=\bigcup_iM_i$ is a submodule, hence is generated by finitely many elements. All these generators lie in some common $M_j$, so $N=M_j$. Therefore $M_i=M_j$ for every $i\ge j$, and the chain stabilizes. Thus $(**)$ implies $(*)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Show that all squares in a group $G$ lie in every subgroup of index $2$. Must all cubes lie in every subgroup of index $3$?

#proof
Every subgroup $H$ of index $2$ is normal, and $G/H$ has order $2$. Therefore
$$
(gH)^2=H
$$
for every $g\in G$, so $g^2\in H$.

The analogous assertion for cubes is false because a subgroup of index $3$ need not be normal. In $S_3$, let
$$
H=\langle(12)\rangle,
$$
which has index $3$, and let $g=(13)$. Then
$$
g^3=g=(13)\notin H.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Suppose $F/\mathbb Q$ is Galois and
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)\cong S_4.
$$
Show that there is an irreducible quartic $g(x)\in\mathbb Q[x]$ whose splitting field is $F$.

#proof
Let $H\cong S_3$ be a point stabilizer in $S_4$, and let $E=F^H$. Then
$$
[E:\mathbb Q]=[S_4:H]=4.
$$
By the primitive element theorem, $E=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ for some $\alpha$. Its minimal polynomial $g$ is irreducible of degree $4$.

The splitting field of $g$ inside $F$ is the fixed field of the core
$$
\operatorname{core}_{S_4}(H)=\bigcap_{\sigma\in S_4}\sigma H\sigma^{-1}.
$$
The intersection of all four point stabilizers is trivial. Hence the core is trivial, and the splitting field is $F$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
How many conjugacy classes are there in $GL_3(\mathbb F_2)$?

#proof
Conjugacy classes are classified by rational canonical form. Because the matrix is invertible, the polynomial $x$ cannot occur among its elementary divisors. The relevant monic irreducibles over $\mathbb F_2$ are
$$
x+1,
\qquad x^2+x+1,
\qquad x^3+x+1,
\qquad x^3+x^2+1.
$$
The possibilities of total dimension $3$ are:

- three $(x+1)$-primary classes, corresponding to the partitions $(3)$, $(2,1)$, and $(1,1,1)$;

- one class with elementary divisors $x+1$ and $x^2+x+1$;

- one class for each of the two irreducible cubics.

Thus the total number of conjugacy classes is
$$
3+1+2=6.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $G$ be finite, $K$ a field, and $\phi:G\to GL_n(K)$ a representation.

(a) Must $\phi(G')\subseteq SL_n(K)$?

(b) Must $\phi(Z(G))\subseteq SL_n(K)$?

#proof
(a) Yes. The composite
$$
G\xrightarrow{\phi}GL_n(K)\xrightarrow{\det}K^*
$$
is a homomorphism into an abelian group, so it annihilates the commutator subgroup. Hence every element of $\phi(G')$ has determinant $1$.

(b) No. Let $G=C_2$, $K=\mathbb R$, and take the one-dimensional representation sending the generator to $-1$. Since $G=Z(G)$ but $SL_1(\mathbb R)=\{1\}$, the image of the center is not contained in $SL_1(\mathbb R)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Determine the Galois group of the splitting field of $x^3+2$ over $\mathbb F_3$, $\mathbb F_7$, and $\mathbb F_{11}$.

#proof
Over $\mathbb F_3$,
$$
x^3+2=x^3-1=(x-1)^3.
$$
The splitting field is already $\mathbb F_3$, so the Galois group is trivial.

Over $\mathbb F_7$, nonzero cubes are only $1$ and $-1$, so $x^3=-2=5$ has no solution. Thus the cubic is irreducible. Its splitting field is $\mathbb F_{7^3}$, and the Galois group is cyclic of order $3$, generated by Frobenius.

Over $\mathbb F_{11}$, $4^3=64\equiv9=-2\pmod{11}$, and
$$
x^3+2=(x-4)(x^2+4x+5).
$$
The quadratic factor has discriminant
$$
16-20=-4\equiv7\pmod{11},
$$
which is not a square modulo $11$. Hence the splitting field is $\mathbb F_{11^2}$, and its Galois group is cyclic of order $2$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Answer each question briefly.

(a) If a group has elements of orders $m$ and $n$, must it have an element of order $\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)$?

(b) If $K/F$ is cyclic Galois and $F\subseteq E\subseteq K$, is $E/F$ cyclic Galois?

(c) Are there at most $(n!)^n$ groups of order $n$, up to isomorphism?

(d) What is the largest element order in $D_{64}$, the symmetry group of a $32$-gon?

(e) Find $\dim_F(V\otimes_FW)$ for finite-dimensional $F$-vector spaces.

#proof
(a) False. The group $S_3$ has elements of orders $2$ and $3$ but none of order $6$.

(b) True. Under Galois correspondence, $E$ corresponds to a subgroup $H$ of the cyclic group $\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$. Every subgroup of a cyclic group is normal, so $E/F$ is Galois, and the quotient Galois group is cyclic.

(c) True. After labeling its elements, every group of order $n$ embeds in $S_n$ by its regular action and is determined by the $n$ permutations representing left multiplication. There are at most $(n!)^n$ such lists, so certainly at most that many isomorphism classes.

(d) The rotations form a cyclic subgroup of order $32$, and every reflection has order $2$. Thus the maximum order is $32$.

(e) If $\dim_FV=a$ and $\dim_FW=b$, then the tensors of basis vectors form a basis, so
$$
\dim_F(V\otimes_FW)=ab.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
For each item, give an example or explain why none exists.

(a) A quadratic field extension that is not separable.

(b) A nonabelian group all of whose proper subgroups are cyclic.

(c) An infinite field in which every nonzero element has finite multiplicative order.

(d) A nonabelian group with trivial automorphism group.

(e) An element of order $4$ in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$.

#proof
(a) Let $F=\mathbb F_2(t)$ and
$$
K=F(\sqrt t).
$$
The polynomial $x^2-t$ is irreducible and has derivative zero, so $K/F$ is an inseparable quadratic extension.

(b) The quaternion group $Q_8$ is nonabelian, and every proper subgroup is cyclic.

(c) The algebraic closure $\overline{\mathbb F_p}$ is infinite. Every nonzero element belongs to some finite subfield and therefore has finite multiplicative order.

(d) No such group exists. If $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ is trivial, then every inner automorphism is trivial, so $G/Z(G)=1$. Hence $G$ is abelian.

(e) The class
$$
\frac14+\mathbb Z
$$
has order $4$ in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$.
::
