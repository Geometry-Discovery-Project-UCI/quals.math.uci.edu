::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
(a) Define a prime ideal.

(b) Define a maximal ideal.

(c) Give a ring $R$ and ideals $P_1,P_2,P_3$ such that $P_1$ is both prime and maximal, $P_2$ is neither, and $P_3$ is prime but not maximal.

#proof
(a) A proper ideal $P$ of a commutative ring $R$ is prime if $ab\in P$ implies $a\in P$ or $b\in P$. Equivalently, $R/P$ is an integral domain.

(b) A proper ideal $M$ is maximal if the only ideals containing it are $M$ and $R$. Equivalently, $R/M$ is a field.

(c) Take $R=\mathbb Z$ and
$$
P_1=(2),\qquad P_2=(6),\qquad P_3=(0).
$$
The quotient $\mathbb Z/(2)$ is a field, so $P_1$ is maximal and prime. The quotient $\mathbb Z/(6)$ has zero divisors and is not a field, so $P_2$ is neither prime nor maximal. Finally, $\mathbb Z/(0)=\mathbb Z$ is a domain but not a field, so $P_3$ is prime but not maximal. In a commutative ring with identity every maximal ideal is prime, so “maximal but not prime” cannot occur.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Show that if a group $G$ has only finitely many subgroups, then $G$ is finite.

#proof
If some $g\in G$ had infinite order, the subgroups
$$
\langle g\rangle,\ \langle g^2\rangle,\ \langle g^3\rangle,\ldots
$$
would be infinitely many distinct subgroups. Hence every element of $G$ has finite order, so every cyclic subgroup of $G$ is finite.

By hypothesis there are only finitely many subgroups, say $H_1,\ldots,H_m$. Every element $g$ lies in the cyclic subgroup $\langle g\rangle$, which is one of these finitely many subgroups and is finite. Therefore
$$
G=\bigcup_{j=1}^m H_j
$$
is a finite union of finite sets, and hence is finite.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ real matrix satisfying $A^2=-I$.

(a) Prove that $n$ is even.

(b) Prove that $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$ and describe the corresponding diagonal matrices.

#proof
(a) Taking determinants gives
$$
(\det A)^2=\det(-I)=(-1)^n.
$$
The left side is nonnegative because $\det A\in\mathbb R$, so $n$ cannot be odd. Hence $n$ is even.

(b) The minimal polynomial of $A$ divides
$$
x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i),
$$
which has distinct roots over $\mathbb C$. Therefore $A$ is diagonalizable over $\mathbb C$, and its eigenvalues are among $i$ and $-i$. Because $A$ is real, nonreal eigenvalues occur with equal algebraic multiplicities. Thus each occurs $n/2$ times, and $A$ is similar over $\mathbb C$ to
$$
\operatorname{diag}(\underbrace{i,\ldots,i}_{n/2},
\underbrace{-i,\ldots,-i}_{n/2}),
$$
up to permutation of the diagonal entries.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $G$ be a group of order $70$. Prove that $G$ has a normal subgroup of order $35$.

#proof
The number of Sylow $7$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_7\equiv1\pmod7,
\qquad n_7\mid10.
$$
Thus $n_7=1$. Let $N$ be the unique Sylow $7$-subgroup; then $N\trianglelefteq G$.

The quotient $G/N$ has order $10$. Its number of Sylow $5$-subgroups is congruent to $1$ modulo $5$ and divides $2$, so it has a unique, hence normal, subgroup $P/N$ of order $5$. The inverse image $P$ is normal in $G$, and
$$
|P|=|N|\,|P/N|=7\cdot5=35.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Construct a Galois extension $F/\mathbb Q$ whose Galois group is $D_8$, the dihedral group of order $8$.

#proof
Let $a=\sqrt[4]{2}$ and let
$$
F=\mathbb Q(a,i).
$$
This is the splitting field of the separable polynomial $x^4-2$, whose roots are $a,ia,-a,-ia$, so $F/\mathbb Q$ is Galois. Eisenstein's criterion gives $[\mathbb Q(a):\mathbb Q]=4$, and $i\notin\mathbb Q(a)$ because the latter is real. Hence $[F:\mathbb Q]=8$.

Define automorphisms
$$
r(a)=ia,\quad r(i)=i,
$$
and
$$
s(a)=a,\quad s(i)=-i.
$$
Then
$$
r^4=s^2=1,
\qquad srs=r^{-1}.
$$
The eight maps $r^k$ and $sr^k$, $0\le k<4$, are distinct and account for all automorphisms. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)\cong D_8.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $F$ be a field. Prove that every ideal of $F[x]$ is principal.

#proof
Let $I$ be an ideal. If $I=(0)$, it is principal. Otherwise, choose a nonzero polynomial $f\in I$ of least degree. We claim that $I=(f)$.

Certainly $(f)\subseteq I$. For any $g\in I$, the division algorithm gives
$$
g=qf+r,
\qquad \deg r<\deg f.
$$
Since $r=g-qf\in I$, minimality of $\deg f$ forces $r=0$. Hence $f\mid g$, so $g\in(f)$. Therefore $I=(f)$, and $F[x]$ is a PID.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Give a module $M$ over a ring $R$ such that $M$ is not finitely generated, and prove it.

#proof
Take $R=\mathbb Z$ and $M=\mathbb Q$, regarded as a $\mathbb Z$-module. Suppose finitely many rationals
$$
q_1,\ldots,q_m
$$
generated $\mathbb Q$. Choose a positive integer $N$ divisible by all their denominators. Every integer linear combination of the $q_i$ then lies in $(1/N)\mathbb Z$. Choose a prime $p$ not dividing $N$. Then $1/p\notin(1/N)\mathbb Z$, contradicting generation. Hence $\mathbb Q$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb Z$-module.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Suppose $H$ is a normal subgroup of a finite group $G$.

(a) If $|H|=2$, must $H\subseteq Z(G)$?

(b) If $|H|=3$, must $H\subseteq Z(G)$?

#proof
(a) Yes. Write $H=\{1,h\}$. For every $g\in G$, normality gives $ghg^{-1}\in H$. Conjugation cannot send the nonidentity element $h$ to $1$, so $ghg^{-1}=h$. Thus $h$ commutes with every $g$, and $H\subseteq Z(G)$.

(b) No. In $S_3$, the alternating subgroup
$$
A_3=\langle(123)\rangle
$$
is normal and has order $3$, but $Z(S_3)=1$. Thus $A_3$ is not contained in the center.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
(a) Define an irreducible representation.

(b) Let $p$ be prime, $G=\mathbb Z/p\mathbb Z$, and $\rho:G\to GL_2(\mathbb F_p)$ a representation. Show that $\rho$ is reducible.

#proof
(a) A nonzero representation $\rho:G\to GL(V)$ is irreducible if its only $G$-invariant subspaces are $0$ and $V$.

(b) Let $g$ generate $G$ and set $A=\rho(g)$. Since $A^p=I$ and the characteristic is $p$,
$$
(A-I)^p=A^p-I=0.
$$
Thus $A-I$ is nilpotent and has nonzero kernel. Choose $0\ne v\in\ker(A-I)$. Then $Av=v$, so the line $\mathbb F_pv$ is invariant under $A$, and hence under all of $G$. It is a nonzero proper subspace of $\mathbb F_p^2$, so the representation is reducible.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
(a) Compute $|GL_4(\mathbb F_{32})|$.

(b) Compute $|SL_4(\mathbb F_{32})|$.

(c) Show that $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{10}]$ is not a UFD.

#proof
(a) Choose the columns of an invertible matrix successively. With $q=32$, the choices are
$$
q^4-1,quad q^4-q,quad q^4-q^2,quad q^4-q^3.
$$
Therefore
$$
|GL_4(\mathbb F_{32})|
=(32^4-1)(32^4-32)(32^4-32^2)(32^4-32^3)
=1169966368187415841996800.
$$

(b) The determinant map $GL_4(\mathbb F_{32})\to\mathbb F_{32}^*$ is surjective and has kernel $SL_4(\mathbb F_{32})$. Since $|\mathbb F_{32}^*|=31$,
$$
|SL_4(\mathbb F_{32})|
=\frac{|GL_4(\mathbb F_{32})|}{31}
=37740850586690833612800.
$$

(c) In $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{10}]$,
$$
6=2\cdot3=(\sqrt{10}-2)(\sqrt{10}+2).
$$
Use the norm
$$
N(a+b\sqrt{10})=a^2-10b^2.
$$
There is no element of norm $\pm2$ or $\pm3$: reducing $a^2-10b^2=\pm2$ or $\pm3$ modulo $5$ would make $a^2$ congruent to $2$ or $3$, neither of which is a quadratic residue modulo $5$. It follows from multiplicativity of the norm that $2$, $3$, and $\sqrt{10}\pm2$ are irreducible. The factors appearing in the two decompositions are not associates; in particular their norms rule out all comparisons except between $\sqrt{10}-2$ and $\sqrt{10}+2$, and their quotient
$$
\frac{\sqrt{10}+2}{\sqrt{10}-2}=\frac{7+2\sqrt{10}}3
$$
does not lie in the ring. Thus $6$ has two inequivalent factorizations into irreducibles, and $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{10}]$ is not a UFD.
