::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $V=\mathbb F_2^3$ and
$$
G=V\rtimes GL_3(\mathbb F_2),
$$
with multiplication $(v_1,g_1)(v_2,g_2)=(v_1+g_1v_2,g_1g_2)$. Put
$$
H=\{(0,g):g\in GL_3(\mathbb F_2)\}.
$$

(a) Show that no subgroup lies properly between $H$ and $G$.

(b) Show that the affine action $(v_1,g_1)\cdot v=v_1+g_1v$ embeds $G$ into $S_8$.

(c) Show that the image is contained in $A_8$.

#proof
(a) Suppose $H<L\le G$ and choose $(v,g)\in L\setminus H$, so $v\ne0$. Multiplying by $(0,g^{-1})\in H$ gives a nonzero translation in $L$. Conjugating it by $H$ gives translations by every vector in the $GL_3(\mathbb F_2)$-orbit of $v$. This group acts transitively on the seven nonzero vectors, so $L$ contains every translation. Together with $H$, these generate $G$. Hence $L=G$.

(b) The displayed formula defines an action on the eight-element set $V$. If $(v,g)$ acts trivially, evaluating at $0$ gives $v=0$, and then $gx=x$ for every $x$, so $g=I$. Thus the action is faithful and yields an embedding $G\hookrightarrow S_8$.

(c) A nonzero translation partitions the eight vectors into four pairs $\{x,x+v\}$, so as a permutation it is a product of four transpositions and is even. The group $GL_3(\mathbb F_2)$ is generated by elementary transvections. A transvection fixes a hyperplane of four vectors and interchanges the remaining four in two pairs, so it too is even. Hence both the translation subgroup and $H$ act by even permutations, and therefore $G\le A_8$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
(a) If $|G|=p^2$, use the nontrivial center of a $p$-group to show that $G$ is abelian.

(b) Give a nonabelian group of order $3^3$.

#proof
(a) The center $Z(G)$ has order $p$ or $p^2$. If it has order $p^2$, then $G=Z(G)$ is abelian. If it has order $p$, then $G/Z(G)$ has order $p$ and is cyclic. Whenever $G/Z(G)$ is cyclic, $G$ is abelian: if $G/Z(G)=\langle gZ(G)\rangle$, all elements have the form $g^az$ and commute. Thus $G$ is abelian in either case.

(b) The group
$$
UT_3(\mathbb F_3)=
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1&a&c\\0&1&b\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}:a,b,c\in\mathbb F_3
\right\}
$$
has $3^3=27$ elements and is nonabelian; for example, the elementary matrices with nonzero $(1,2)$ and $(2,3)$ entries do not commute.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
(a) Prove that every group of order $15$ is abelian.

(b) For distinct primes $p,q$, determine when there is exactly one group of order $pq$ up to isomorphism.

#proof
(a) If $|G|=3\cdot5$, then
$$
n_5\equiv1\pmod5,quad n_5\mid3,
$$
so $n_5=1$, and
$$
n_3\equiv1\pmod3,quad n_3\mid5,
$$
so $n_3=1$. Both Sylow subgroups are normal, and therefore
$$
G\cong C_3\times C_5\cong C_{15}.
$$

(b) Write $p<q$. There is always the cyclic group $C_{pq}$. A nonabelian group exists exactly when
$$
p\mid(q-1),
$$
because this is exactly when there is a nontrivial homomorphism
$$
C_p\to\operatorname{Aut}(C_q)\cong C_{q-1},
$$
giving a nontrivial semidirect product $C_q\rtimes C_p$. Thus there is exactly one group of order $pq$ precisely when the smaller prime does not divide one less than the larger prime.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $f(x)$ be an irreducible polynomial of prime degree $p$ over $\mathbb Q$, with exactly $p-2$ real roots. Regard its Galois group as a subgroup of $S_p$.

(a) Show that it contains a transposition.

(b) Show that it equals $S_p$.

(c) Apply this to $f(x)=x^5-9x+2$.

#proof
(a) There is exactly one pair of nonreal roots. Complex conjugation fixes the $p-2$ real roots and interchanges that pair, so it acts as a transposition.

(b) Irreducibility makes the action on the roots transitive, so $p$ divides the group order. By Cauchy's theorem the group contains an element of order $p$, necessarily a $p$-cycle. Conjugating the transposition by powers of that cycle produces transpositions corresponding to the edges of a connected graph on the $p$ roots. Such transpositions generate $S_p$. Hence the Galois group is $S_p$.

(c) Modulo $7$, the polynomial becomes $x^5-2x+2$, which is irreducible. For example, the finite-field criterion is verified by
$$
\gcd(x^5-2x+2,x^7-x)=1,
\qquad
x^{7^5}\equiv x\pmod{x^5-2x+2}.
$$
Thus $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

Its derivative is $5x^4-9$, with real critical points $\pm a$, where $a=(9/5)^{1/4}$. Since $a^4=9/5$,
$$
f(-a)=\frac{36}{5}a+2>0,
\qquad
f(a)=2-\frac{36}{5}a<0.
$$
Together with the limits at $\pm\infty$, this gives exactly three real roots. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb Q)=S_5.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $D_4$ denote the dihedral group of order $8$.

(a) Give representatives of all conjugacy classes, and find the center and commutator subgroup.

(b) Describe the standard two-dimensional representation.

(c) List all irreducible complex representations.

#proof
Write
$$
D_4=\langle r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1, srs=r^{-1}\rangle.
$$
The conjugacy classes are
$$
\{1\},\quad\{r^2\},\quad\{r,r^3\},\quad
\{s,r^2s\},\quad\{rs,r^3s\}.
$$
Thus representatives are $1,r^2,r,s,rs$. Moreover,
$$
Z(D_4)=\{1,r^2\}
$$
and
$$
[D_4,D_4]=\{1,r^2\}.
$$

The standard representation is the symmetry action on the plane:
$$
r\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
s\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$

Since $D_4^{\mathrm{ab}}\cong C_2\times C_2$, there are four one-dimensional representations, obtained by independently assigning $r,s$ the values $\pm1$. The standard two-dimensional representation is irreducible and is the fifth. The degree sum
$$
4\cdot1^2+2^2=8
$$
shows that the list is complete.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $S$ be the real vector space of antisymmetric $n\times n$ matrices. For fixed $A\in M_n(\mathbb R)$, define
$$
F_A(X)=A^tX+XA.
$$

(a) Find $\dim S$.

(b) Show that $F_A(S)\subseteq S$.

(c) Compute the rank and eigenvalues of $F_A$ in terms of the eigenvalues of $A$.

#proof
(a) An antisymmetric matrix has zero diagonal and one free entry for every pair $i<j$, so
$$
\dim S=\binom n2=\frac{n(n-1)}2.
$$

(b) If $X^t=-X$, then
$$
F_A(X)^t=X^tA+A^tX^t=-XA-A^tX=-F_A(X).
$$

(c) Under the natural identification of antisymmetric bilinear forms with $\bigwedge^2(\mathbb C^n)^*$, $F_A$ is the operator induced by $A$ on the second exterior power. If the eigenvalues of $A$ over $\mathbb C$ are $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$, then the eigenvalues of $F_A$ are
$$
\lambda_i+\lambda_j,
\qquad1\le i<j\le n,
$$
counted with algebraic multiplicity.

If $A$ is diagonalizable, so is the induced operator, and therefore
$$
\operatorname{rank}F_A
=\#\{(i,j):i<j, \lambda_i+\lambda_j\ne0\},
$$
counting multiplicities.

For an arbitrary matrix $A$, the rank is not determined by its eigenvalues alone, so the rank request needs a diagonalizability hypothesis. For instance, $A=0$ and $A=J_2(0)\oplus[0]$ in dimension $3$ have the same eigenvalues, but $F_0=0$ while $F_{J_2(0)\oplus[0]}\ne0$. In general the exact rank is
$$
\binom n2-\dim\{X\in S:A^tX+XA=0\},
$$
and its nullity depends on the Jordan structure of $A$.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Use the natural action of $S_3$ on $\{1,2,3\}$ to construct a representation on $V=\mathbb C^3$.

(a) Give a homomorphism $S_3\to GL_3(\mathbb C)$ with trivial kernel.

(b) Find a one-dimensional invariant subspace $V_1$.

(c) Find a two-dimensional invariant complement $V_2$ and give generators for it.

#proof
(a) Let $S_3$ permute the standard basis:
$$
\rho(\sigma)e_i=e_{\sigma(i)}.
$$
These are permutation matrices. If $\rho(\sigma)=I$, then $\sigma$ fixes all three indices, so $\sigma=1$. Thus the kernel is trivial.

(b) The line
$$
V_1=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,1)\}
$$
is fixed pointwise.

(c) Take
$$
V_2=\{(z_1,z_2,z_3):z_1+z_2+z_3=0\}.
$$
It is permutation-invariant and is generated by
$$
e_1-e_2,
\qquad
e_2-e_3.
$$
Since $V_1\cap V_2=0$ and their dimensions add to $3$,
$$
V=V_1\oplus V_2.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Prove that every finite subgroup $G$ of the multiplicative group $K^*$ of a field is cyclic.

#proof
The group $G$ is finite abelian. Let $m$ be its exponent, the least common multiple of the orders of its elements. Every element of $G$ is a root of $x^m-1$, so
$$
|G|\le m.
$$
On the other hand, the exponent of a finite group divides its order, so $m\le|G|$. Hence $m=|G|$.

For a finite abelian group, there is an element whose order equals the exponent: choose elements of maximal prime-power order in each primary component and multiply them. Therefore $G$ has an element of order $|G|$, and that element generates $G$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
(a) Find all maximal ideals of $T=\mathbb Q[X]/(X^2-4)$.

(b) Find all maximal ideals of $S=\mathbb Q[X]/(X^2-1)$.

(c) Express $T$ as a direct sum of fields.

#proof
(a) Since $X^2-4=(X-2)(X+2)$, the maximal ideals are
$$
(\overline{X-2})
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(\overline{X+2}).
$$

(b) Since $X^2-1=(X-1)(X+1)$, the maximal ideals are
$$
(\overline{X-1})
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
(\overline{X+1}).
$$

(c) The two factors in part (a) are coprime, so the Chinese remainder theorem gives
$$
T\cong\mathbb Q[X]/(X-2)\times\mathbb Q[X]/(X+2)
\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
(a) Find an irreducible cubic $f(x)\in\mathbb F_2[x]$ and explain why, for any root $\alpha$, $\mathbb F_8=\mathbb F_2[\alpha]$.

(b) For the basis $B=\{1,\alpha,\alpha^2\}$, compute the matrix of the Frobenius map $T_2(\beta)=\beta^2$.

#proof
(a) Take
$$
f(x)=x^3+x+1.
$$
It has no root in $\mathbb F_2$, so as a cubic it is irreducible. Therefore
$$
[\mathbb F_2(\alpha):\mathbb F_2]=3,
$$
and this field has $2^3=8$ elements; hence it is $\mathbb F_8$.

(b) The relation $f(\alpha)=0$ gives $\alpha^3=\alpha+1$. Therefore
$$
T_2(1)=1,
\qquad
T_2(\alpha)=\alpha^2,
\qquad
T_2(\alpha^2)=\alpha^4=\alpha^2+\alpha.
$$
Using these coordinate vectors as columns, the matrix is
$$
[T_2]_B=
\begin{pmatrix}
1&0&0\\
0&0&1\\
0&1&1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=11}
#problem
Let $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-15})$.

(a) Describe its ring $A$ of algebraic integers.

(b) Does $3$ remain prime in $A$?

(c) Does $2$ ramify in $A$?

#proof
(a) Since $-15$ is squarefree and congruent to $1$ modulo $4$,
$$
A=\mathbb Z[\omega],
\qquad
\omega=\frac{1+\sqrt{-15}}2.
$$
The minimal polynomial of $\omega$ is
$$
x^2-x+4.
$$

(b) No. The field discriminant is $-15$, so $3$ ramifies. More explicitly, modulo $3$,
$$
x^2-x+4\equiv(x+1)^2,
$$
and
$$
(3)=\mathfrak p_3^2,
\qquad
\mathfrak p_3=(3,\omega+1).
$$
Thus $(3)$ is not prime.

(c) No. Since $2$ does not divide the discriminant $-15$, it is unramified. In fact
$$
x^2-x+4\equiv x(x+1)\pmod2,
$$
so $2$ splits:
$$
(2)=(2,\omega)(2,\omega-1).
$$
