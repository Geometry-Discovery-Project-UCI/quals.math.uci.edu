::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ have order $60$ and contain a normal subgroup of order $5$. Show that $G$ has an element of order $15$.

#proof
Let $P\triangleleft G$ have order $5$. The quotient $G/P$ has order $12$, so by Cauchy's theorem it contains an element of order $3$. Since $P$ is finite, lifting this element and taking a suitable power gives an element $x\in G$ of order $3$.

Conjugation by $x$ defines a homomorphism
$$
\langle x\rangle\to\operatorname{Aut}(P).
$$
The source has order $3$, while $\operatorname{Aut}(P)\cong C_4$, so the homomorphism is trivial. Thus $x$ commutes with every element of $P$. If $y$ generates $P$, then $xy$ has order $3\cdot5=15$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $H\ne G$ be a subgroup containing every proper subgroup $K\ne G$ of $G$. What can be said about $G$?

#proof
Choose $g\notin H$. The cyclic subgroup $\langle g\rangle$ cannot be proper, because every proper subgroup lies in $H$. Hence $G=\langle g\rangle$ is cyclic.

It cannot be infinite cyclic: if $H=m\mathbb Z$ under an identification $G\cong\mathbb Z$, then one can choose a proper subgroup $k\mathbb Z$ not contained in $m\mathbb Z$. Thus $G$ is finite cyclic. The hypothesis says it has a unique maximal proper subgroup. A finite cyclic group has one maximal subgroup for each distinct prime divisor of its order, so its order has only one prime divisor. Therefore
$$
G\cong C_{p^n}
$$
for some prime $p$, and $H$ is its unique maximal subgroup of order $p^{n-1}$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $G$ be finite and simple. If $G$ has exactly $p+1$ Sylow $p$-subgroups, show that $p^2\nmid|G|$.

#proof
Conjugation on the set of $p+1$ Sylow $p$-subgroups gives a nontrivial homomorphism
$$
G\to S_{p+1}.
$$
Its kernel is normal, so simplicity makes the action faithful. Hence $|G|$ divides $(p+1)!$. But
$$
v_p((p+1)!)=1,
$$
because among $1,\ldots,p+1$ only $p$ is divisible by $p$. Therefore $p^2$ does not divide $(p+1)!$, and consequently cannot divide $|G|$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Determine which of the matrices
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
B=\begin{pmatrix}1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
C=\begin{pmatrix}1&2\\0&1\end{pmatrix},\quad
D=\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
are similar over $\mathbb Z$, and which are similar over $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$.

#proof
Over $\mathbb Z$, $A$ has minimal polynomial $x-1$, while $B$ and $C$ have $(x-1)^2$, and $D$ has characteristic polynomial $x^2-1$. Thus only $B$ and $C$ could possibly be similar. But conjugation by a unimodular integer matrix preserves the ideal generated by all entries of $M-I$. The entries of $B-I$ generate $(1)$, while those of $C-I$ generate $(2)$. Hence $B$ and $C$ are not similar. Thus no two distinct matrices in the list are similar over $\mathbb Z$.

Over $\mathbb F_2$, $C=A$. Both $B-I$ and $D-I$ are nonzero nilpotent matrices of rank $1$ whose square is zero, so both have the single Jordan form $J_2(1)$ and are similar. Therefore the two similarity classes are
$$
\{A,C\}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\{B,D\}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $A=\mathbb Z[i]$. Compute the order of the unit group of $A/13A$.

#proof
Since $5^2=25\equiv-1\pmod{13}$, the polynomial $x^2+1$ splits into two distinct linear factors over $\mathbb F_{13}$. Hence
$$
A/13A
\cong\mathbb F_{13}[x]/(x^2+1)
\cong\mathbb F_{13}\times\mathbb F_{13}.
$$
Its unit group has order
$$
(13-1)^2=144.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $A=M_2(\mathbb R)$. Prove that its center is exactly the set of matrices
$$
(xy-yx)^2,
\qquad x,y\in A.
$$

#proof
Every commutator $C=xy-yx$ has trace zero. For a $2\times2$ trace-zero matrix, Cayley-Hamilton gives
$$
C^2=-\det(C)I.
$$
Thus every square of a commutator is scalar and lies in $Z(A)$.

Conversely, every scalar matrix occurs. If $\lambda\ge0$, put $t=\sqrt\lambda$ and take
$$
x=\begin{pmatrix}0&t\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
y=\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then $[x,y]=\operatorname{diag}(t,-t)$ and $[x,y]^2=\lambda I$.

If $\lambda<0$, put $t=\sqrt{-\lambda}$ and take
$$
x=\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
y=\begin{pmatrix}0&-t\\-t&0\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then
$$
[x,y]=\begin{pmatrix}0&-t\\t&0\end{pmatrix}
$$
and its square is $-t^2I=\lambda I$. Since $Z(M_2(\mathbb R))$ is precisely the scalar matrices, the two sets coincide.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $R$ be an associative ring with $1$, let $M$ be a right $R$-module, and let $f:M\to R$ be a surjective module homomorphism. Prove that
$$
M=K\oplus L,
$$
where $f(K)=0$ and $f|_L:L\to R$ is an isomorphism.

#proof
Choose $m\in M$ with $f(m)=1$. Put
$$
K=\ker f,
\qquad
L=mR.
$$
The map $R\to L$, $r\mapsto mr$, is injective because $mr=0$ implies
$$
r=f(mr)=f(m)r=r=0,
$$
and it is surjective by definition. Moreover, $f(mr)=r$, so $f|_L$ is its inverse and is an isomorphism.

For any $x\in M$,
$$
x=(x-mf(x))+mf(x),
$$
where the first term lies in $K$ and the second in $L$. Also $K\cap L=0$ because $f|_L$ is injective. Hence $M=K\oplus L$.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Find the Galois group over $\mathbb Q$ of
$$
3x^3-9x^2+9x-5.
$$

#proof
Writing $y=x-1$ transforms the equation into
$$
3y^3-2=0,
$$
so its splitting field is that of $y^3-2/3$. The polynomial is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$ because $2/3$ is not a rational cube. Its discriminant is
$$
-27(2/3)^2=-12,
$$
which is not a square in $\mathbb Q$. An irreducible cubic has Galois group $A_3$ when its discriminant is a square and $S_3$ otherwise. Therefore the Galois group is
$$
S_3.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $E$ be the splitting field of $x^8-1$ over a field $F$ with four elements. Find $|E|$.

#proof
The field $F$ has characteristic $2$. Hence
$$
x^8-1=(x-1)^8
$$
in $F[x]$. Its only root is $1$, already in $F$, so its splitting field is $E=F$ and
$$
|E|=4.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Let $f(x)\in F[x]$ be nonzero and monic. Suppose its roots in its splitting field are distinct and the root set is closed under multiplication. Prove that $f(x)=x^n-1$ or $f(x)=x^n-x$ for some natural number $n$.

#proof
Let $S$ be the root set. If $0\notin S$, then $S$ is a finite nonempty multiplicatively closed subset of the field's multiplicative group. For any $a\in S$, two positive powers agree, and cancellation shows that $1$ and $a^{-1}$ belong to $S$. Thus $S$ is a finite subgroup of the multiplicative group of a field, hence cyclic. If $|S|=n$, then $S$ is exactly the set of roots of $x^n-1$, so the monic squarefree polynomial with root set $S$ is
$$
f(x)=x^n-1.
$$

If $0\in S$ and $S$ contains a nonzero element, then $S\setminus\{0\}$ is similarly a finite cyclic group, say of order $m$. Therefore
$$
f(x)=x(x^m-1)=x^{m+1}-x.
$$

There is one exceptional case omitted from the printed statement: if $S=\{0\}$, then $f(x)=x$, which is not of either displayed form for a positive natural number $n$. Thus the claimed conclusion is correct provided the root set contains a nonzero element; without that hypothesis, $f(x)=x$ is a counterexample.
