::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ be the group of orientation-preserving rotations of $\mathbb R^3$ that carry the cube

$$
C=\{(x,y,z):|x|,|y|,|z|\leq1\}
$$

to itself. Prove that $G\cong S_4$.

#proof
A cube has four pairs of opposite vertices, equivalently four long body diagonals. Every rotational symmetry permutes these four diagonals, giving a homomorphism

$$
\varphi:G\to S_4.
$$

If a rotation fixes all four body diagonals, it fixes at least three linearly independent directions. An orientation-preserving orthogonal transformation with this property is the identity, so $\varphi$ is injective.

There are $24$ rotational symmetries of the cube: the image of a chosen face can be any of six faces, and after choosing that image there are four rotations determining the image of an adjacent vertex. Hence $|G|=24=|S_4|$. The injective homomorphism $\varphi$ is therefore an isomorphism.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $p>2$ be prime. Prove that a group of order

$$
p^{p+3}(p^2+p+1)
$$

is not simple.

#proof
Let $P$ be a Sylow $p$-subgroup and let $r$ be the number of its conjugates. Then

$$
r\mid p^2+p+1,
$$

so $r\leq p^2+p+1$. Conjugation on the Sylow $p$-subgroups gives a homomorphism $G\to S_r$. If $G$ were simple, this nontrivial action would be faithful, so $|G|$ would divide $r!$ and hence $(p^2+p+1)!$.

Legendre's formula gives

$$
v_p((p^2+p+1)!)
=\left\lfloor\frac{p^2+p+1}{p}\right\rfloor
+\left\lfloor\frac{p^2+p+1}{p^2}\right\rfloor
=p+1+1=p+2.
$$

There are no further terms. But $v_p(|G|)=p+3$, so $|G|$ cannot divide $(p^2+p+1)!$. This contradiction proves that $G$ is not simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Can the quaternion group $Q_8$ be a quotient of $S_4$?

#proof
No. If there were a surjection $S_4\to Q_8$, its kernel would have order

$$
\frac{24}{8}=3.
$$

Thus $S_4$ would have a normal subgroup of order $3$. Its subgroups of order $3$ are generated by $3$-cycles, and conjugation acts transitively on the eight $3$-cycles. Hence no individual subgroup of order $3$ is normal. Therefore $Q_8$ cannot be a quotient of $S_4$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Show that $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-2}]$ is Euclidean for the norm, and find a noninteger irreducible element.

#proof
For $z=a+b\sqrt{-2}$ define

$$
N(z)=a^2+2b^2=|z|^2.
$$

Given $\alpha,\beta$ with $\beta\neq0$, write

$$
\frac\alpha\beta=x+y\sqrt{-2},
\qquad x,y\in\mathbb R.
$$

Choose integers $m,n$ with $|x-m|\leq1/2$ and $|y-n|\leq1/2$, and put $q=m+n\sqrt{-2}$. Then

$$
N\left(\frac\alpha\beta-q\right)
\leq\frac14+2\cdot\frac14=\frac34<1.
$$

For $r=\alpha-\beta q$, multiplicativity of the norm gives $N(r)<N(\beta)$ unless $r=0$. Thus the ring is Euclidean.

The element $\sqrt{-2}$ has norm $2$. If it factored into two nonunits, their positive integer norms would both be at least $2$, while their product would be $2$, impossible. Hence $\sqrt{-2}$ is a noninteger irreducible.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $R$ be finite, and suppose strictly more than two-thirds of its elements are idempotent. Let $A$ be the set of idempotents and $Z=A\cap(A-1)$.

**(a)** Prove that $2a=0$ for $a\in Z$.

**(b)** Prove that $(Z,+)$ is a subgroup of index at most $2$ in $(R,+)$.

**(c)** Prove that $A$ is an additive subgroup and conclude that every element of $R$ is idempotent.

#proof
**(a)** If $a\in Z$, then both $a$ and $a+1$ are idempotent. Thus

$$
a^2=a,
\qquad
(a+1)^2=a+1.
$$

Subtracting gives $2a=0$.

**(b)** If $a,b\in Z$, then $2a=2b=0$. Consequently

$$
(a+b)^2=a^2+b^2+2ab=a+b,
$$

and similarly $(a+b+1)^2=a+b+1$. Thus $a+b\in Z$, and additive inverses equal the elements themselves. Hence $Z$ is a subgroup.

Since $|A-1|=|A|$, inclusion-exclusion gives

$$
|Z|\geq2|A|-|R|>\frac{|R|}{3}.
$$

As $|Z|$ divides $|R|$, its index is an integer smaller than $3$, hence at most $2$.

**(c)** If $Z=R$, then every element is idempotent. Otherwise $[R:Z]=2$. Since $|A|>|R|/2=|Z|$, choose $a\in A\setminus Z$. For $z\in Z$,

$$
(a+z)^2=a^2+z^2+2az=a+z
$$

because $2z=0$. Thus $a+Z\subseteq A$, and already $Z\subseteq A$. The two cosets exhaust $R$, so $A=R$. In particular, $A$ is an additive subgroup and every element is idempotent.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
A finitely generated $R$-module $M$ is called invertible if $M\otimes_RN\cong R$ for some finitely generated $N$.

**(a)** Find all invertible $\mathbb Z$-modules.

**(b)** Prove that the tensor product of two invertible modules is invertible.

**(c)** Prove that every invertible module is projective.

#proof
**(a)** By the structure theorem, a finitely generated abelian group is $\mathbb Z^r\oplus T$. If $M\otimes N\cong\mathbb Z$, comparing ranks gives $\operatorname{rank}M=\operatorname{rank}N=1$. Tensoring the torsion subgroup of either module with the free rank-one part of the other would produce torsion in $M\otimes N$, so both torsion subgroups vanish. Hence $M\cong\mathbb Z$, and $\mathbb Z$ is indeed invertible. It is the only isomorphism class.

**(b)** If $M_i\otimes N_i\cong R$, then associativity and commutativity give

$$
(M_1\otimes M_2)\otimes(N_1\otimes N_2)
\cong(M_1\otimes N_1)\otimes(M_2\otimes N_2)
\cong R.
$$

Thus $M_1\otimes M_2$ is invertible.

**(c)** Let $\theta:M\otimes N\to R$ be an isomorphism and write

$$
\theta^{-1}(1)=\sum_{j=1}^r m_j\otimes n_j.
$$

Define $f_j\in\operatorname{Hom}_R(M,R)$ by $f_j(m)=\theta(m\otimes n_j)$. The inverse identities for the tensor equivalence imply the dual-basis formula

$$
m=\sum_{j=1}^r f_j(m)m_j.
$$

Thus $M$ has a finite dual basis. By the dual-basis criterion, $M$ is finitely generated projective.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
For $g(x)=x^{20}+x^{10}+1$, find its splitting field over **(a)** $\mathbb F_5$ and **(b)** $\mathbb F_7$.

#proof
**(a)** In characteristic $5$,

$$
g(x)=(x^4+x^2+1)^5.
$$

The roots of $x^4+x^2+1$ are the sixth roots of unity that are not roots of $x^2-1$. They all lie in $\mathbb F_{25}$ because $6\mid25-1$, but not all lie in $\mathbb F_5$ because $3\nmid5-1$. Hence the splitting field is $\mathbb F_{25}$.

**(b)** In characteristic $7$, the roots of $g$ are the $30$th roots of unity that are not $10$th roots. Thus the splitting field is the smallest $\mathbb F_{7^d}$ containing all $30$th roots of unity, equivalently the smallest $d$ with

$$
30\mid7^d-1.
$$

The order of $7$ modulo $30$ is $4$, so the splitting field is $\mathbb F_{7^4}$.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $f\in\mathbb Q[x]$ be monic cubic with distinct roots $r,s,t$. Let $g$ be the monic polynomial with roots

$$
r^2+s+t,\quad s^2+t+r,\quad t^2+r+s.
$$

**(a)** Prove that $g\in\mathbb Q[x]$.

**(b)** If $\operatorname{Gal}(f)\cong S_3$, prove that $\operatorname{Gal}(g)\cong S_3$.

#proof
Put $e_1=r+s+t\in\mathbb Q$ and $h(x)=x^2-x+e_1$. The three displayed roots are $h(r),h(s),h(t)$. Every automorphism of the splitting field permutes $r,s,t$, and therefore permutes $h(r),h(s),h(t)$. Their elementary symmetric functions are fixed by the Galois group and lie in $\mathbb Q$, proving **(a)**.

For **(b)**, the three transformed roots are distinct. Indeed,

$$
h(r)-h(s)=(r-s)(r+s-1).
$$

If $h(r)=h(s)$, then $r+s=1$ and $t=e_1-1\in\mathbb Q$, contradicting the transitivity of the $S_3$-action on the roots of $f$. The same applies to other pairs. Thus the $S_3$-action on the transformed roots is the same faithful permutation action as on $r,s,t$. Their splitting field is therefore the original splitting field, and its Galois group is $S_3$.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $L=\mathbb F_{p^2}$, $K=\mathbb F_p$, and $F:L\to L$ be $F(x)=x^p$. Regard $L$ as a two-dimensional $K$-vector space. Prove that $F$ is $K$-linear and find its characteristic and minimal polynomials.

#proof
In characteristic $p$,

$$
(x+y)^p=x^p+y^p,
$$

and for $c\in K$, $c^p=c$. Hence $F(x+y)=F(x)+F(y)$ and $F(cx)=cF(x)$, so $F$ is $K$-linear.

Every $x\in L$ satisfies $x^{p^2}=x$, so $F^2=I$. The map $F$ is not the identity, since its fixed field is exactly $K$, a proper subfield of $L$. Therefore its minimal polynomial is

$$
m_F(t)=t^2-1.
$$

Since the vector-space dimension is $2$, the characteristic polynomial is also

$$
\chi_F(t)=t^2-1.
$$

In characteristic $2$, this is $(t-1)^2$, and the conclusion remains valid.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
For every $n\geq3$, construct a nonsingular matrix $A\neq\pm I$ over $\mathbb F_3$ such that $I+A^2=A^{-1}$.

#proof
The required identity is equivalent to

$$
A^3+A-I=0.
$$

Over $\mathbb F_3$, let $C$ be the companion matrix of

$$
f(x)=x^3+x-1.
$$

Then $f(C)=0$, $C$ is nonsingular because the constant term of $f$ is nonzero, and $C$ is not scalar. For $n\geq3$, set

$$
A=C\oplus(-I_{n-3}).
$$

The scalar $-1$ is also a root of $f$ in $\mathbb F_3$, since $(-1)^3+(-1)-1=-3=0$. Hence $f(A)=0$, so

$$
A(I+A^2)=A+A^3=I.
$$

Thus $I+A^2=A^{-1}$. The matrix is nonsingular and is not $\pm I$ because of the companion block.
