#problem
**(a)** Prove that every group of order $15$ is cyclic.

**(b)** Is every group of order $21$ cyclic?

#proof
**(a)** Let $G$ have order $15$. Sylow's theorems give

$$
n_5\equiv1\pmod5,
\quad n_5\mid3,
$$

so $n_5=1$. Similarly,

$$
n_3\equiv1\pmod3,
\quad n_3\mid5,
$$

so $n_3=1$. The Sylow subgroups $P_5$ and $P_3$ are therefore normal. Their intersection is trivial, and elements of the two subgroups commute because their commutator lies in both. Hence

$$
G\cong P_5\times P_3\cong C_5\times C_3\cong C_{15}.
$$

**(b)** No. Since the automorphism $a\mapsto a^2$ of $C_7$ has order $3$, the semidirect product

$$
C_7\rtimes C_3
=\langle a,b\mid a^7=b^3=1,\ bab^{-1}=a^2\rangle
$$

has order $21$ and is nonabelian, hence is not cyclic.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
**(a)** If $|G|=12$ and $H\leq G$ has order $6$, prove that $g^2\in H$ for every $g\in G$.

**(b)** Prove that $A_4$ has no subgroup of order $6$.

#proof
**(a)** The subgroup $H$ has index $2$, so it is normal and $G/H$ has order $2$. Therefore

$$
(gH)^2=H
$$

for every $g$, which is equivalent to $g^2\in H$.

**(b)** Suppose $H\leq A_4$ had order $6$. By part **(a)**, $H$ would contain $g^2$ for every $g\in A_4$. Every $3$-cycle $c$ is a square, since

$$
c=(c^2)^2.
$$

Thus $H$ would contain all eight $3$-cycles of $A_4$, as well as the identity. This gives at least nine elements in a group of order $6$, a contradiction.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
If $H$ has finite index $n$ in a group $G$, prove that there is a normal subgroup $K\triangleleft G$ such that

$$
K\leq H
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
[G:K]\leq n!.
$$

#proof
Let $G$ act by left multiplication on the $n$ left cosets of $H$. This action gives a homomorphism

$$
\varphi:G\longrightarrow S_n.
$$

Set $K=\ker\varphi$. Then $K\triangleleft G$. Every element of $K$ fixes the coset $H$, so $K\leq H$. By the first isomorphism theorem,

$$
[G:K]=|\operatorname{im}\varphi|.
$$

Since the image is a subgroup of $S_n$, its order is at most $n!$. This proof does not require $G$ to be finite.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Is $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ finitely generated as a $\mathbb Z$-module?

#proof
No. Suppose it were generated by finitely many elements. All those generators can be written with denominators dividing some fixed power $2^N$. Every integer linear combination of them would then belong to

$$
\frac1{2^N}\mathbb Z.
$$

But $2^{-(N+1)}\in\mathbb Z[1/2]$ and

$$
\frac1{2^{N+1}}\notin\frac1{2^N}\mathbb Z.
$$

This contradiction proves that $\mathbb Z[1/2]$ is not finitely generated as a $\mathbb Z$-module.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $R$ be Noetherian, let $I\subseteq\mathfrak p$ be ideals, and suppose $\mathfrak p$ is the only prime ideal containing $I$. Prove that

$$
\mathfrak p^m\subseteq I
$$

for some $m>0$.

#proof
Pass to $\overline R=R/I$. By the correspondence theorem, the only prime ideal of $\overline R$ is $\overline{\mathfrak p}=\mathfrak p/I$. The nilradical is the intersection of all prime ideals, so

$$
\sqrt{(0)}=\overline{\mathfrak p}.
$$

Thus every element of $\overline{\mathfrak p}$ is nilpotent. Because $R$ is Noetherian, $\mathfrak p$ is finitely generated, say

$$
\mathfrak p=(a_1,\ldots,a_r).
$$

For each $j$, choose $n_j>0$ such that $\overline a_j^{,n_j}=0$. If

$$
m=n_1+\cdots+n_r,
$$

then every monomial of total degree $m$ in the $\overline a_j$ contains some $\overline a_j^{,n_j}$ and is zero. Hence $\overline{\mathfrak p}^{,m}=0$, which is equivalent to $\mathfrak p^m\subseteq I$.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $F$ be a field and let $A\in M_n(F)$ satisfy $A^2=I_n$ and $A\neq I_n$. Prove that $-1$ is an eigenvalue of $A$.

#proof
We have

$$
(A-I_n)(A+I_n)=A^2-I_n=0.
$$

If $-1$ were not an eigenvalue, then $A+I_n$ would be invertible. Multiplying the displayed equation by its inverse would give $A-I_n=0$, contrary to $A\neq I_n$. Therefore $A+I_n$ is singular and $-1$ is an eigenvalue. This argument also covers characteristic $2$, where $-1=1$.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
For which positive integers $e$ is $x^2+x+1$ irreducible over $\mathbb F_{2^e}$?

#proof
The roots of $x^2+x+1$ are the nonidentity cube roots of unity, because

$$
x^3-1=(x-1)(x^2+x+1).
$$

Thus the polynomial is reducible over $\mathbb F_{2^e}$ exactly when $\mathbb F_{2^e}^{\times}$ contains an element of order $3$. This multiplicative group is cyclic of order $2^e-1$, so such an element exists exactly when

$$
3\mid 2^e-1.
$$

Since $2\equiv-1\pmod3$, this divisibility holds exactly when $e$ is even. Therefore $x^2+x+1$ is irreducible precisely for odd positive integers $e$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $K/F$ be finite Galois. Suppose $\alpha\in K$, $0\neq\beta\in F$, and

$$
\sigma(\alpha)=\alpha+\beta
$$

for some $\sigma\in\operatorname{Gal}(K/F)$. Prove that $K$ has positive characteristic.

#proof
Because the Galois group is finite, $\sigma$ has finite order, say $r>0$. Since $\sigma$ fixes $\beta$, induction gives

$$
\sigma^j(\alpha)=\alpha+j\beta
$$

for every $j\geq0$. Taking $j=r$ yields

$$
\alpha=\sigma^r(\alpha)=\alpha+r\beta.
$$

Thus $r\beta=0$. Since $\beta\neq0$, the field cannot have characteristic zero. Its characteristic is therefore a positive prime dividing $r$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Give an example or explain that none exists.

**(a)** A UFD that is not a Euclidean domain.

**(b)** A positive integer $n$ such that $\mathbb Q(\zeta_n)$ contains two distinct quadratic extensions of $\mathbb Q$.

#proof
**(a)** Let $F$ be any field. The ring $F[x,y]$ is a UFD by Gauss's lemma, applied successively to polynomial rings. It is not a PID because the ideal $(x,y)$ is not principal. Since every Euclidean domain is a PID, $F[x,y]$ is not Euclidean.

**(b)** Take $n=8$. We have

$$
\mathbb Q(\zeta_8)=\mathbb Q(i,\sqrt2).
$$

It contains the two distinct quadratic fields $\mathbb Q(i)$ and $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$. Therefore $n=8$ is an example.
