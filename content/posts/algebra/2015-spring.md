::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Prove that every finite group of order greater than $2$ has a nontrivial automorphism.

#proof
If $G$ is nonabelian, then some inner automorphism $x\mapsto gxg^{-1}$ is nontrivial.

Suppose $G$ is abelian. The inversion map $x\mapsto x^{-1}$ is an automorphism. If it is nontrivial, we are done. If it is the identity, then every element satisfies $x^2=1$, so $G$ is a vector space over $\mathbb F_2$. Since $|G|>2$, its dimension is at least $2$, and a linear automorphism interchanging two basis vectors is nontrivial. Thus in all cases $G$ has a nonidentity automorphism.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
(a) Define a UFD.

(b) Define a PID.

(c) Give integral domains that satisfy both properties, exactly one property, and neither property.

#proof
(a) A unique factorization domain is an integral domain in which every nonzero nonunit is a product of irreducibles and this factorization is unique up to reordering and multiplication of factors by units.

(b) A principal ideal domain is an integral domain in which every ideal is generated by one element.

(c) Examples are:

- $\mathbb Z$ is both a PID and a UFD.

- $k[x,y]$, for any field $k$, is a UFD but not a PID; the ideal $(x,y)$ is not principal. There is no example that is a PID but not a UFD because every PID is a UFD.

- $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ is neither. The equality
$$
6=2\cdot3=(1+\sqrt{-5})(1-\sqrt{-5})
$$
gives inequivalent factorizations into irreducibles, so it is not a UFD; since every PID is a UFD, it is not a PID either.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $T$ be an indeterminate.

(a) Prove that $\mathbb Q(\sqrt[4]{T})/\mathbb Q(T)$ is not Galois.

(b) Find its Galois closure and determine the Galois group abstractly and by explicit automorphisms.

#proof
Put $K=\mathbb Q(T)$ and $a=\sqrt[4]{T}$. The polynomial $x^4-T$ is irreducible in $K[x]$, for example by Eisenstein's criterion at the prime $T$ in $\mathbb Q[T]$. Thus $[K(a):K]=4$.

(a) The roots are
$$
a,\quad ia,\quad-a,\quad-ia.
$$
The field $K(a)$ embeds in a real function field and does not contain $i$, so it does not contain all roots. Hence it is not normal and therefore not Galois.

(b) The Galois closure is
$$
L=K(a,i).
$$
Since $i\notin K(a)$, $[L:K]=8$. Define
$$
r(a)=ia,\quad r(i)=i,
$$
and
$$
s(a)=a,\quad s(i)=-i.
$$
Then $r$ has order $4$, $s$ has order $2$, and
$$
srs=r^{-1}.
$$
The eight automorphisms are $r^k$ and $sr^k$ for $0\le k<4$. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)\cong D_8.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $R$ be a commutative ring with identity. An element is nilpotent if some positive power is zero.

(a) Prove that every nilpotent element lies in every prime ideal.

(b) If every element of $R$ is either nilpotent or a unit, prove that $R$ has a unique prime ideal.

#proof
(a) Let $r^n=0$ and let $\mathfrak p$ be prime. Since $r^n\in\mathfrak p$, repeated use of primality gives $r\in\mathfrak p$.

(b) Let $N$ be the set of nilpotent elements. In a commutative ring, $N$ is an ideal: if $r^m=s^n=0$, then $(r+s)^{m+n-1}=0$, and every multiple of a nilpotent is nilpotent. By part (a), $N$ lies in every prime ideal.

If $r\notin N$, the hypothesis says that $r$ is a unit. Hence every nonzero class in $R/N$ is a unit, so $R/N$ is a field. Thus $N$ is maximal, and in particular prime. Since $N$ is contained in every prime ideal and is itself maximal, it is the unique prime ideal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Write
$$
D_n=\langle a,b\mid a^n=b^2=1,\ bab=a^{-1}\rangle,
$$
so $D_n$ has order $2n$.

(a) Prove that every subgroup of $\langle a\rangle$ is normal in $D_n$.

(b) If $n=2m$ with $m$ odd, prove that $D_n\cong C_2\times D_m$.

(c) Is $D_{12}\cong C_3\times D_4$?

#proof
(a) Every subgroup of the cyclic group $\langle a\rangle$ has the form $\langle a^d\rangle$. Conjugation by $a$ fixes it, and conjugation by $b$ sends $a^d$ to $a^{-d}$, which generates the same subgroup. Since $a,b$ generate $D_n$, the subgroup is normal.

(b) The element $a^m$ has order $2$ and is central. The subgroup
$$
H=\langle a^2,b\rangle
$$
is isomorphic to $D_m$ because $a^2$ has order $m$. Since $m$ is odd, $a^m\notin\langle a^2\rangle$, and hence
$$
\langle a^m\rangle\cap H=1.
$$
Their orders multiply to $2\cdot2m=4m=|D_{2m}|$, and the first factor is central. Therefore
$$
D_{2m}=\langle a^m\rangle\times H\cong C_2\times D_m.
$$

(c) No. The center of $D_{12}$ is $\{1,a^6\}$ and has order $2$. But
$$
Z(C_3\times D_4)=C_3\times Z(D_4)
$$
has order $3\cdot2=6$. Isomorphic groups have isomorphic centers, so the groups are not isomorphic.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Suppose $p<q$ are primes. Prove that no group of order $p^2q$ is simple.

#proof
Let $G$ have order $p^2q$. Sylow's theorems give
$$
n_q\equiv1\pmod q,
\qquad n_q\mid p^2.
$$
Thus $n_q=1$ or $p^2$. If $n_q=1$, the Sylow $q$-subgroup is normal.

If $n_q=p^2$, then $q$ divides $p^2-1=(p-1)(p+1)$. Since $q>p$, this forces $q\mid p+1$, hence $q=p+1$. Two consecutive integers greater than $2$ cannot both be prime, so the only possibility is $p=2,q=3$.

It remains to consider groups of order $12$. The number of Sylow $3$-subgroups is $1$ or $4$. If it is $1$, that subgroup is normal. If it is $4$, the four Sylow $3$-subgroups contribute $4(3-1)=8$ nonidentity elements, leaving exactly four elements including the identity. Every Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $4$ and must be this remaining set, so it is unique and normal. Thus no group of order $p^2q$ is simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Determine the maximal ideals of:

(a) $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2-5x+6)$;

(b) $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2+4x+6)$.

#proof
(a) Since
$$
x^2-5x+6=(x-2)(x-3)
$$
with relatively prime factors, the Chinese remainder theorem gives
$$
\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2-5x+6)\cong\mathbb Q\times\mathbb Q.
$$
Its maximal ideals are the images of $(x-2)$ and $(x-3)$, namely
$$
(\overline{x-2})\quad\text{and}\quad(\overline{x-3}).
$$

(b) The discriminant is
$$
4^2-4\cdot6=-8,
$$
which is not a square in $\mathbb Q$. Thus $x^2+4x+6$ is irreducible, the quotient is a field, and its only maximal ideal is the zero ideal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Find two matrices with the same characteristic and minimal polynomials but different Jordan canonical forms.

#proof
Take nilpotent $6\times6$ matrices
$$
A=J_3(0)\oplus J_3(0),
$$
and
$$
B=J_3(0)\oplus J_2(0)\oplus J_1(0).
$$
Both have characteristic polynomial $x^6$. In each case the largest Jordan block has size $3$, so both have minimal polynomial $x^3$. Their Jordan block partitions are $(3,3)$ and $(3,2,1)$, which are different; hence the matrices are not similar.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
(a) Define a perfect field.

(b) Give an example of a perfect field.

(c) Give an example of a nonperfect field.

#proof
(a) A field $F$ is perfect if every algebraic extension of $F$ is separable. Equivalently, every irreducible polynomial over $F$ is separable. In characteristic $p>0$, this is equivalent to surjectivity of the Frobenius map $x\mapsto x^p$.

(b) Every field of characteristic $0$ is perfect; for example, $\mathbb Q$ is perfect. Every finite field is also perfect.

(c) The rational function field $\mathbb F_p(t)$ is not perfect: $t$ is not a $p$th power in it, and $x^p-t$ is inseparable.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
(a) Classify the conjugacy classes of $S_3$.

(b) Construct its character table.

#proof
(a) In a symmetric group, conjugacy classes are determined by cycle type. Thus the three classes are
$$
\{1\},
\qquad\{(12),(13),(23)\},
\qquad\{(123),(132)\},
$$
of sizes $1,3,2$.

(b) The irreducible representations are the trivial representation, the sign representation, and the two-dimensional standard representation. Their character table is
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
&1&(12)&(123)\\ \hline
\text{class size}&1&3&2\\
\chi_{\mathrm{triv}}&1&1&1\\
\chi_{\mathrm{sgn}}&1&-1&1\\
\chi_{\mathrm{std}}&2&0&-1
\end{array}
$$
The squared degrees sum to $1+1+4=6=|S_3|$, so the table is complete.
::
