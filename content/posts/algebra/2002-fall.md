::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
(a) Let $D$ be differentiation on the complex vector space of polynomials of degree at most $n$. Find its Jordan normal form.

(b) On the complex vector space of polynomials in $x,y$ of total degree at most $n-1$, let
$$
D=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}+\frac{\partial}{\partial y}.
$$
Find its Jordan normal form.

#proof
(a) Differentiation is nilpotent, and
$$
x^n,Dx^n,D^2x^n,\ldots,D^nx^n
$$
form a basis. Thus $x^n$ is a cyclic vector and the Jordan form is one nilpotent block
$$
J_{n+1}(0).
$$

(b) Set $u=x+y$ and $v=x-y$. Then
$$
D=2\frac{\partial}{\partial u}.
$$
For each $b=0,1,\ldots,n-1$, the subspace
$$
v^b\operatorname{span}\{1,u,\ldots,u^{n-1-b}\}
$$
is $D$-invariant and gives one nilpotent Jordan chain of length $n-b$. These subspaces form a direct sum of the whole polynomial space. Hence the Jordan form is
$$
J_n(0)\oplus J_{n-1}(0)\oplus\cdots\oplus J_1(0).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
(a) Describe the conjugacy classes of $A_5$.

(b) Show that a normal subgroup of a group is a union of conjugacy classes.

(c) Use (a) and (b) to prove that $A_5$ is simple.

#proof
(a) The conjugacy classes in $A_5$ have the following representatives and sizes:
$$
1\quad(1),
$$
$$
(123)\quad(20),
$$
$$
(12)(34)\quad(15),
$$
and two distinct classes of $5$-cycles, each of size $12$, represented for example by
$$
(12345)\quad(12),
\qquad
(13524)\quad(12).
$$
The $S_5$-class of $5$-cycles splits in $A_5$ because its centralizer lies in $A_5$.

(b) If $H\triangleleft G$, $h\in H$, and $g\in G$, then $ghg^{-1}\in H$. Thus, whenever $H$ contains one member of a conjugacy class, it contains the whole class.

(c) A normal subgroup of $A_5$ must contain the identity and be a union of some of the four nonidentity classes of sizes $20,15,12,12$. Its order must also divide $60$. Checking sums of $1$ with subsets of these four sizes shows that none is a divisor of $60$ except $1$ and $60$. Therefore the only normal subgroups are $\{1\}$ and $A_5$, so $A_5$ is simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $p_1<p_2$ be primes.

(a) Determine the abelian groups of order $p_1p_2$.

(b) If $|G|=p_1p_2$ and $p_1\nmid(p_2-1)$, show that $G$ is abelian.

(c) List all abelian groups of order $64$.

(d) Are all groups of order $p^3$ abelian?

#proof
(a) The primary decomposition theorem gives
$$
G\cong C_{p_1}\times C_{p_2}\cong C_{p_1p_2}.
$$
Thus there is exactly one abelian group of this order.

(b) The number $n_{p_2}$ of Sylow $p_2$-subgroups divides $p_1$ and is congruent to $1$ modulo $p_2$, so it is $1$. The number $n_{p_1}$ divides $p_2$ and is congruent to $1$ modulo $p_1$. If it were $p_2$, then $p_1\mid p_2-1$, contrary to the hypothesis. Thus it is also $1$. Both Sylow subgroups are normal, intersect trivially, and commute, so
$$
G\cong C_{p_1}\times C_{p_2}
$$
is abelian.

(c) Since $64=2^6$, the eleven groups correspond to the partitions of $6$:
$$
C_{64},
$$
$$
C_{32}\times C_2,
$$
$$
C_{16}\times C_4,
\qquad C_{16}\times C_2^2,
$$
$$
C_8\times C_8,
\qquad C_8\times C_4\times C_2,
\qquad C_8\times C_2^3,
$$
$$
C_4^3,
\qquad C_4^2\times C_2^2,
\qquad C_4\times C_2^4,
$$
and
$$
C_2^6.
$$

(d) No. The unitriangular group
$$
UT_3(\mathbb F_p)=
\left\{
\begin{pmatrix}1&a&c\\0&1&b\\0&0&1\end{pmatrix}:a,b,c\in\mathbb F_p
\right\}
$$
has order $p^3$ and is nonabelian.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
(a) For $A,B\in M_n(\mathbb C)$, can $AB-BA=I$?

(b) For $A,B\in M_n(\mathbb F_p)$, can $AB-BA=I$?

#proof
(a) No. The trace of every commutator is zero because
$$
\operatorname{tr}(AB-BA)=\operatorname{tr}(AB)-\operatorname{tr}(BA)=0,
$$
whereas $\operatorname{tr}(I)=n\ne0$ over $\mathbb C$.

(b) Such matrices exist exactly when $p\mid n$. The same trace argument proves necessity. For sufficiency when $n=p$, act on
$$
V=\mathbb F_p[x]/(x^p).
$$
Let $A=d/dx$ and let $B$ be multiplication by $x$. Then the product rule gives
$$
AB-BA=I_V.
$$
These are $p\times p$ matrices over $\mathbb F_p$. If $n=kp$, take block-diagonal matrices consisting of $k$ copies of this pair. Their commutator is $I_n$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Use the surjective homomorphism $\rho:SU(2)\to SO(3,\mathbb R)$ with kernel $\{\pm I\}$.

(a) If $H\triangleleft G$, $a\in G$, and $h\in H$, show that $[h,a]=hah^{-1}a^{-1}\in H$.

(b) Describe all conjugacy classes of $SU(2)$.

(c) Show that $\{\pm I\}$ is the only nontrivial proper normal subgroup of $SU(2)$ and deduce that $SO(3,\mathbb R)$ is simple.

#proof
(a) Since $H$ is normal, $aha^{-1}\in H$. Therefore
$$
[h,a]=h(ah^{-1}a^{-1})\in H.
$$

(b) Every unitary matrix is unitarily diagonalizable. If it has determinant $1$, its eigenvalues are $e^{i\theta}$ and $e^{-i\theta}$. The diagonalizing matrix may be adjusted by a scalar to have determinant $1$, so every element of $SU(2)$ is conjugate in $SU(2)$ to
$$
d_\theta=\begin{pmatrix}e^{i\theta}&0\\0&e^{-i\theta}\end{pmatrix},
\qquad0\le\theta\le\pi.
$$
Two such matrices are conjugate exactly when they have the same trace $2\cos\theta$, so this list parametrizes all conjugacy classes.

(c) Let $N\triangleleft SU(2)$ contain a noncentral element $d_\theta$, where $0<\theta<\pi$. Part (a), applied while the second element varies through conjugates near the identity, shows that $N$ contains rotations through all sufficiently small angles about one axis. Normality then gives the corresponding small rotations about every axis. Products of these elements form a neighborhood of the identity in $SU(2)$. Hence $N$ is an open subgroup. Since $SU(2)$ is connected, it has no proper open subgroup, so $N=SU(2)$. Therefore every proper normal subgroup is contained in the center $\{\pm I\}$, and the only nontrivial one is $\{\pm I\}$.

Normal subgroups of $SO(3)$ correspond under $\rho$ to normal subgroups of $SU(2)$ containing $\ker\rho=\{\pm I\}$. The only such subgroups are the kernel and all of $SU(2)$, whose images are $\{1\}$ and $SO(3)$. Hence $SO(3,\mathbb R)$ is simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $p$ be prime, let $a=q_1\cdots q_r$ be a product of distinct primes, and let $\alpha$ generate $\mathbb F_p^*$.

(a) Show that $x^2\equiv q_i\pmod p$ is solvable if and only if $q_i\equiv\alpha^{2k}\pmod p$ for some integer $k$.

(b) If $q_i\equiv\alpha^{k_i}\pmod p$, show that $x^2\equiv a\pmod p$ is solvable if and only if $\sum_i k_i$ is even.

(c) Let $P_q$ be the primes outside $\{q_1,\ldots,q_r\}$. For $p\in P_q$, define $v_p=(\varepsilon_1,\ldots,\varepsilon_r)\in\mathbb F_2^r$, where $\varepsilon_i=0$ or $1$ according as $q_i$ is or is not a square modulo $p$. The printed problem assumes $p_1p_2\equiv p_3\pmod{q_i}$ for every $i$ and asks to prove $v_{p_1}+v_{p_2}=v_{p_3}$.

#proof
(a) The squares in the cyclic group $\mathbb F_p^*=\langle\alpha\rangle$ are exactly its even powers $\alpha^{2k}$.

(b) We have
$$
a=q_1\cdots q_r\equiv\alpha^{k_1+\cdots+k_r}\pmod p.
$$
Because $p-1$ is even, a power of $\alpha$ is a square exactly when its exponent is even. This proves the assertion.

(c) As printed, this assertion is false because the congruences modulo the $q_i$ do not control the sign in quadratic reciprocity. Take $r=1$, $q_1=3$, and
$$
p_1=5,\qquad p_2=7,\qquad p_3=17.
$$
Then
$$
p_1p_2=35\equiv2\equiv17=p_3\pmod3.
$$
But $3$ is a nonsquare modulo each of $5,7,17$, so
$$
v_5=v_7=v_{17}=(1).
$$
Consequently $v_5+v_7=(0)\ne(1)=v_{17}$ in $\mathbb F_2$. A condition controlling the primes modulo $4$ as well as modulo each $q_i$ would make the intended quadratic-reciprocity argument possible.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Chevalley's theorem says that if a homogeneous polynomial in $n$ variables over $\mathbb F_p$ has degree $r<n$, then its number of zeros is divisible by $p$.

(a) Show that $r<n$ is essential, using $x^2-2y^2$ over $\mathbb F_5$.

(b) For $p>5$, use Chevalley's theorem on $x^2+y^2-z^2$ to show that $x^2+y^2=1$ has a solution over $\mathbb F_p$.

#proof
(a) Here the number of variables and the degree are both $2$. Since $2$ is not a square modulo $5$, the equation
$$
x^2=2y^2
$$
has only the solution $(0,0)$. Thus the number of zeros is $1$, not divisible by $5$.

(b) The homogeneous polynomial
$$
F(x,y,z)=x^2+y^2-z^2
$$
has degree $2<3$, so its number $N$ of zeros is divisible by $p$. There is at least the zero solution, and therefore there must be nonzero solutions.

We need one with $z\ne0$. If $-1$ is not a square, the only solution with $z=0$ is $(0,0,0)$, so any other solution works. If $-1$ is a square, there are exactly $2(p-1)+1=2p-1$ solutions with $z=0$, a number not divisible by $p$. Since the total $N$ is divisible by $p$, some solution must have $z\ne0$. Dividing its coordinates by $z$ gives
$$
(x/z)^2+(y/z)^2=1.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
(a) Let $f(x)$ be an irreducible degree-$5$ polynomial over $\mathbb Q$ with exactly three real roots. Show that its Galois group is $S_5$.

(b) Prove that $f(x)=x^5-25x+3$ is irreducible.

(c) Find the Galois group of this polynomial.

#proof
(a) Irreducibility makes the Galois group $G\le S_5$ transitive, so $5\mid|G|$ and Cauchy's theorem gives a $5$-cycle in $G$. Complex conjugation fixes the three real roots and interchanges the two nonreal roots, so $G$ also contains a transposition. The conjugates of this transposition by powers of the $5$-cycle are transpositions along a connected $5$-cycle graph, and such transpositions generate $S_5$. Hence $G=S_5$.

(b) Translate by $2$:
$$
f(x+2)=x^5+10x^4+40x^3+80x^2+55x-15.
$$
Every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $5$, while the constant term is not divisible by $25$. Eisenstein's criterion at $5$ proves that $f(x+2)$, and hence $f(x)$, is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

(c) Put $a=5^{1/4}$. The derivative is
$$
f'(x)=5(x^4-5),
$$
so the real critical points are $-a$ and $a$. Moreover,
$$
f(-a)=20a+3>0,
\qquad
f(a)=3-20a<0.
$$
Together with the behavior at $\pm\infty$, this shows that $f$ has exactly three real roots. Parts (a) and (b) now give
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(f/\mathbb Q)\cong S_5.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $Q_8=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$.

(a) Give representatives of its conjugacy classes, and find its center and commutator subgroup.

(b) Show that every subgroup of $Q_8$ is normal.

(c) List all irreducible complex representations of $Q_8$.

#proof
(a) The conjugacy classes are
$$
\{1\},\quad\{-1\},\quad\{i,-i\},\quad\{j,-j\},\quad\{k,-k\}.
$$
Thus representatives are $1,-1,i,j,k$. The center is
$$
Z(Q_8)=\{\pm1\},
$$
and, since $Q_8/Z(Q_8)\cong C_2\times C_2$ while $[i,j]=-1$, the commutator subgroup is
$$
[Q_8,Q_8]=\{\pm1\}.
$$

(b) The subgroups are $\{1\}$, $\{\pm1\}$, the three cyclic subgroups $\langle i\rangle$, $\langle j\rangle$, $\langle k\rangle$ of order $4$, and $Q_8$. The order-$4$ subgroups have index $2$ and hence are normal; the remaining cases are immediate.

(c) The abelianization is $C_2\times C_2$, so there are four one-dimensional representations. They all send $-1$ to $1$, and are obtained by independently choosing the signs assigned to $i$ and $j$; the value on $k=ij$ is their product.

There is one further irreducible representation of degree $2$, given by
$$
i\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}i&0\\0&-i\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
j\longmapsto
\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{pmatrix},
$$
with $k$ mapped to the product of these matrices. It is irreducible because the two displayed matrices have no common invariant line. The squared dimensions sum to
$$
4\cdot1^2+2^2=8,
$$
so this list is complete.
