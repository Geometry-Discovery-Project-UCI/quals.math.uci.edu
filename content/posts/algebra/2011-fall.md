::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Show that $\sqrt[4]{2}$ is not contained in any field $L$ that is Galois over $\mathbb Q$ with
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)\cong S_n
$$
for any positive integer $n$. You may use that the Galois group of $x^4-2$ over $\mathbb Q$ is $D_8$.

#proof
Suppose $\alpha=\sqrt[4]{2}\in L$. Since $L/\mathbb Q$ is normal, it contains every conjugate of $\alpha$, hence it contains the splitting field
$$
K=\mathbb Q(\alpha,i)
$$
of $x^4-2$. Because $K/\mathbb Q$ is Galois, restriction gives a surjection
$$
S_n\cong\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb Q)
\twoheadrightarrow\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)cong D_8.
$$
But $D_8$ is not a quotient of any symmetric group. For $n\ge5$, the only proper nontrivial normal subgroup of $S_n$ is $A_n$, so its only nontrivial proper quotient is $C_2$. For $n=4$, the normal subgroups are $1,V_4,A_4,S_4$, giving no quotient of order $8$; and for $n\le3$, the group order is too small. This contradiction proves the claim.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $F$ be algebraically closed of characteristic $p$, and write
$$
n=p^am,
$$
where $p\nmid m$. How many $n$th roots of unity are in $F$?

#proof
In characteristic $p$,
$$
x^n-1=x^{p^am}-1=(x^m-1)^{p^a}.
$$
Thus $x^n-1$ and $x^m-1$ have the same distinct roots. Since $p\nmid m$, the derivative
$$
mx^{m-1}
$$
has no common root with $x^m-1$, so $x^m-1$ is separable. It splits in the algebraically closed field $F$ and has exactly $m$ roots. Therefore there are exactly
$$
m
$$
distinct $n$th roots of unity in $F$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
A commutative ring $R$ with $1\ne0$ is Boolean if $x^2=x$ for every $x\in R$.

(a) Find all Boolean integral domains.

(b) Prove that every prime ideal in a Boolean ring is maximal.

#proof
(a) In a Boolean domain,
$$
x(x-1)=0
$$
for every $x$. Since there are no zero divisors, every element is $0$ or $1$. Thus the only Boolean integral domain is $\mathbb F_2$, which is indeed Boolean.

(b) If $P$ is prime, then $R/P$ is an integral domain. It is also Boolean because the identity $x^2=x$ passes to quotients. By part (a),
$$
R/P\cong\mathbb F_2,
$$
which is a field. Hence $P$ is maximal.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Determine the Galois closure $F$ of
$$
\mathbb Q\bigl(\sqrt{1+\sqrt2}\bigr)/\mathbb Q.
$$
Describe all elements of its Galois group by their actions on generators, and identify the group abstractly.

#proof
Let
$$
\alpha=\sqrt{1+\sqrt2},
\qquad
\beta=\sqrt{1-\sqrt2}.
$$
Then $\alpha$ satisfies
$$
f(x)=x^4-2x^2-1.
$$
The element $1+\sqrt2$ is not a square in $\mathbb Q(\sqrt2)$: writing it as $(a+b\sqrt2)^2$ would give $2ab=1$ and $a^2+2b^2=1$, which have no rational solution. Thus $f$ is irreducible and $[\mathbb Q(\alpha):\mathbb Q]=4$.

Since
$$
\alpha\beta=\sqrt{-1}=i,
$$
the splitting field of $f$, whose roots are $\pm\alpha,\pm\beta$, is
$$
F=\mathbb Q(\alpha,i).
$$
The field $\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ is real, so $i\notin\mathbb Q(\alpha)$ and $[F:\mathbb Q]=8$.

Define
$$
r(\alpha)=\beta=\frac{i}{\alpha},
\qquad r(i)=-i,
$$
and
$$
s(\alpha)=\alpha,
\qquad s(i)=-i.
$$
Then $r$ cycles the roots as
$$
\alpha\mapsto\beta\mapsto-\alpha\mapsto-\beta\mapsto\alpha,
$$
so $r$ has order $4$; $s$ has order $2$, and $srs=r^{-1}$. The eight automorphisms are
$$
1,r,r^2,r^3,s,sr,sr^2,sr^3,
$$
with their actions obtained from the displayed formulas. Hence
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)\cong D_8.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Suppose $G$ is finite of odd order and $H\trianglelefteq G$ has order $5$. Show that $H\subseteq Z(G)$.

#proof
Conjugation defines a homomorphism
$$
G\longrightarrow\operatorname{Aut}(H).
$$
Since $H\cong C_5$, its automorphism group has order $4$. The image of the homomorphism has order dividing both $|G|$, which is odd, and $4$. Hence the image is trivial. Therefore every element of $G$ centralizes every element of $H$, so $H\subseteq Z(G)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Classify all finite groups whose automorphism group is trivial.

#proof
If $\operatorname{Aut}(G)=1$, then every inner automorphism is trivial. Hence
$$
G/Z(G)=1,
$$
so $G$ is abelian. Inversion $g\mapsto g^{-1}$ is then an automorphism and must be the identity. Thus every element has order dividing $2$, and
$$
G\cong(C_2)^r
$$
for some $r\ge0$.

Its automorphism group is $GL_r(\mathbb F_2)$, which is trivial only for $r=0$ or $r=1$. Therefore the only finite groups with trivial automorphism group are
$$
1\quad\text{and}\quad C_2.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $f(x)\in F[x]$ be irreducible, let $E$ be its splitting field, and suppose both $\alpha$ and $\alpha+1$ are roots. Show that $\operatorname{char}F\ne0$.

#proof
Because $\alpha$ and $\alpha+1$ have the same irreducible polynomial, the $F$-embedding
$$
F(\alpha)\to E,
\qquad \alpha\mapsto\alpha+1,
$$
extends to an $F$-automorphism $\sigma$ of the splitting field $E$. Hence
$$
\sigma^k(\alpha)=\alpha+k
$$
for every positive integer $k$.

The automorphism group of the finite extension $E/F$ is finite, so $\sigma$ has finite order $m$. Then
$$
\alpha=\sigma^m(\alpha)=\alpha+m,
$$
which gives $m\cdot1_F=0$. Therefore $F$ has positive characteristic.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $V\subset\mathbb C[X,Y,Z]$ be the six-dimensional space of homogeneous quadratic polynomials. Let $S_3$ act by permuting the variables.

(a) Give the character table of $S_3$.

(b) Find the character $\chi$ of this representation.

(c) Decompose $\chi$ into irreducible characters.

#proof
(a) With classes represented by $1,(12),(123)$, the character table is
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrr}
&1&(12)&(123)\\ \hline
\chi_{\mathrm{triv}}&1&1&1\\
\chi_{\mathrm{sgn}}&1&-1&1\\
\chi_{\mathrm{std}}&2&0&-1
\end{array}
$$

(b) Use the basis
$$
X^2,Y^2,Z^2,XY,XZ,YZ.
$$
The identity fixes all six basis vectors. A transposition fixes two of them, for example $(12)$ fixes $Z^2$ and $XY$. A $3$-cycle fixes none. Thus
$$
\chi=(6,2,0).
$$

(c) Taking inner products with the three irreducible characters gives multiplicities
$$
2,0,2,
$$
respectively. Therefore
$$
\chi=2\chi_{\mathrm{triv}}+2\chi_{\mathrm{std}}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $A$ be an $8\times8$ complex matrix such that
$$
\dim\ker(A-2I)=2,
\qquad \dim\ker(A-2I)^2=3,
$$
and
$$
\dim\ker(A-3I)=2,
\quad \dim\ker(A-3I)^2=4,
\quad \dim\ker(A-3I)^3=5.
$$
Find its characteristic polynomial, minimal polynomial, Jordan form, and rational canonical form.

#proof
For eigenvalue $2$, the first kernel dimension shows there are two Jordan blocks, and the second shows their sizes are $2$ and $1$. For eigenvalue $3$, there are two blocks; the successive dimensions $2,4,5$ force sizes $3$ and $2$. These blocks have total size $3+5=8$, so there are no other eigenvalues.

Thus
$$
\chi_A(x)=(x-2)^3(x-3)^5,
$$
and
$$
m_A(x)=(x-2)^2(x-3)^3.
$$
The Jordan form is
$$
J_2(2)\oplus[2]\oplus J_3(3)\oplus J_2(3).
$$

Pairing elementary divisors from smallest to largest gives the invariant factors
$$
d_1=(x-2)(x-3)^2,
\qquad
d_2=(x-2)^2(x-3)^3.
$$
Therefore the rational canonical form is
$$
C(d_1)\oplus C(d_2),
$$
where $C(d)$ denotes the companion matrix of $d$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Determine whether each statement is true or false.

(a) If every finitely generated subgroup of $G$ is abelian, then $G$ is abelian.

(b) If all proper subgroups of $G$ are normal, then $G$ is abelian.

(c) If $\gcd(m,n)=1$, then
$$
(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)=0.
$$

(d) If two $4\times4$ complex matrices have the same minimal and characteristic polynomials, then they are similar.

(e) If $x$ lies in every maximal ideal of a commutative ring $R$, then $1+x$ is a unit.

#proof
(a) True. Any two elements lie in the finitely generated subgroup they generate, which is abelian, so they commute.

(b) False. Every subgroup of the quaternion group $Q_8$ is normal, but $Q_8$ is nonabelian.

(c) True. Bezout's identity applied to $m,n$ annihilates every pure tensor.

(d) False. The nilpotent matrices with Jordan block partitions $(2,2)$ and $(2,1,1)$ both have characteristic polynomial $x^4$ and minimal polynomial $x^2$, but are not similar.

(e) True. If $1+x$ were a nonunit, it would lie in a maximal ideal $M$. Since $x\in M$, this would imply $1=(1+x)-x\in M$, a contradiction.
::
