::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $G$ be a group of order $42$.

(a) Prove that $G$ has a subgroup $H$ of order $6$ and that any two such subgroups are conjugate in $G$.

(b) Deduce that $G=H\ltimes N$, where $N$ is a normal subgroup of order $7$.

#proof
By Sylow's theorems, the number $n_7$ of Sylow $7$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_7\equiv1\pmod7,\qquad n_7\mid6.
$$
Hence $n_7=1$. Let $N$ be the unique Sylow $7$-subgroup; then $N\trianglelefteq G$.

To produce a subgroup of order $6$, let $Q$ be a Sylow $2$-subgroup and let $Q$ act by conjugation on the set of Sylow $3$-subgroups. Their number is either $1$ or $7$. In either case it is odd, so the involution group $Q$ has a fixed point. Thus $Q$ normalizes some Sylow $3$-subgroup $P$. Therefore
$$
H=PQ
$$
is a subgroup and $|H|=6$.

Since $|H|$ and $|N|$ are relatively prime, $H\cap N=1$. Also $|HN|=42$, so $HN=G$. Hence
$$
G=H\ltimes N
$$
(equivalently, $G=N\rtimes H$, depending on the semidirect-product convention).

Finally, $N$ is a normal Hall subgroup of $G$. The Schur--Zassenhaus theorem says that complements to a normal Hall subgroup exist and are all conjugate. Every subgroup of order $6$ is a complement to $N$, so any two such subgroups are conjugate in $G$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $p$ be prime. Show that for any Sylow $p$-subgroup $H\subseteq GL_n(\mathbb F_p)$, there is a basis of $V=\mathbb F_p^n$ in which every element of $H$ is upper triangular with all diagonal entries equal to $1$.

#proof
We prove the stronger statement for every finite $p$-group acting linearly on a nonzero finite-dimensional vector space over $\mathbb F_p$.

Let $H$ act on the underlying finite set of $V$. Every nontrivial orbit has size divisible by $p$, so
$$
|V|\equiv |V^H|\pmod p,
$$
where $V^H$ is the common fixed subspace. Since $|V|$ is divisible by $p$ and $0\in V^H$, the number $|V^H|$ is divisible by $p$; hence $V^H$ contains a nonzero vector $v_1$.

The induced action on $V/\langle v_1\rangle$ is again an action of a $p$-group. Induction on $\dim V$ produces an $H$-stable complete flag
$$
0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V,
\qquad \dim V_i=i,
$$
such that $H$ acts trivially on each quotient $V_i/V_{i-1}$. Choose a basis $v_1,\ldots,v_n$ adapted to this flag. Every $h\in H$ then has an upper-triangular matrix, and triviality on each one-dimensional quotient makes every diagonal entry $1$.

A Sylow $p$-subgroup of $GL_n(\mathbb F_p)$ is a finite $p$-group, so the conclusion applies to the given $H$.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
For a group $G$, define $G_0=G$ and $G_{n+1}=[G,G_n]$. A group is nilpotent if $G_N=1$ for some $N$. Prove that every finite $p$-group is nilpotent.

#proof
We use induction on $|G|$. The assertion is clear for the trivial group. If $G$ is a nontrivial finite $p$-group, then its center $Z(G)$ is nontrivial. The quotient $G/Z(G)$ is a smaller $p$-group and is nilpotent by induction. Therefore, for some $c$,
$$
(G/Z(G))_c=1.
$$
The lower central series is compatible with quotients, so this means
$$
G_c\subseteq Z(G).
$$
Consequently,
$$
G_{c+1}=[G,G_c]\subseteq[G,Z(G)]=1.
$$
Thus the lower central series of $G$ terminates, and $G$ is nilpotent.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $R\subset\mathbb Q$ be the subring consisting of fractions $a/b$ with $a\in\mathbb Z$ and $b=2^k3^\ell$, where $k,\ell\ge0$. Describe the ideals of $R$. Is $R$ a PID?

#proof
The ring is the localization
$$
R=\mathbb Z[1/6]=S^{-1}\mathbb Z,
\qquad S=\{2^k3^\ell:k,\ell\ge0\}.
$$
Ideals of a localization correspond to ideals of $\mathbb Z$ disjoint from $S$, after saturation. Since every ideal of $\mathbb Z$ is principal, every nonzero ideal of $R$ is principal.

More explicitly, if an integer $m$ is written
$$
m=2^a3^bn,
\qquad \gcd(n,6)=1,
$$
then $2$ and $3$ are units in $R$, so $(m)R=(n)R$. Hence the ideals of $R$ are
$$
(0)\quad\text{and}\quad nR,
$$
where $n$ ranges over positive integers relatively prime to $6$. This description is unique: if $m,n>0$ are both relatively prime to $6$ and $mR=nR$, then $m/n$ is a unit of $R$, forcing $m=n$.

Therefore $R$ is a PID.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $S=\{a+bi:a,b\in\mathbb Z\}\subset\mathbb C$ be the ring of Gaussian integers.

(a) Show that $S$ is a Euclidean domain.

(b) Factor $11$ into irreducibles in $S$.

(c) Factor $13$ into irreducibles in $S$.

#proof
(a) Use the norm
$$
N(a+bi)=a^2+b^2.
$$
For $\alpha,\beta\in\mathbb Z[i]$ with $\beta\ne0$, choose a Gaussian integer $q$ by rounding the real and imaginary parts of $\alpha/\beta$ to nearest integers. Then
$$
\left|\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-q\right|^2\le\frac12<1.
$$
With $r=\alpha-q\beta$, this gives
$$
N(r)=N(\beta)\left|\frac{\alpha}{\beta}-q\right|^2<N(\beta).
$$
Thus $\mathbb Z[i]$ is Euclidean.

(b) A rational prime congruent to $3$ modulo $4$ remains prime in $\mathbb Z[i]$. Since $11\equiv3\pmod4$, the element $11$ is already irreducible. Thus, up to multiplication by a unit, its irreducible factorization is simply
$$
11.
$$

(c) Since $13=3^2+2^2$,
$$
13=(3+2i)(3-2i).
$$
Each factor has norm $13$, a rational prime, so both factors are irreducible.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $V$ be a nonzero finite-dimensional complex vector space.

(a) If $S$ and $T$ are commuting linear operators on $V$, prove that every eigenspace of $S$ is mapped into itself by $T$.

(b) Let $A_1,\ldots,A_k$ be pairwise commuting linear operators on $V$. Prove that they have a common eigenvector.

(c) If $\dim V=n$, show that there is a chain
$$
0=V_0\subset V_1\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V,
$$
where $\dim V_j=j$ and every $V_j$ is invariant under every $A_i$.

#proof
(a) If $v$ lies in the $\lambda$-eigenspace of $S$, then
$$
S(Tv)=T(Sv)=T(\lambda v)=\lambda Tv.
$$
Thus $Tv$ lies in the same eigenspace.

(b) Choose an eigenvalue $\lambda_1$ of $A_1$ and its nonzero eigenspace $W_1$. By part (a), $W_1$ is invariant under all the other operators. Restrict $A_2$ to $W_1$ and choose a nonzero eigenspace $W_2\subseteq W_1$. Continue. After finitely many steps, every nonzero vector in $W_k$ is an eigenvector for all $A_i$.

(c) By part (b), choose a common eigenvector $v_1$ and set $V_1=\mathbb Cv_1$. The commuting operators induce commuting operators on $V/V_1$. By induction on $n$, the quotient has a complete common invariant flag. Taking inverse images of that flag under the quotient map produces
$$
V_1\subset V_2\subset\cdots\subset V_n=V
$$
with the required dimensions and invariance. Together with $V_0=0$, this is the desired chain.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ complex matrix such that every eigenvalue $\lambda$ of $A$ satisfies $\operatorname{Im}\lambda>0$. Consider
$$
B=
\begin{bmatrix}
A&0\\
0&\overline A
\end{bmatrix}.
$$
Find the invariant factors of $B$ in terms of those of $A$, and prove that $B$ is similar to a real matrix.

#proof
Let the nonconstant invariant factors of $A$ over $\mathbb C$ be
$$
f_1\mid f_2\mid\cdots\mid f_r.
$$
The invariant factors of $\overline A$ are
$$
\overline{f_1}\mid\overline{f_2}\mid\cdots\mid\overline{f_r},
$$
where coefficients are conjugated. Every root of every $f_i$ lies in the open upper half-plane, while every root of every $\overline{f_j}$ lies in the open lower half-plane. Hence
$$
\gcd(f_i,\overline{f_j})=1
$$
for all $i,j$.

The $\mathbb C[x]$-module associated with $B$ is the direct sum of those associated with $A$ and $\overline A$. By the Chinese remainder theorem,
$$
\mathbb C[x]/(f_i)\oplus\mathbb C[x]/(\overline{f_i})
\cong
\mathbb C[x]/(f_i\overline{f_i}).
$$
Therefore the invariant factors of $B$ are
$$
f_1\overline{f_1}\mid f_2\overline{f_2}\mid\cdots\mid f_r\overline{f_r}.
$$
Each polynomial $f_i\overline{f_i}$ has real coefficients. The rational canonical matrix whose companion blocks correspond to these invariant factors is therefore a real matrix. Since two complex matrices with the same invariant factors are similar over $\mathbb C$, $B$ is similar to that real matrix.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Find the Galois group of $x^6-2$ over $\mathbb Q$ and over $\mathbb F_5$.

#proof
Over $\mathbb Q$, let $a=2^{1/6}$ and let $\zeta_6$ be a primitive sixth root of unity. The polynomial is Eisenstein at $2$, so
$$
[\mathbb Q(a):\mathbb Q]=6.
$$
Its splitting field is
$$
K=\mathbb Q(a,\zeta_6).
$$
The field $\mathbb Q(a)$ is real but $\zeta_6$ is not, so $[K:\mathbb Q(a)]=2$ and $[K:\mathbb Q]=12$. Define
$$
r(a)=\zeta_6a,\quad r(\zeta_6)=\zeta_6,
$$
and let $s$ be complex conjugation. Then $r$ has order $6$, $s$ has order $2$, and
$$
srs=r^{-1}.
$$
These automorphisms account for all $12$ elements, so
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)\cong C_6\rtimes C_2\cong D_{12},
$$
where the nontrivial element of $C_2$ acts by inversion.

Over $\mathbb F_5$, the polynomial is separable because its derivative is
$$
6x^5=x^5,
$$
which has no common root with $x^6-2$. The group $\mathbb F_{25}^*$ is cyclic of order $24$. If $g$ is a generator, then $2$ is one of $g^6$ or $g^{18}$ because it has order $4$ in $\mathbb F_5^*$. In either case the equation $x^6=2$ has a solution in $\mathbb F_{25}$. Moreover, all sixth roots of unity lie in $\mathbb F_{25}$ because $6\mid24$. Hence $x^6-2$ splits in $\mathbb F_{25}$.

It does not have a root in $\mathbb F_5$, since for $x\ne0$ one has $x^6=x^2$, and $2$ is not a square modulo $5$. Thus its splitting field is exactly $\mathbb F_{25}$, and
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(\mathbb F_{25}/\mathbb F_5)\cong C_2,
$$
generated by the Frobenius automorphism $z\mapsto z^5$.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $K/F$ be a finite Galois extension with no proper intermediate fields. Prove that $[K:F]$ is prime.

#proof
Let
$$
G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/F).
$$
By the fundamental theorem of Galois theory, intermediate fields correspond bijectively to subgroups of $G$. Therefore $G$ has no subgroup other than $1$ and $G$.

Choose $1\ne g\in G$. The subgroup $\langle g\rangle$ is nontrivial, so it must equal $G$; hence $G$ is cyclic. If $|G|$ were composite, Cauchy's theorem (or the subgroup structure of a cyclic group) would give a nontrivial proper subgroup of $G$, a contradiction. Thus $|G|$ is prime. Finally,
$$
[K:F]=|G|,
$$
so $[K:F]$ is prime.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Let $Q=\{\pm1,\pm i,\pm j,\pm k\}$ be the quaternion group, with
$$
i^2=j^2=k^2=ijk=-1.
$$

(a) Classify the conjugacy classes of $Q$.

(b) Construct the character table of $Q$.

#proof
(a) The elements $1$ and $-1$ are central, so each forms its own conjugacy class. Direct calculation shows that each noncentral element is conjugate to its negative and to no other elements. Thus the conjugacy classes are
$$
\{1\},\quad\{-1\},\quad\{\pm i\},\quad\{\pm j\},\quad\{\pm k\}.
$$

(b) Since
$$
Q/\{\pm1\}\cong C_2\times C_2,
$$
there are four one-dimensional characters. The remaining irreducible character has degree $2$ because
$$
8-4\cdot1^2=4.
$$
It is afforded, for example, by the usual faithful two-dimensional complex representation of the quaternion group. With columns ordered by the classes above, the table is
$$
\begin{array}{c|rrrrr}
&1&-1&\{\pm i\}&\{\pm j\}&\{\pm k\}\\ \hline
\chi_1&1&1&1&1&1\\
\chi_2&1&1&1&-1&-1\\
\chi_3&1&1&-1&1&-1\\
\chi_4&1&1&-1&-1&1\\
\chi_5&2&-2&0&0&0
\end{array}
$$
The rows are pairwise orthonormal under the character inner product, confirming that this is the complete irreducible character table.
