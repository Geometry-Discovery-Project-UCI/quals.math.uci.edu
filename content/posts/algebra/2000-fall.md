#problem
Let $S_3$ act on $\{1,2,3\}$ and hence on $V=\mathbb C^3$.

(a) Describe the resulting faithful homomorphism $S_3\to GL_3(\mathbb C)$.

(b) Is $V$ irreducible under the definition in the problem that the only fixed vector must be zero?

(c) Find invariant subspaces $V_1,V_2$ with $V=V_1\oplus V_2$.

(d) Give matrices for the actions on $V_1$ and $V_2$.

#proof
(a) Let
$$
\rho(\sigma)e_i=e_{\sigma(i)}.
$$
This gives permutation matrices. The kernel is trivial because a permutation fixing every basis vector is the identity.

(b) No. The nonzero vector $(1,1,1)$ is fixed by every permutation.

(c) Take
$$
V_1=\operatorname{span}\{(1,1,1)\},
$$
and
$$
V_2=\{(z_1,z_2,z_3):z_1+z_2+z_3=0\}.
$$
Both are invariant and $V=V_1\oplus V_2$.

(d) The action on $V_1$ is the trivial $1\times1$ matrix $[1]$. For $V_2$, use the basis
$$
u=e_1-e_2,
\qquad
v=e_2-e_3.
$$
For the generators $s=(12)$ and $r=(123)$, the matrices are
$$
[\rho(s)]_{(u,v)}=
\begin{pmatrix}-1&1\\0&1\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
[\rho(r)]_{(u,v)}=
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}.
$$
These determine the entire representation.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $G=Q_8$ be the quaternion group.

(a) Explain why every subgroup of $G$ is normal.

(b) Count its conjugacy classes.

(c) If $N\triangleleft K$ and $K\triangleleft L$, must $N\triangleleft L$?

#proof
(a) The proper nontrivial subgroups are $\{\pm1\}$ and the three cyclic subgroups $\langle i\rangle$, $\langle j\rangle$, $\langle k\rangle$. The latter have index $2$ and hence are normal, while $\{\pm1\}$ is central. Thus every subgroup is normal.

(b) The five conjugacy classes are
$$
\{1\},\quad\{-1\},\quad\{i,-i\},\quad\{j,-j\},\quad\{k,-k\}.
$$

(c) False. In $L=S_4$, let
$$
K=V_4=\{1,(12)(34),(13)(24),(14)(23)\}
$$
and $N=\{1,(12)(34)\}$. Then $K\triangleleft S_4$ and $N\triangleleft K$ because $K$ is abelian, but $N$ is not normal in $S_4$ because conjugation permutes the three double transpositions.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $p$ be prime.

(a) Show that
$$
x^4+15x^3+20x^2+10x+45
$$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

(b) Show that
$$
x^{p-1}+x^{p-2}+\cdots+x+1
$$
is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

#proof
(a) Every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $5$, and the constant term $45$ is not divisible by $25$. Eisenstein's criterion at $5$ proves irreducibility.

(b) This polynomial is $\Phi_p(x)=(x^p-1)/(x-1)$. Translate by $1$:
$$
\Phi_p(x+1)=\frac{(x+1)^p-1}{x}
=x^{p-1}+\binom p1x^{p-2}+\cdots+\binom p{p-1}.
$$
Every nonleading coefficient is divisible by $p$, while the constant term is $p$, not divisible by $p^2$. Thus the translated polynomial is Eisenstein at $p$, and translation preserves irreducibility.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $\mathbb F_p$ be the field with $p$ elements.

(a) Prove that $\mathbb F_p^*$ is cyclic.

(b) Deduce Wilson's theorem: $(p-1)!\equiv-1\pmod p$.

#proof
(a) Let $m$ be the exponent of the finite abelian group $\mathbb F_p^*$. Every element is a root of $x^m-1$, so $p-1\le m$. But $m$ divides $p-1$, hence $m=p-1$. A finite abelian group contains an element whose order is its exponent, so $\mathbb F_p^*$ contains an element of order $p-1$ and is cyclic.

(b) In the product of all nonzero residues, pair each element with its inverse. Every pair contributes $1$. The only self-inverse elements solve $x^2=1$, hence are $1$ and $-1$. Therefore
$$
(p-1)!\equiv1\cdot(-1)\equiv-1\pmod p.
$$
The statement is also immediate for $p=2$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Using the surjection $\rho:SU(2)\to SO(3,\mathbb R)$ with kernel $\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$, prove that $SO(3,\mathbb R)$ is simple.

#proof
The group $SO(3,\mathbb R)$ consists of real orthogonal $3\times3$ matrices of determinant $1$, while
$$
SU(2)=\{U\in M_2(\mathbb C):U^*U=I,\ \det U=1\}.
$$
Every element of $SU(2)$ is conjugate to exactly one matrix
$$
d_\theta=\operatorname{diag}(e^{i\theta},e^{-i\theta}),
\qquad0\le\theta\le\pi,
$$
with conjugacy class determined by its trace $2\cos\theta$.

If $H\triangleleft SU(2)$, it is a union of conjugacy classes. Moreover, for $h\in H$ and $a\in SU(2)$,
$$
[h,a]=hah^{-1}a^{-1}\in H.
$$
If $H$ contains a noncentral element $d_\theta$, varying $a$ shows that these commutators contain rotations through all sufficiently small angles; conjugacy supplies all axes. Their products contain a neighborhood of the identity. Thus $H$ is open, and connectedness of $SU(2)$ forces $H=SU(2)$. Therefore the only nontrivial proper normal subgroup is its center
$$
Z(SU(2))=\{\pm I\}=\ker\rho.
$$

The inverse image of a normal subgroup of $SO(3)$ is a normal subgroup of $SU(2)$ containing $\ker\rho$. The only possibilities are the kernel and all of $SU(2)$, whose images are trivial and all of $SO(3)$. Hence $SO(3,\mathbb R)$ is simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
(a) Determine the direct-sum structure of the abelian group generated by $x,y,z$ with relations
$$
7x+5y+2z=0,
$$
$$
10x+8y+2z=0,
$$
$$
13x+11y+2z=0.
$$

(b) Describe all abelian groups of order $72$.

#proof
(a) The relation matrix is
$$
R=\begin{pmatrix}7&5&2\\10&8&2\\13&11&2\end{pmatrix}.
$$
It has rank $2$. The gcd of all entries is $1$, and the gcd of its $2\times2$ minors is $6$. Therefore its Smith normal form is
$$
\operatorname{diag}(1,6,0).
$$
Consequently,
$$
A\cong\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z.
$$

(b) Since $72=2^3\cdot3^2$, the $2$-primary part is one of
$$
C_8,qquad C_4\times C_2,qquad C_2^3,
$$
and the $3$-primary part is one of
$$
C_9,qquad C_3^2.
$$
All six groups are obtained by taking one direct product from each list, and these six are pairwise nonisomorphic.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let a finite group $G$ act on a finite set $S$. Put
$$
S^g=\{s\in S:g(s)=s\},
\qquad
G_s=\{g\in G:g(s)=s\}.
$$

(a) Prove
$$
\sum_{s\in S}|G_s|=\sum_{g\in G}|S^g|.
$$

(b) Prove Burnside's formula
$$
|G|\,|S/G|=\sum_{g\in G}|S^g|.
$$

#proof
(a) Count the set
$$
\Omega=\{(g,s)\in G\times S:g(s)=s\}
$$
in two ways. Fixing $s$ first gives the left sum, while fixing $g$ first gives the right sum.

(b) Partition $S$ into orbits. For an orbit $O$ and any $s\in O$, orbit-stabilizer gives
$$
|G_s|=\frac{|G|}{|O|}.
$$
Thus
$$
\sum_{s\in O}|G_s|=|O|\frac{|G|}{|O|}=|G|.
$$
Summing over the orbits and applying part (a) proves the formula.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $\alpha$ be a root of a monic irreducible polynomial $f\in\mathbb Q[x]$ of degree $9$, and let $K=\mathbb Q(\alpha)$.

(a) If $\alpha$ is real but no other root is real, explain why $K$ has no nontrivial automorphisms.

(b) If $\mathbb Q\subsetneq M\subsetneq K$, what is $[M:\mathbb Q]$?

(c) If the Galois closure $L$ has Galois group $S_9$, prove that no such intermediate $M$ exists.

#proof
(a) The field $K\subset\mathbb R$. Every $\mathbb Q$-automorphism of $K$ sends $\alpha$ to another root of its minimal polynomial lying in $K$, hence to a real root. Since $\alpha$ is the unique real root, it must be fixed, and therefore the automorphism is the identity.

(b) The tower formula gives
$$
9=[K:M][M:\mathbb Q].
$$
For a proper nontrivial intermediate field, both factors exceed $1$, so both equal $3$. Hence $[M:\mathbb Q]=3$.

(c) In $S_9$, the subgroup fixing the root $\alpha$ is a point stabilizer isomorphic to $S_8$, and its fixed field is $K$. Intermediate fields between $\mathbb Q$ and $K$ correspond to subgroups between $S_8$ and $S_9$. A point stabilizer is a maximal subgroup of $S_9$, so no proper intermediate subgroup, and hence no proper intermediate field, exists.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Describe all irreducible complex representations of $A_4$.

(a) Find its class equation.

(b) Determine the irreducible representations.

(c) Tensor the three-dimensional irreducible representation with each linear representation. Do new irreducible representations result?

#proof
(a) The conjugacy classes have sizes
$$
1,qquad3,qquad4,qquad4.
$$
They are the identity, the three double transpositions, and two separate classes of four $3$-cycles. Thus
$$
12=1+3+4+4.
$$

(b) Since $A_4/[A_4,A_4]\cong C_3$, there are three one-dimensional representations. There are four conjugacy classes, so one further irreducible representation remains. The degree-sum formula gives
$$
12=1^2+1^2+1^2+d^2,
$$
so $d=3$. This is the standard rotation representation of the tetrahedron. Its character on the four classes is
$$
(3,-1,0,0).
$$

(c) Tensoring multiplies character values. Every linear character is $1$ on the double transpositions and takes cube roots of unity on the two classes of $3$-cycles. Since the three-dimensional character vanishes on both $3$-cycle classes, the product character remains
$$
(3,-1,0,0).
$$
Thus every tensor product is isomorphic to the same three-dimensional representation; no new representation appears.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Compute the ideal class group of $K=\mathbb Q(\sqrt{-13})$.

#proof
Since $-13\equiv3\pmod4$, the ring of integers is
$$
\mathcal O_K=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-13}],
$$
with discriminant $D_K=-52$. The Minkowski bound for an imaginary quadratic field is
$$
\mu_K=\frac{2}{\pi}\sqrt{|D_K|}
=\frac{4\sqrt{13}}{\pi},
$$
which lies between $4$ and $5$. Therefore every ideal class has an integral ideal representative of norm at most $4$.

The relevant rational primes are $2$ and $3$. Since $2\mid D_K$, it ramifies:
$$
(2)=\mathfrak p_2^2,
\qquad
\mathfrak p_2=(2,1+\sqrt{-13}),
\qquad
N\mathfrak p_2=2.
$$
Modulo $3$, $x^2+13\equiv x^2+1$ is irreducible, so $3$ is inert and there is no ideal of norm $3$. Ideals of norm $4$ introduce no new class because they are products involving $\mathfrak p_2$ or are principal.

Thus the class group is generated by $[\mathfrak p_2]$, and
$$
[\mathfrak p_2]^2=[(2)]=1.
$$
It is nontrivial: if $\mathfrak p_2$ were principal, there would be an algebraic integer of norm $2$, requiring
$$
a^2+13b^2=2,
$$
which has no integer solution. Therefore
$$
\operatorname{Cl}(K)\cong C_2.
$$
