::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
(a) Show that
$$
|GL_2(\mathbb F_p)|=(p^2-1)(p^2-p).
$$

(b) Give an example of a Sylow $p$-subgroup of $GL_2(\mathbb F_p)$.

(c) How many Sylow $p$-subgroups does $GL_2(\mathbb F_p)$ have?

#proof
(a) The first column of an invertible matrix can be any nonzero vector, giving $p^2-1$ choices. The second column can be any vector outside the span of the first, giving $p^2-p$ choices. This proves the formula.

(b) The subgroup
$$
U=\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}1&a\\0&1\end{bmatrix}:a\in\mathbb F_p
\right\}
$$
has order $p$. Since the highest power of $p$ dividing
$$
(p^2-1)(p^2-p)=p(p-1)^2(p+1)
$$
is $p$, this is a Sylow $p$-subgroup.

(c) The normalizer of $U$ is the group of invertible upper-triangular matrices:
$$
N(U)=\left\{
\begin{bmatrix}a&b\\0&d\end{bmatrix}:a,d\ne0
\right\},
$$
which has order $p(p-1)^2$. Therefore the number of conjugates of $U$, and hence the number of Sylow $p$-subgroups, is
$$
[GL_2(\mathbb F_p):N(U)]
=\frac{p(p-1)^2(p+1)}{p(p-1)^2}=p+1.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
(a) Describe all automorphisms of the additive group
$$
\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z
$$
and count them.

(b) Describe and count all automorphisms of this ring.

#proof
(a) As an abelian group,
$$
C_2\oplus C_6\cong C_2\oplus C_2\oplus C_3.
$$
Every automorphism preserves the primary components. On the $2$-primary component it is an arbitrary element of $GL_2(\mathbb F_2)$, and on the $3$-primary component it is an arbitrary automorphism of $C_3$. Thus
$$
\operatorname{Aut}_{\mathrm{grp}}(C_2\oplus C_6)
\cong GL_2(\mathbb F_2)\times(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)^*,
$$
and the number of automorphisms is
$$
(2^2-1)(2^2-2)\cdot2=6\cdot2=12.
$$

(b) By the Chinese remainder theorem,
$$
\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z\oplus\mathbb Z/6\mathbb Z
\cong\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_2\times\mathbb F_3
$$
as rings. A ring automorphism may permute the two isomorphic $\mathbb F_2$ factors, while it must fix the unique $\mathbb F_3$ factor. Each prime field has only the identity automorphism. Hence the ring has exactly two automorphisms: the identity and the transposition of the two $\mathbb F_2$ factors.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Suppose $R$ is a commutative ring with identity and $x\in R$ lies in every maximal ideal. Prove that $1+x$ is a unit.

#proof
Suppose $1+x$ were not a unit. Then the proper ideal $(1+x)$ would be contained in some maximal ideal $M$. By hypothesis, $x\in M$ as well. Therefore
$$
1=(1+x)-x\in M,
$$
contradicting that $M$ is proper. Hence $1+x$ is a unit.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $M$ be an $n\times n$ matrix over a field $F$.

(a) If $\operatorname{char}F=0$, prove that $M$ is nilpotent if and only if
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(M^i)=0\qquad(1\le i\le n).
$$

(b) Give a counterexample in positive characteristic.

#proof
(a) If $M$ is nilpotent, all its eigenvalues in an algebraic closure are zero, and therefore every $M^i$ has trace zero.

Conversely, let $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_n$ be the eigenvalues in an algebraic closure, counted with multiplicity. The hypotheses say that the power sums
$$
p_i=\lambda_1^i+\cdots+\lambda_n^i
$$
vanish for $1\le i\le n$. Newton's identities express the elementary symmetric functions $e_k$ recursively by
$$
ke_k=\sum_{i=1}^k(-1)^{i-1}e_{k-i}p_i.
$$
Because the characteristic is zero, each $k$ is invertible in $F$. Inductively, $e_1=\cdots=e_n=0$. Hence the characteristic polynomial of $M$ is $x^n$, and Cayley--Hamilton gives $M^n=0$.

(b) Over $F=\mathbb F_p$, take the $p\times p$ identity matrix $M=I_p$. For every $1\le i\le p$,
$$
\operatorname{Tr}(M^i)=\operatorname{Tr}(I_p)=p=0
$$
in $\mathbb F_p$, but $M$ is not nilpotent.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
For a field $F$ and positive integer $n$, prove that
$$
\mu_n(F)=\{x\in F:x^n=1\}
$$
is cyclic.

#proof
The set $\mu_n(F)$ is a finite subgroup of $F^*$, of order at most $n$. We use the standard fact that every finite subgroup $G$ of the multiplicative group of a field is cyclic.

To prove the fact, let $e$ be the exponent of the finite abelian group $G$. Every element of $G$ is a root of $x^e-1$, so
$$
|G|\le e.
$$
On the other hand, $e\le|G|$, and the structure theorem for finite abelian groups supplies an element whose order is $e$. Therefore $e=|G|$, and that element generates $G$. Applying this to $G=\mu_n(F)$ proves the claim.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $f(x)=x^2+x+2\in\mathbb F_5[x]$.

(a) Prove that $f$ is irreducible.

(b) Explain why $f$ divides $x^{25}-x$ in $\mathbb F_5[x]$.

(c) How many irreducible quadratic polynomials are there in $\mathbb F_5[x]$?

#proof
(a) Direct substitution gives
$$
f(0)=2,quad f(1)=4,quad f(2)=3,quad f(3)=4,quad f(4)=2.
$$
Thus $f$ has no root in $\mathbb F_5$. A quadratic is reducible if and only if it has a root, so $f$ is irreducible.

(b) Every monic irreducible polynomial over $\mathbb F_5$ whose degree divides $2$ divides $x^{5^2}-x$. Since $f$ is irreducible of degree $2$, it follows that
$$
f(x)\mid x^{25}-x.
$$

(c) The $25$ elements of $\mathbb F_{25}$ consist of the five elements of $\mathbb F_5$ and $20$ elements of degree exactly $2$. Each monic irreducible quadratic contributes its two conjugate roots, so there are
$$
\frac{25-5}{2}=10
$$
monic irreducible quadratics. If scalar multiples are counted as distinct polynomials, each monic one has four nonzero scalar multiples, so there are $40$ irreducible quadratic polynomials in total.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $E$ be the splitting field of $x^{21}-1$ over $\mathbb Q$.

(a) Find $[E:\mathbb Q]$.

(b) How many subfields does $E$ have?

#proof
(a) The splitting field is the cyclotomic field
$$
E=\mathbb Q(\zeta_{21}).
$$
Therefore
$$
[E:\mathbb Q]=\varphi(21)=\varphi(3)\varphi(7)=2\cdot6=12.
$$

(b) Its Galois group is
$$
(\mathbb Z/21\mathbb Z)^*
\cong(\mathbb Z/3\mathbb Z)^*\times(\mathbb Z/7\mathbb Z)^*
\cong C_2\times C_6
\cong(C_2\times C_2)\times C_3.
$$
Every subgroup is the product of its $2$-primary and $3$-primary parts. The group $C_2\times C_2$ has five subgroups, and $C_3$ has two. Hence the Galois group has
$$
5\cdot2=10
$$
subgroups. By the Galois correspondence, $E$ has ten subfields, including $\mathbb Q$ and $E$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
For relatively prime positive integers $m,n$, show that
$$
(\mathbb Z/m\mathbb Z)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}(\mathbb Z/n\mathbb Z)=0.
$$

#proof
Choose integers $a,b$ such that $am+bn=1$. For every pure tensor $u\otimes v$,
$$
u\otimes v=(am+bn)(u\otimes v)
=a(mu)\otimes v+b,u\otimes(nv)=0.
$$
Since pure tensors generate the tensor product, the tensor product is zero.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Consider
$$
\mathbb R(T)\subset\mathbb R(T^{1/4}),
$$
where $T$ is an indeterminate.

(a) Is this extension Galois?

(b) Find all intermediate fields.

#proof
Let $t=T^{1/4}$. The polynomial $x^4-T$ is irreducible over $\mathbb R(T)$ by Eisenstein's criterion at $T$, so the extension has degree $4$.

(a) Its roots are $t,-t,it,-it$. The real field $\mathbb R(t)$ does not contain $it$, so the polynomial does not split there. Thus the extension is not normal and hence not Galois.

(b) Its Galois closure is $L=\mathbb C(t)$. Let
$$
r(t)=it,quad r(i)=i,
$$
and let $s$ be complex conjugation, so $s(t)=t$ and $s(i)=-i$. Then
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb R(T))
=\langle r,s\mid r^4=s^2=1,\ srs=r^{-1}\rangle
\cong D_8.
$$
Moreover, $\mathbb R(t)=L^{\langle s\rangle}$. Intermediate fields inside $\mathbb R(t)$ correspond to subgroups of $D_8$ containing $\langle s\rangle$. These are
$$
\langle s\rangle,
\qquad \langle s,r^2\rangle,
\qquad D_8.
$$
Their fixed fields are respectively
$$
\mathbb R(t),
\qquad\mathbb R(t^2),
\qquad\mathbb R(T).
$$
Thus the complete list is
$$
\mathbb R(T)\subset\mathbb R(T^{1/2})\subset\mathbb R(T^{1/4}).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
Let a finite group $G$ act on a finite set $S$, and let $\mathbb C[S]$ be the permutation representation with basis $S$. Let $\chi$ be its character.

(a) Show that $\chi(\sigma)$ is the number of points of $S$ fixed by $\sigma$.

(b) Show that $\langle\chi,1_G\rangle$ is the number of $G$-orbits in $S$.

#proof
(a) In the basis $S$, the matrix of $\sigma$ is a permutation matrix. Its diagonal entry corresponding to $s\in S$ is $1$ exactly when $\sigma s=s$, and is otherwise $0$. Therefore its trace is
$$
\chi(\sigma)=|\operatorname{Fix}_S(\sigma)|.
$$

(b) Since the trivial character is identically $1$,
$$
\langle\chi,1_G\rangle
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}\chi(\sigma)
=\frac1{|G|}\sum_{\sigma\in G}|\operatorname{Fix}_S(\sigma)|.
$$
By Burnside's orbit-counting lemma, this average is exactly the number of $G$-orbits in $S$.
::
