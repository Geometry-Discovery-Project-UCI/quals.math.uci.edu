::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
For each ring, list all maximal ideals.

(a) $\mathbb Z/90\mathbb Z$.

(b) $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^2+1)$.

(c) $\mathbb C[x]/(x^2+1)$.

(d) $\mathbb Q[x]/(x^3+x^2)$.

#proof
(a) Maximal ideals of $\mathbb Z/90\mathbb Z$ correspond to maximal ideals $(p)$ of $\mathbb Z$ containing $(90)$, so they are
$$
(2)/(90),\qquad(3)/(90),\qquad(5)/(90).
$$

(b) The polynomial $x^2+1$ is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$, so the quotient is a field. Its only maximal ideal is $(0)$.

(c) Since $x^2+1=(x-i)(x+i)$ over $\mathbb C$, the maximal ideals are
$$
(\overline{x-i})\qquad\text{and}\qquad(\overline{x+i}).
$$

(d) We have $x^3+x^2=x^2(x+1)$. The irreducible factors give the two maximal ideals
$$
(\overline x)qquad\text{and}\qquad(\overline{x+1}).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $M$ be a $9\times9$ complex matrix with characteristic polynomial
$$
(x^2+1)^3(x+1)^3
$$
and minimal polynomial
$$
(x^2+1)^2(x+1).
$$

(a) Find $\operatorname{tr}(M)$ and $\det(M)$.

(b) How many conjugacy classes of such matrices are there under $GL_9(\mathbb C)$?

(c) Write down a $9\times9$ rational matrix with the given characteristic and minimal polynomials.

#proof
Over $\mathbb C$, the eigenvalues $i,-i,-1$ each have algebraic multiplicity $3$. Hence
$$
\operatorname{tr}(M)=3i-3i-3=-3,
$$
and
$$
\det(M)=i^3(-i)^3(-1)^3=-1.
$$

For each of $i$ and $-i$, the largest Jordan block has size $2$, and the only partition of $3$ with largest part $2$ is $2+1$. For $-1$, the exponent in the minimal polynomial is $1$, so there are three size-one blocks. Thus there is exactly one complex conjugacy class.

A rational representative is
$$
C((x^2+1)^2)\oplus C(x^2+1)\oplus[-1]\oplus[-1]\oplus[-1],
$$
where $C(g)$ denotes the companion matrix of the monic polynomial $g$. The first two blocks have sizes $4$ and $2$, so the total size is $9$. Their characteristic polynomials multiply to the required polynomial, and their largest elementary divisors give the required minimal polynomial.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $G$ be a finite group of order $n>2$, and let $H<G$ have index $r>1$. Assume $r!<2n$. Prove that $G$ is not simple.

#proof
The action of $G$ on the left cosets $G/H$ gives a homomorphism
$$
\varphi:G\to S_r.
$$
Its kernel is normal. The action is nontrivial and transitive because $r>1$, so the kernel is not all of $G$. If $G$ were simple, the kernel would be trivial, and $G$ would embed in $S_r$. Thus $n$ would divide $r!$.

The inequalities $n\le r!<2n$ and divisibility $n\mid r!$ force $r!=n$. The embedding would then be an isomorphism $G\cong S_r$. Since $n>2$, we have $r\ge3$, but $S_r$ is not simple: $A_r$ is a nontrivial proper normal subgroup. This contradiction proves that $G$ is not simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $F$ be the splitting field of $x^{10}-1$ over $\mathbb Q$. Find $\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)$ both abstractly and as explicitly described automorphisms.

#proof
If $\zeta=\zeta_{10}=e^{2\pi i/10}$, then
$$
F=\mathbb Q(\zeta),
\qquad
[F:\mathbb Q]=\varphi(10)=4.
$$
Every automorphism is of the form
$$
\sigma_a(\zeta)=\zeta^a,
\qquad
a\in(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z)^\times=\{1,3,7,9\}.
$$
Composition corresponds to multiplication of the subscripts modulo $10$. The element $3$ has order $4$ modulo $10$, so
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(F/\mathbb Q)
\cong(\mathbb Z/10\mathbb Z)^\times
\cong C_4,
$$
generated by $\sigma_3$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $a_1,\ldots,a_n\in\mathbb Z$, where $n>1$, and suppose
$$
\gcd(a_1,\ldots,a_n)=1.
$$
Show that some $A\in SL_n(\mathbb Z)$ has first row $(a_1,\ldots,a_n)$.

#proof
The Euclidean algorithm, implemented by elementary integral column operations, reduces the unimodular row
$$
a=(a_1,\ldots,a_n)
$$
to $(1,0,\ldots,0)$. Thus there is a matrix $U\in GL_n(\mathbb Z)$ such that
$$
aU=(1,0,\ldots,0).
$$
Equivalently, the first row of $U^{-1}$ is $a$. Since $U$ is unimodular, $\det(U^{-1})=\pm1$. If it is $1$, take $A=U^{-1}$. If it is $-1$, multiply $U^{-1}$ on the left by
$$
\operatorname{diag}(1,-1,1,\ldots,1).
$$
This leaves the first row unchanged and changes the determinant to $1$. The resulting matrix is in $SL_n(\mathbb Z)$ and has the desired first row.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
(a) Prove that the additive groups $\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$, $\mathbb Z[i]$, and $\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2)$ are isomorphic.

(b) Prove that no two of these rings are isomorphic.

#proof
(a) Each additive group is free abelian of rank $2$. Explicit isomorphisms are
$$
(a,b)\longmapsto a+bi
$$
and
$$
(a,b)\longmapsto a+b\overline x.
$$

(b) The ring $\mathbb Z[i]$ is an integral domain. The other two are not: $(1,0)(0,1)=0$ in $\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$, and $\overline x\ne0$ but $\overline x^2=0$ in $\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2)$. Hence $\mathbb Z[i]$ is not isomorphic to either one. Finally, $\mathbb Z[x]/(x^2)$ has a nonzero nilpotent element, whereas $\mathbb Z\times\mathbb Z$ has no nonzero nilpotents. Therefore those two rings are also not isomorphic.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $\mathbb F_q$ be a finite field, let $K/\mathbb F_q$ be finite, and put $n=[K:\mathbb F_q]$.

(a) How many elements does $K$ have?

(b) Show that every extension of $\mathbb F_q$ is separable.

(c) Show that $K/\mathbb F_q$ is Galois.

(d) Exhibit an automorphism of $K$ of order $n$ fixing $\mathbb F_q$, and conclude that the Galois group is cyclic.

#proof
(a) As an $n$-dimensional vector space over a field of $q$ elements, $K$ has
$$
|K|=q^n.
$$

(b) Frobenius $a\mapsto a^p$ is injective and hence surjective on a finite field of characteristic $p$. Thus finite fields are perfect, and all their algebraic extensions are separable.

(c) Every element of $K$ is a root of $x^{q^n}-x$, and this polynomial has exactly the $q^n$ elements of $K$ as its roots. Its derivative is $-1$, so it is separable. Hence $K$ is the splitting field over $\mathbb F_q$ of a separable polynomial and is Galois.

(d) The Frobenius automorphism
$$
\sigma(a)=a^q
$$
fixes $\mathbb F_q$. We have $\sigma^n=1$. If $0<d<n$ and $\sigma^d=1$, all $q^n$ elements of $K$ would be roots of $x^{q^d}-x$, which has at most $q^d<q^n$ roots. Thus $\sigma$ has order $n$. Since $|\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb F_q)|=n$, it follows that
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb F_q)=\langle\sigma\rangle\cong C_n.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Suppose $f(x)\in\mathbb Q[x]$ is irreducible and $K$ is its splitting field.

(a) If $\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)=Q_8$, what are the possible degrees of $f$?

(b) If $\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)=D_8$, what are the possible degrees of $f$?

#proof
If $\alpha$ is a root and $G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/\mathbb Q)$, irreducibility makes the action of $G$ on the roots transitive, and
$$
\deg f=[G:G_\alpha].
$$
Because $K$ is the splitting field, the action is faithful, so the core of the stabilizer $G_\alpha$ is trivial.

(a) Every subgroup of $Q_8$ is normal. Therefore a core-free subgroup must be trivial. Hence $G_\alpha=1$ and
$$
\deg f=8.
$$

(b) In $D_8$, the core-free subgroups are the trivial subgroup and the order-$2$ reflection subgroups. They have indices $8$ and $4$, respectively. All other nontrivial subgroups contain a nontrivial normal subgroup in their core. Thus
$$
\deg f\in\{4,8\}.
$$
Both possibilities occur: a reflection fixed field has degree $4$, while a primitive element with trivial stabilizer has degree $8$.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Prove that there are no simple groups of order $132$.

#proof
Let $|G|=132=2^2\cdot3\cdot11$. The number of Sylow $11$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_{11}\equiv1\pmod{11},
\qquad
n_{11}\mid12,
$$
so $n_{11}=1$ or $12$. If it is $1$, $G$ is not simple.

Assume $n_{11}=12$. The distinct Sylow $11$-subgroups contribute
$$
12(11-1)=120
$$
nonidentity elements. If $G$ were simple, the number of Sylow $3$-subgroups would be greater than $1$. It is congruent to $1$ modulo $3$, so there are at least $4$, contributing at least $4(3-1)=8$ further nonidentity elements. Only three nonidentity elements remain. Every Sylow $2$-subgroup has order $4$ and therefore has exactly three nonidentity elements, so all Sylow $2$-subgroups must consist of the identity together with precisely those same three remaining elements. Hence the Sylow $2$-subgroup is unique and normal, a contradiction. Thus $G$ cannot be simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
(a) Find all positive integers that occur as the order of an element of $GL_2(\mathbb Q)$. Exhibit an element of order $3$.

(b) Find all positive integers that occur as the order of an element of $GL_2(\mathbb R)$. Exhibit an element of order $11$.

#proof
(a) If $A\in GL_2(\mathbb Q)$ has finite order, its minimal polynomial divides $x^m-1$ and is a product of cyclotomic polynomials of total degree at most $2$. A primitive $d$th root can occur only if
$$
\varphi(d)\le2,
$$
which gives $d=1,2,3,4,6$. A two-dimensional rational representation cannot combine a primitive root of degree $2$ with another eigenvalue, so no additional least common multiples occur. Hence the possible orders are exactly
$$
1,2,3,4,6.
$$
For example,
$$
\begin{pmatrix}0&-1\\1&-1\end{pmatrix}
$$
has characteristic polynomial $x^2+x+1$ and order $3$.

(b) Every positive integer occurs. For $m\ge3$, rotation through angle $2\pi/m$,
$$
R_m=
\begin{pmatrix}
\cos(2\pi/m)&-\sin(2\pi/m)\\
\sin(2\pi/m)&\cos(2\pi/m)
\end{pmatrix},
$$
has order $m$. The identity and $-I$ give orders $1$ and $2$. In particular, $R_{11}$ has order $11$.

::ProblemBlock{number=11}
#problem
Let $R=\mathbb Z[\sqrt{10}]$.

(a) Show that $13R$ is not a prime ideal.

(b) Show that $17R$ is a prime ideal.

#proof
For a rational prime $p$,
$$
R/pR\cong\mathbb F_p[x]/(x^2-10).
$$

(a) Modulo $13$, $10$ is a square because $6^2=36\equiv10\pmod{13}$. Thus $x^2-10$ is reducible, so the quotient is not an integral domain. Explicitly,
$$
(\sqrt{10}-6)(\sqrt{10}+6)=10-36=-26\in13R,
$$
but neither factor lies in $13R$. Hence $13R$ is not prime.

(b) The nonzero squares modulo $17$ are
$$
1,2,4,8,9,13,15,16,
$$
so $10$ is not a square modulo $17$. Hence $x^2-10$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_{17}$. The quotient $R/17R$ is therefore a field, and $17R$ is maximal, hence prime.
