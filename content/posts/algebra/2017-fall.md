#problem
Suppose $A\in M_3(\mathbb C)$ has eigenvalues $-1$ and $2$, and no others. Determine all possible pairs $(c_A(x),m_A(x))$ of characteristic and minimal polynomials, and give an example for each.

#proof
Both eigenvalues must occur. Thus the algebraic multiplicities are either $(2,1)$ or $(1,2)$. The possible pairs and examples are
$$
\begin{array}{c|c|c}
c_A(x)&m_A(x)&A\\ \hline
(x+1)^2(x-2)&(x+1)(x-2)&\operatorname{diag}(-1,-1,2)\\
(x+1)^2(x-2)&(x+1)^2(x-2)&J_2(-1)\oplus[2]\\
(x+1)(x-2)^2&(x+1)(x-2)&\operatorname{diag}(-1,2,2)\\
(x+1)(x-2)^2&(x+1)(x-2)^2&[-1]\oplus J_2(2)
\end{array}
$$
These exhaust the possibilities because the exponent of each factor in the minimal polynomial is the size of the largest Jordan block for that eigenvalue.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Prove that no group of order $150$ is simple.

#proof
Since $150=2\cdot3\cdot5^2$, the number of Sylow $5$-subgroups satisfies
$$
n_5\equiv1\pmod5,
\qquad n_5\mid6.
$$
Thus $n_5=1$ or $6$. If $n_5=1$, the Sylow $5$-subgroup is normal.

If $n_5=6$, conjugation on the six Sylow $5$-subgroups gives a nontrivial homomorphism
$$
G\longrightarrow S_6.
$$
Were $G$ simple, its kernel would be trivial, so $G$ would embed in $S_6$. Then $150$ would divide $|S_6|=720$ by Lagrange's theorem, which is false because $25\nmid720$. Therefore $G$ is not simple.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Suppose $G$ is abelian and $H_1,H_2\le G$. Decide whether
$$
G/H_1\cong G/H_2\quad\Longrightarrow\quad H_1\cong H_2.
$$

#proof
The statement is false. Take
$$
G=C_4\oplus C_2.
$$
Let
$$
H_1=C_4\oplus0
$$
and let
$$
H_2=\langle(2,0),(0,1)\rangle\cong C_2\oplus C_2.
$$
Both subgroups have index $2$, and hence
$$
G/H_1\cong C_2\cong G/H_2.
$$
But $H_1\cong C_4$ and $H_2\cong C_2\oplus C_2$ are not isomorphic.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Determine, up to isomorphism, all $\mathbb F_2[x]$-modules of order $4$.

#proof
Such a module is a two-dimensional $\mathbb F_2$-vector space, and multiplication by $x$ is an arbitrary linear operator. Module isomorphism is therefore the same as similarity of the corresponding $2\times2$ matrices over $\mathbb F_2$.

The rational canonical forms give exactly six possibilities:
$$
\begin{aligned}
&\mathbb F_2[x]/(x)\oplus\mathbb F_2[x]/(x),\\
&\mathbb F_2[x]/(x+1)\oplus\mathbb F_2[x]/(x+1),\\
&\mathbb F_2[x]/(x^2),\\
&\mathbb F_2[x]/((x+1)^2),\\
&\mathbb F_2[x]/(x(x+1)),\\
&\mathbb F_2[x]/(x^2+x+1).
\end{aligned}
$$
They correspond respectively to the zero operator, the identity, the two nontrivial Jordan blocks, an operator with distinct eigenvalues $0,1$, and the companion matrix of the unique irreducible quadratic over $\mathbb F_2$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Suppose $K$ has characteristic $0$, $L$ is the splitting field of an irreducible $f(x)\in K[x]$, and $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ is abelian. If $a\in L$ is a root of $f$, prove that $L=K(a)$.

#proof
Let $G=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ and let
$$
H=\operatorname{Gal}(L/K(a))=\{\sigma\in G:\sigma(a)=a\}.
$$
Because $f$ is irreducible in characteristic $0$, it is separable, and $G$ acts transitively on its roots. If $b=\tau(a)$ is another root and $\sigma\in H$, then, using commutativity of $G$,
$$
\sigma(b)=\sigma\tau(a)=\tau\sigma(a)=\tau(a)=b.
$$
Thus every element of $H$ fixes every root of $f$. Since those roots generate the splitting field $L$, every element of $H$ fixes all of $L$. Hence $H=1$, and the Galois correspondence gives $K(a)=L$.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
(a) For $f(x)=x^{31}-1\in\mathbb F_2[x]$, find the Galois group of its splitting field.

(b) Do the same over $\mathbb F_5$.

#proof
The splitting field over $\mathbb F_q$ is the smallest $\mathbb F_{q^m}$ containing all $31$st roots of unity, so $m$ is the multiplicative order of $q$ modulo $31$.

(a) Since
$$
2^5=32\equiv1\pmod{31}
$$
and no smaller positive power of $2$ is $1$ modulo $31$, the splitting field is $\mathbb F_{2^5}$. Therefore its Galois group is cyclic of order $5$, generated by $z\mapsto z^2$.

(b) Since
$$
5^3=125\equiv1\pmod{31},
$$
while $5\not\equiv1$ and $5^2\not\equiv1\pmod{31}$, the splitting field is $\mathbb F_{5^3}$. Its Galois group is cyclic of order $3$, generated by $z\mapsto z^5$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Which of the following ideals of $\mathbb Z[x,y]$ are prime, and which are maximal?
$$
(x,y),\quad(x,3y),\quad(x^2+1,y),\quad
(x^2+1,3,y),\quad(x^2+1,5,y).
$$

#proof
We examine the quotient rings.

For $(x,y)$,
$$
\mathbb Z[x,y]/(x,y)\cong\mathbb Z,
$$
which is a domain but not a field. Thus the ideal is prime but not maximal.

For $(x,3y)$,
$$
\mathbb Z[x,y]/(x,3y)\cong\mathbb Z[y]/(3y).
$$
The nonzero classes of $3$ and $y$ multiply to zero, so the ideal is neither prime nor maximal.

For $(x^2+1,y)$,
$$
\mathbb Z[x,y]/(x^2+1,y)\cong\mathbb Z[i],
$$
which is a domain but not a field. Thus the ideal is prime but not maximal.

For $(x^2+1,3,y)$, the quotient is
$$
\mathbb F_3[x]/(x^2+1).
$$
The polynomial $x^2+1$ has no root in $\mathbb F_3$, so it is irreducible. The quotient is a field; hence the ideal is maximal and prime.

For $(x^2+1,5,y)$, the quotient is
$$
\mathbb F_5[x]/(x^2+1).
$$
But $x^2+1=(x-2)(x+2)$ over $\mathbb F_5$, so the quotient is not a domain. The ideal is neither prime nor maximal.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
If $N$ is a finite normal subgroup of a group $G$, $d>0$, and $G/N$ has an element of order $d$, prove that $G$ has an element of order $d$.

#proof
Let $gN$ have order $d$. Then $g^d\in N$. Since $N$ is finite, $g^d$ has finite order, so $g$ has finite order, say $m$. The order of the image $gN$ divides the order of $g$, so $d\mid m$. In the cyclic group $\langle g\rangle$ of order $m$, the element
$$
g^{m/d}
$$
has order exactly $d$. Thus $G$ contains an element of order $d$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Determine whether each statement is true or false, with justification.

(a) Every commutative ring with identity having exactly $200$ elements has zero divisors.

(b) For every prime $p$, there is a nonzero ring homomorphism $\mathbb Z[i]\to\mathbb F_p$.

(c) The center of a nonabelian group $G$ is properly contained in some abelian subgroup.

(d) If $K$ is a subfield of $F$ and $F$ is isomorphic to $K$ as fields, then $F=K$.

(e) For every integral domain $R$ and every $R$-module $M$, the torsion elements form a submodule.

#proof
(a) True. A finite commutative ring with identity and no zero divisors is a field. A finite field has prime-power order, but
$$
200=2^3\cdot5^2
$$
is not a prime power.

(b) False. Any nonzero homomorphism to a field sends $1$ to $1$. The image of $i$ would have to satisfy $x^2=-1$. For $p=3$, no such element exists in $\mathbb F_3$.

(c) True. Choose $g\notin Z(G)$. Then
$$
\langle Z(G),g\rangle
$$
is abelian because every element of $Z(G)$ commutes with $g$, and it properly contains $Z(G)$.

(d) False. For example,
$$
\mathbb Q(t^2)\subsetneq\mathbb Q(t),
$$
but the fields are isomorphic by sending the transcendental element $t$ to $t^2$.

(e) True. If $rm=0$ and $sn=0$ for nonzero $r,s\in R$, then
$$
rs(m+n)=0.
$$
Since $R$ is a domain, $rs\ne0$, so $m+n$ is torsion. Also, if $m$ is torsion and $a\in R$, the same nonzero element annihilating $m$ annihilates $am$. Thus the torsion elements form a submodule.
::
