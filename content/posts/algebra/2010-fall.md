::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $K/F$ be an algebraic field extension and let $R$ be a ring with
$$
F\subseteq R\subseteq K.
$$
Prove that $R$ is a field.

#proof
Let $0\ne r\in R$. Since $r$ is algebraic over $F$, it satisfies a relation of least positive degree
$$
a_0+a_1r+\cdots+a_nr^n=0,
$$
with $a_i\in F$ and $a_0\ne0$; otherwise one could factor out $r$ and obtain a relation of smaller degree. Rearranging gives
$$
r^{-1}=-a_0^{-1}(a_1+a_2r+\cdots+a_nr^{n-1}).
$$
The right side lies in $R$ because $F\subseteq R$. Thus every nonzero element of $R$ is invertible in $R$, so $R$ is a field.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Show that a group of order $35^3$ is not simple.

#proof
Since
$$
35^3=5^3\cdot7^3,
$$
Burnside's $p^aq^b$ theorem implies that every group of this order is solvable. A nontrivial finite simple solvable group must be cyclic of prime order: its last nontrivial derived subgroup is a nontrivial abelian normal subgroup and hence must be the whole group. Since $35^3$ is composite, a group of this order cannot be simple.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Determine the splitting field of $x^5-2$ over $\mathbb F_3$, and describe its Galois group abstractly and explicitly.

#proof
In $\mathbb F_3$, $2=-1$, so the polynomial is $x^5+1$. The element $-1$ is one root; the other roots are obtained by multiplying it by fifth roots of unity. Thus the splitting field is the smallest finite extension containing all fifth roots of unity.

The multiplicative order of $3$ modulo $5$ is $4$, since
$$
3^2\equiv-1\pmod5,
\qquad 3^4\equiv1\pmod5.
$$
Therefore the splitting field is
$$
L=\mathbb F_{3^4}=\mathbb F_{81}.
$$
The polynomial is separable because its derivative is $5x^4=2x^4$, which has no common root with it.

Every finite-field Galois group is cyclic, so
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(L/\mathbb F_3)\cong C_4.
$$
Explicitly, if $\Phi(z)=z^3$ is Frobenius, the four automorphisms are
$$
1,\quad\Phi,\quad\Phi^2,\quad\Phi^3.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $\chi$ be the character of a $d$-dimensional complex representation $\rho$ of a finite group $G$. Prove that
$$
|\chi(g)|\le d
$$
for every $g\in G$, and that equality implies $\rho(g)=\zeta I$ for some root of unity $\zeta$.

#proof
Because $G$ is finite, the representation admits a $G$-invariant Hermitian inner product, so every $\rho(g)$ is unitary. Also $g$ has finite order, so $\rho(g)$ is diagonalizable with eigenvalues $\lambda_1,\ldots,\lambda_d$ that are roots of unity. Therefore
$$
|\chi(g)|=|\lambda_1+cdots+\lambda_d|le
|\lambda_1|+cdots+|\lambda_d|=d.
$$
Equality in the triangle inequality occurs only when all the unit complex numbers $\lambda_j$ have the same argument. Hence they are all equal to one root of unity $\zeta$. Since $\rho(g)$ is diagonalizable, this gives
$$
\rho(g)=\zeta I.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Find the Galois group over $\mathbb Q$ of
$$
x^3+4x+2.
$$

#proof
The rational-root test shows that the cubic has no rational root, so it is irreducible. For a cubic $x^3+ax+b$, the discriminant is
$$
\Delta=-4a^3-27b^2.
$$
Here
$$
\Delta=-4\cdot4^3-27\cdot2^2=-364=-4\cdot91,
$$
which is not a square in $\mathbb Q$. The Galois group of an irreducible cubic is $A_3$ exactly when its discriminant is a square, and is otherwise $S_3$. Therefore the Galois group is
$$
S_3.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $R=\mathbb R^n$. Prove that an ideal $I\subseteq R$ is maximal if and only if, for some coordinate $i$,
$$
I=\{(r_1,\ldots,r_n):r_i=0\}.
$$

#proof
Let $e_i$ be the standard coordinate idempotents. For any ideal $I$, define
$$
J_i=\{r\in\mathbb R:re_i\in I\}.
$$
Each $J_i$ is an ideal of the field $\mathbb R$, hence is either $0$ or $\mathbb R$, and
$$
I=J_1\times\cdots\times J_n.
$$
The quotient is the product of the coordinates for which $J_i=0$. It is a field exactly when precisely one $J_i$ is zero. In that case $I$ consists exactly of the tuples whose $i$th coordinate vanishes. Since an ideal is maximal exactly when its quotient is a field, the result follows.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Define ring, module, ring homomorphism, and module homomorphism.

#proof
(a) A ring is an abelian group under addition with an associative multiplication distributing over addition; under the convention used here it has an identity.

(b) An $R$-module is an abelian group $M$ with scalar multiplication by $R$ satisfying the distributive, associative, and identity axioms.

(c) A ring homomorphism preserves addition, multiplication, and the multiplicative identity.

(d) An $R$-module homomorphism $f:M\to N$ is additive and satisfies
$$
f(rm)=rf(m)
$$
for every $r\in R$ and $m\in M$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $A$ be a finite abelian group, let $S$ be its Sylow $p$-subgroup, and suppose $|S|=p^k$. Prove that
$$
(\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z)\otimes_{\mathbb Z}A\cong S.
$$

#proof
Write
$$
A=S\oplus T,
$$
where $T$ is the direct sum of the primary components for primes other than $p$. Since
$$
(\mathbb Z/p^k\mathbb Z)\otimes A\cong A/p^kA,
$$
it is enough to compute this quotient. The group $S$ is annihilated by $p^k$, because its order is $p^k$, so $S/p^kS=S$. Multiplication by $p^k$ is an automorphism of $T$, because $p$ is relatively prime to $|T|$, so $T/p^kT=0$. Therefore
$$
A/p^kA\cong S,
$$
as required.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Give an inseparable field extension and compute its separable and inseparable degrees.

#proof
Let
$$
F=\mathbb F_p(t),
\qquad K=F(t^{1/p}),
$$
where $t$ is an indeterminate. The polynomial
$$
x^p-t
$$
is irreducible over $F$ because $t$ is not a $p$th power in $F$. Its derivative is zero, and in an algebraic closure it has only one distinct root. Hence $K/F$ is purely inseparable of degree $p$. Therefore
$$
[K:F]_{\mathrm{sep}}=1,
\qquad
[K:F]_{\mathrm{insep}}=p.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
(a) How many similarity classes of rational matrices have characteristic polynomial
$$
(x^4-1)(x^2-1)?
$$

(b) Give one representative of each class.

(c) Give the minimal polynomial in each class.

#proof
Factor over $\mathbb Q$:
$$
(x^4-1)(x^2-1)=(x-1)^2(x+1)^2(x^2+1).
$$
For each of the linear factors $x-1$ and $x+1$, the multiplicity-$2$ primary part has either partition $(2)$ or $(1,1)$. The irreducible factor $x^2+1$ occurs once. Hence there are
$$
2\cdot2=4
$$
similarity classes.

Let $C(f)$ denote the companion matrix of a monic polynomial $f$. Representatives and minimal polynomials are:
$$
\begin{array}{c|c}
\text{representative}&\text{minimal polynomial}\\ \hline
C((x-1)^2)\oplus C((x+1)^2)\oplus C(x^2+1)
&(x-1)^2(x+1)^2(x^2+1)\\
C((x-1)^2)\oplus[-1]\oplus[-1]\oplus C(x^2+1)
&(x-1)^2(x+1)(x^2+1)\\
[1]\oplus[1]\oplus C((x+1)^2)\oplus C(x^2+1)
&(x-1)(x+1)^2(x^2+1)\\
[1]\oplus[1]\oplus[-1]\oplus[-1]\oplus C(x^2+1)
&(x-1)(x+1)(x^2+1)
\end{array}
$$
