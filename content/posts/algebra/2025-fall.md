#problem
Consider a finite group $G$ of order $p^n$, where $p$ is a prime and $n\in \mathbb Z^+$. Prove that every subgroup $H$ of $G$ of index $p$ is a normal subgroup of $G$.

Hint. Consider the action of $G$ on the collection of cosets of $H$ by left multiplication.

#proof
Let $G$ act on the set $G/H$ of left cosets of $H$ by left multiplication:
$$
g\cdot xH=(gx)H.
$$
Since $[G:H]=p$, this action gives a homomorphism
$$
\varphi:G\to S_p.
$$
Let
$$
N=\ker\varphi.
$$
Then $N$ is a normal subgroup of $G$, and $N\subset H$, because any element in the kernel fixes the coset $H$.

The image $\varphi(G)$ is a subgroup of $S_p$, so $|\varphi(G)|$ divides $p!$. Also
$$
|\varphi(G)|=[G:N],
$$
which is a power of $p$ because $G$ is a $p$-group. The largest power of $p$ dividing $p!$ is $p$. Hence
$$
|\varphi(G)|=1 \quad \text{or} \quad |\varphi(G)|=p.
$$

If $|\varphi(G)|=1$, then the action is trivial. In particular, for every $g\in G$,
$$
gH=H,
$$
so $g\in H$. Thus $H=G$, contradicting $[G:H]=p>1$. Therefore
$$
|\varphi(G)|=p.
$$
Hence
$$
[G:N]=p.
$$
Since $N\subset H\subset G$ and $[G:H]=p$, we must have $N=H$. Therefore $H=N$ is normal in $G$.

Thus every subgroup of index $p$ in a finite $p$-group is normal.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Consider a finite group $G$ and a normal subgroup $H$ of $G$. Let $a\in H$ be such that the conjugacy class $K$ of $a$ in $G$ is contained in $H$. Prove the following.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $K$ is the union of $k$ many conjugacy classes in $H$ of equal size.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** $k$ is the index of $H C_G(a)$ in $G$.

Remark. Recall that $C_G(a)$ is the centralizer of $a$ in $G$. Also, $H C_G(a)$ is the product of the two subgroups in $G$.

#proof
Let
$$
K=\{gag^{-1}:g\in G\}.
$$
The group $H$ acts on $K$ by conjugation. The orbits of this action are exactly the $H$-conjugacy classes contained in $K$. Therefore $K$ is a union of $H$-conjugacy classes.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** We show that all these $H$-conjugacy classes have the same size.

Let $b=gag^{-1}\in K$. The $H$-conjugacy class of $b$ has size
$$
[H:C_H(b)].
$$
Since $b=gag^{-1}$,
$$
C_H(b)=H\cap C_G(b)
      =H\cap gC_G(a)g^{-1}.
$$
Because $H$ is normal in $G$,
$$
H\cap gC_G(a)g^{-1}
=
g\bigl(H\cap C_G(a)\bigr)g^{-1}.
$$
Thus
$$
|C_H(b)|=|H\cap C_G(a)|=|C_H(a)|.
$$
Therefore every $H$-conjugacy class contained in $K$ has size
$$
[H:C_H(a)].
$$
Hence $K$ is the union of some number $k$ of $H$-conjugacy classes, all of equal size.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Since the $G$-conjugacy class of $a$ has size
$$
|K|=[G:C_G(a)]
$$
and each $H$-conjugacy class has size
$$
[H:C_H(a)]=[H:H\cap C_G(a)],
$$
we get
$$
k
=
\frac{[G:C_G(a)]}{[H:H\cap C_G(a)]}.
$$
Now
$$
|H C_G(a)|
=
\frac{|H|\,|C_G(a)|}{|H\cap C_G(a)|}.
$$
Therefore
$$
[G:H C_G(a)]
=
\frac{|G|}{|H C_G(a)|}
=
\frac{|G|\,|H\cap C_G(a)|}{|H|\,|C_G(a)|}
=
\frac{[G:C_G(a)]}{[H:H\cap C_G(a)]}.
$$
Hence
$$
k=[G:H C_G(a)].
$$
This proves both claims.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Denote $G=\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R)$, the group of invertible $2\times 2$ real matrices, and
$$
H=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)=\{A\in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R):\det A=1\}.
$$
Prove that $H$ is a normal subgroup of $G$ and that $G/H$ is an abelian group.

#proof
Consider the determinant homomorphism
$$
\det:\mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R)\to \mathbb R^\times.
$$
It is a group homomorphism because
$$
\det(AB)=\det(A)\det(B).
$$
Its kernel is
$$
\ker(\det)=\{A\in \mathrm{GL}_2(\mathbb R):\det A=1\}
=\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)=H.
$$
Since the kernel of a group homomorphism is normal, $H$ is normal in $G$.

By the First Isomorphism Theorem,
$$
G/H\cong \det(G).
$$
Since every nonzero real number occurs as the determinant of an invertible $2\times 2$ real matrix, for example
$$
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
r&0\\
0&1
\end{pmatrix}
=r,
$$
we have
$$
\det(G)=\mathbb R^\times.
$$
Therefore
$$
G/H\cong \mathbb R^\times.
$$
The group $\mathbb R^\times$ is abelian, so $G/H$ is abelian.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Assume $F$ is a field and $x,y$ are variables. Prove that the rings
$$
F[x,y]/(y^2-x)
$$
and
$$
F[x,y]/(y^2-x^2)
$$
are not isomorphic.

Remark. Recall that if $R$ is a ring and $r\in R$, then $(r)$ is the ideal of $R$ generated by $r$.

#proof
First consider
$$
R_1=F[x,y]/(y^2-x).
$$
Define a homomorphism
$$
\Phi:F[x,y]\to F[y]
$$
by
$$
\Phi(x)=y^2,\qquad \Phi(y)=y.
$$
Then $\Phi$ is surjective and
$$
\ker\Phi=(x-y^2)=(y^2-x).
$$
Hence
$$
R_1\cong F[y].
$$
Therefore $R_1$ is an integral domain.

Now consider
$$
R_2=F[x,y]/(y^2-x^2).
$$
We show that $R_2$ is not an integral domain.

If $\operatorname{char}F\neq 2$, then
$$
y^2-x^2=(y-x)(y+x).
$$
In $R_2$,
$$
\overline{y-x}\,\overline{y+x}=0.
$$
Neither $\overline{y-x}$ nor $\overline{y+x}$ is zero in $R_2$, because neither $y-x$ nor $y+x$ belongs to the principal ideal generated by $(y-x)(y+x)$. Thus $R_2$ has zero divisors.

If $\operatorname{char}F=2$, then
$$
y^2-x^2=(y-x)^2.
$$
In $R_2$,
$$
\overline{y-x}^{\,2}=0,
$$
but $\overline{y-x}\neq 0$. Thus $R_2$ has a nonzero nilpotent element, so it is not an integral domain.

Therefore $R_1$ is an integral domain while $R_2$ is not. Since being an integral domain is preserved by ring isomorphism, the two rings are not isomorphic.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
An element $x$ of a ring is nilpotent if $x^n=0$ for some integer $n\ge 1$. Let $R$ be a commutative ring, and let
$$
N=\{x\in R:x \text{ is nilpotent}\}.
$$
Prove that $N$ is an ideal of $R$ and that $R/N$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements.

#proof
First we prove that $N$ is an ideal.

Clearly $0\in N$. Let $x,y\in N$. Then there exist positive integers $m,n$ such that
$$
x^m=0,\qquad y^n=0.
$$
Since $R$ is commutative, the binomial theorem gives
$$
(x+y)^{m+n-1}
=
\sum_{j=0}^{m+n-1}\binom{m+n-1}{j}x^j y^{m+n-1-j}.
$$
In each term, either $j\ge m$ or $m+n-1-j\ge n$. Hence each term is zero. Therefore
$$
(x+y)^{m+n-1}=0.
$$
So $x+y\in N$.

Also, if $r\in R$ and $x\in N$, with $x^m=0$, then
$$
(rx)^m=r^m x^m=0.
$$
Thus $rx\in N$. Hence $N$ is an ideal of $R$.

Now we prove that $R/N$ has no nonzero nilpotent elements. Suppose that $r+N\in R/N$ is nilpotent. Then for some $m\ge 1$,
$$
(r+N)^m=N.
$$
Thus
$$
r^m\in N.
$$
Since $r^m$ is nilpotent, there exists $s\ge 1$ such that
$$
(r^m)^s=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
r^{ms}=0.
$$
So $r$ is nilpotent, meaning $r\in N$. Hence
$$
r+N=N.
$$
Thus the only nilpotent element of $R/N$ is zero.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Suppose that $E$ and $F$ are two fields whose characteristics satisfy
$$
\operatorname{char}(E)=p>0,\qquad \operatorname{char}(F)=q>0,
$$
and $p\neq q$. Prove that the abelian group
$$
E\otimes_{\mathbb Z}F
$$
is zero.

#proof
Since $\operatorname{char}(E)=p$, every element $e\in E$ satisfies
$$
p e=0.
$$
Since $\operatorname{char}(F)=q$, every element $f\in F$ satisfies
$$
q f=0.
$$
For a pure tensor $e\otimes f\in E\otimes_{\mathbb Z}F$, we have
$$
p(e\otimes f)=(pe)\otimes f=0
$$
and
$$
q(e\otimes f)=e\otimes(qf)=0.
$$
Because $p$ and $q$ are distinct primes, there exist integers $r,s$ such that
$$
rp+sq=1.
$$
Therefore
$$
e\otimes f
=
(rp+sq)(e\otimes f)
=
r\,p(e\otimes f)+s\,q(e\otimes f)
=
0.
$$
Every element of $E\otimes_{\mathbb Z}F$ is a finite sum of pure tensors, and each pure tensor is zero. Hence
$$
E\otimes_{\mathbb Z}F=0.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Consider an $n\times n$ matrix $A$ with entries in $\mathbb Q$ which satisfies
$$
(A-I)^2=A^3-A^2-I,
$$
where $I$ is the identity matrix. Prove the following.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $n$ is divisible by $3$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** If $B$ is another $n\times n$ matrix satisfying the same equation, then $A$ and $B$ are similar.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** For $n=3$, give examples of two such matrices with entries in $\mathbb Q$. Explain how you obtained these matrices.

Quote the relevant theorems from the theory and explain how you use them.

#proof
The equation
$$
(A-I)^2=A^3-A^2-I
$$
is equivalent to
$$
A^2-2A+I=A^3-A^2-I.
$$
Thus
$$
A^3-2A^2+2A-2I=0.
$$
Let
$$
q(t)=t^3-2t^2+2t-2.
$$
Then
$$
q(A)=0.
$$

By the rational root test, the only possible rational roots of $q(t)$ are
$$
\pm 1,\quad \pm 2.
$$
A direct check shows
$$
q(1)=-1,\qquad q(-1)=-7,\qquad q(2)=2,\qquad q(-2)=-22.
$$
So $q(t)$ has no rational root. Since $q(t)$ is cubic, it is irreducible over $\mathbb Q$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The minimal polynomial $m_A(t)$ divides $q(t)$. Since $q(t)$ is irreducible and $A$ is not annihilated by the constant polynomial $1$, we must have
$$
m_A(t)=q(t).
$$

We use the rational canonical form theorem: if a matrix over $\mathbb Q$ has minimal polynomial dividing $q(t)$, then its invariant factors divide $q(t)$. Since $q(t)$ is irreducible, every nonconstant invariant factor must equal $q(t)$. Therefore the characteristic polynomial of $A$ is
$$
\chi_A(t)=q(t)^r
$$
for some integer $r\ge 1$. Hence
$$
n=\deg \chi_A=3r.
$$
Thus $3$ divides $n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $B$ be another $n\times n$ rational matrix satisfying
$$
(B-I)^2=B^3-B^2-I.
$$
Then also
$$
q(B)=0.
$$
The same argument shows that the minimal polynomial of $B$ is $q(t)$, and all nonconstant invariant factors of $B$ are equal to $q(t)$.

Since $n=3r$, both $A$ and $B$ have rational canonical form equal to the direct sum of $r$ companion blocks for $q(t)$. Therefore $A$ and $B$ have the same rational canonical form. By uniqueness of rational canonical form, $A$ and $B$ are similar over $\mathbb Q$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** For
$$
q(t)=t^3-2t^2+2t-2,
$$
a companion matrix is
$$
C=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&2\\
1&0&-2\\
0&1&2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its characteristic polynomial is
$$
\chi_C(t)=q(t).
$$
By the Cayley-Hamilton theorem,
$$
q(C)=0.
$$
Therefore $C$ satisfies the required equation.

A second example is
$$
D=C^T=
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
2&-2&2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since
$$
q(D)=q(C^T)=q(C)^T=0,
$$
the matrix $D$ also satisfies the required equation.

Thus, for $n=3$, two examples are
$$
\begin{pmatrix}
0&0&2\\
1&0&-2\\
0&1&2
\end{pmatrix}
\quad\text{and}\quad
\begin{pmatrix}
0&1&0\\
0&0&1\\
2&-2&2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
They were obtained from the companion matrix of the irreducible polynomial $q(t)=t^3-2t^2+2t-2$ and its transpose.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $K/F$ be a Galois extension with Galois group
$$
G=\operatorname{Gal}(K/F).
$$
Consider a normal subgroup $H\trianglelefteq G$, and denote
$$
E=K^H.
$$
For any two automorphisms $\sigma,\varphi\in G$, prove that their restrictions to $E$ satisfy
$$
\sigma|_E=\varphi|_E
$$
if and only if $\sigma$ and $\varphi$ lie in the same left coset of $H$ in $G$.

#proof
By the Fundamental Theorem of Galois Theory,
$$
\operatorname{Gal}(K/E)=H,
$$
because
$$
E=K^H.
$$

Now suppose first that
$$
\sigma|_E=\varphi|_E.
$$
Then for every $x\in E$,
$$
\sigma(x)=\varphi(x).
$$
Thus
$$
\varphi^{-1}\sigma(x)=x
$$
for every $x\in E$. Therefore
$$
\varphi^{-1}\sigma\in \operatorname{Gal}(K/E)=H.
$$
Hence
$$
\sigma\in \varphi H.
$$
So $\sigma$ and $\varphi$ lie in the same left coset of $H$.

Conversely, suppose $\sigma$ and $\varphi$ lie in the same left coset of $H$. Then
$$
\sigma\in \varphi H,
$$
so there exists $h\in H$ such that
$$
\sigma=\varphi h.
$$
Every element of $H$ fixes every element of $E=K^H$. Therefore, for every $x\in E$,
$$
\sigma(x)=\varphi h(x)=\varphi(x).
$$
Hence
$$
\sigma|_E=\varphi|_E.
$$

Thus the restrictions of $\sigma$ and $\varphi$ to $E$ are equal if and only if $\sigma$ and $\varphi$ lie in the same left coset of $H$.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Find a polynomial $f(x)\in \mathbb F_3[x]$ of the form
$$
f(x)=x^3+ax^2+b
$$
such that $\mathbb F_{27}$ is the splitting field of $f(x)$ over $\mathbb F_3$. Give a complete proof that your polynomial satisfies the desired conditions.

#proof
We claim that
$$
f(x)=x^3+x^2+2
$$
works.

First we check that $f$ has no root in $\mathbb F_3$. We compute:
$$
f(0)=2\neq 0,
$$
$$
f(1)=1+1+2=4\equiv 1\pmod 3,
$$
and
$$
f(2)=2^3+2^2+2=8+4+2=14\equiv 2\pmod 3.
$$
Thus $f$ has no root in $\mathbb F_3$. Since $f$ has degree $3$, it follows that $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_3$.

Let $\alpha$ be a root of $f$. Then
$$
[\mathbb F_3(\alpha):\mathbb F_3]=3.
$$
Therefore $\mathbb F_3(\alpha)$ has
$$
3^3=27
$$
elements, so
$$
\mathbb F_3(\alpha)\cong \mathbb F_{27}.
$$

Since finite fields are perfect, $f$ has no repeated roots. Equivalently,
$$
f'(x)=3x^2+2x=2x
$$
in $\mathbb F_3[x]$, and $f$ is not divisible by $x$, so $\gcd(f,f')=1$.

The Frobenius automorphism
$$
\tau:\mathbb F_{27}\to \mathbb F_{27},\qquad \tau(z)=z^3,
$$
fixes $\mathbb F_3$. Therefore, if $\alpha$ is a root of $f$, then
$$
\alpha,\quad \alpha^3,\quad \alpha^9
$$
are also roots of $f$. Since $f$ is irreducible of degree $3$ and separable, these are its three distinct roots.

Thus $f$ splits over $\mathbb F_3(\alpha)=\mathbb F_{27}$. Since $f$ is irreducible over $\mathbb F_3$, it cannot split over $\mathbb F_3$. The splitting field must contain a root $\alpha$, and hence must contain $\mathbb F_3(\alpha)=\mathbb F_{27}$.

Therefore the splitting field of
$$
f(x)=x^3+x^2+2
$$
over $\mathbb F_3$ is exactly $\mathbb F_{27}$.

::ProblemBlock{number=10}
#problem
In this problem
$$
i=\sqrt{-1},
$$
that is, a root of the polynomial $x^2+1\in \mathbb Q[x]$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Is $i\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2)$? Prove or disprove.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Is $i\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt[3]2)$? Prove or disprove.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** We claim that
$$
i\notin \mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2).
$$
Every element of $\mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2)$ has the form
$$
a+b\,i\sqrt 2
$$
with $a,b\in \mathbb Q$, because $\mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2)$ is a quadratic extension of $\mathbb Q$.

If $i\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2)$, then there would exist $a,b\in \mathbb Q$ such that
$$
i=a+b\,i\sqrt 2.
$$
Comparing real and imaginary parts gives
$$
a=0,\qquad b\sqrt 2=1.
$$
Thus
$$
b=\frac{1}{\sqrt 2},
$$
which is not rational. This is impossible. Hence
$$
i\notin \mathbb Q(i\sqrt 2).
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** We claim that
$$
i\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt[3]2).
$$
Let
$$
\alpha=i\sqrt[3]2.
$$
Then
$$
\alpha^3=i^3(\sqrt[3]2)^3=-2i.
$$
Therefore
$$
i=-\frac{\alpha^3}{2}.
$$
Since $\alpha\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt[3]2)$, it follows that $\alpha^3\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt[3]2)$, and hence
$$
i\in \mathbb Q(i\sqrt[3]2).
$$
