#problem
Consider the planar ODE
$$
\begin{aligned}
&u'=v,\\
&v'=2v-u(1-u)^2.
\end{aligned}
\hspace{2cm}(1)
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Find the linearization of $(1)$ at the fixed points $p_0=(0,0)$ and $p_1=(1,0)$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Show that the triangular region $T$ bounded by the lines $v=0$, $u=1$, and $v=u$, is negatively invariant under the flow of $(1)$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Show that $(1)$ admits a heteroclinic orbit between the fixed points $p_0$ and $p_1$ which approaches $p_1$ along a center manifold.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Write
$$
F(u,v)=\begin{pmatrix} v \\ 2v-u(1-u)^2 \end{pmatrix}.
$$
The fixed points satisfy $v=0$ and
$$
-u(1-u)^2=0.
$$
Hence the fixed points are
$$
p_0=(0,0),
\qquad
p_1=(1,0).
$$
The Jacobian matrix is
$$
DF(u,v)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
\frac{d}{du}\bigl[-u(1-u)^2\bigr] & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Since
$$
\frac{d}{du}\bigl[-u(1-u)^2\bigr]
=-(1-u)^2+2u(1-u)
=-1+4u-3u^2,
$$
we get
$$
DF(0,0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-1 & 2
\end{pmatrix},
$$
and
$$
DF(1,0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus the linearization at $p_0$ is
$$
\begin{pmatrix} \xi' \\ \eta' \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \xi \\ \eta \end{pmatrix},
$$
and the linearization at $p_1$ is
$$
\begin{pmatrix} \xi' \\ \eta' \end{pmatrix}
=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 2
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix} \xi \\ \eta \end{pmatrix},
$$
where $\xi=u-1$ and $\eta=v$ near $p_1$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** The triangular region is
$$
T=\{(u,v):0\le v\le u\le 1\}.
$$
To prove that $T$ is negatively invariant, it is enough to show that the vector field points out of $T$ along the boundary for forward time. Equivalently, it points into $T$ for the reversed flow.

On the side $v=0$, where $0<u<1$, we have
$$
u'=v=0
$$
and
$$
v'=2v-u(1-u)^2=-u(1-u)^2<0.
$$
Thus the vector field points below the side $v=0$, hence outward for forward time.

On the side $u=1$, where $0<v<1$, we have
$$
u'=v>0.
$$
Thus the vector field points to the right of the side $u=1$, hence outward for forward time.

On the side $v=u$, define
$$
h(u,v)=v-u.
$$
The region $T$ lies in $h\le 0$. Along $v=u$, we have
$$
h'=v'-u'
=\bigl(2v-u(1-u)^2\bigr)-v
=v-u(1-u)^2.
$$
Since $v=u$ on this side,
$$
h'=u-u(1-u)^2
=u\bigl[1-(1-u)^2\bigr]
=u^2(2-u)>0
$$
for $0<u<1$. Hence the vector field points toward $h>0$, again outward for forward time.

Therefore the vector field points outward along each side of $T$ for forward time. Hence it points inward along each side for backward time. Therefore $T$ is negatively invariant.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** At $p_1=(1,0)$, the linearization is
$$
DF(1,0)=
\begin{pmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
0 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its eigenvalues are
$$
\lambda=0,
\qquad
\lambda=2.
$$
Thus $p_1$ has a one-dimensional center direction and a one-dimensional unstable direction.

Let
$$
w=1-u.
$$
Then near $p_1$, the system becomes
$$
w'=-v,
$$
$$
v'=2v-(1-w)w^2.
$$
A center manifold has the form
$$
v=h(w),
\qquad
h(0)=0,
\qquad
h'(0)=0.
$$
The invariance equation is
$$
h'(w)(-h(w))=2h(w)-(1-w)w^2.
$$
If
$$
h(w)=aw^2+bw^3+O(w^4),
$$
then comparing powers of $w$ gives
$$
a=\frac{1}{2},
\qquad
b=-\frac{3}{4}.
$$
Therefore
$$
h(w)=\frac{1}{2}w^2+O(w^3).
$$
On this center manifold,
$$
w'=-h(w)=-\frac{1}{2}w^2+O(w^3).
$$
Hence for small $w>0$, we have $w'<0$, so $w(t)\to 0$ as $t\to \infty$. Equivalently, the center manifold branch inside $T$ approaches $p_1$ as $t\to \infty$.

Now choose a point on this center manifold branch inside $T$. Since $T$ is negatively invariant, the backward orbit through this point remains in the compact set $T$. Therefore its alpha-limit set is nonempty, compact, connected, and invariant.

Also,
$$
\operatorname{div}F
=\frac{\partial}{\partial u}(v)+\frac{\partial}{\partial v}\bigl(2v-u(1-u)^2\bigr)
=2>0.
$$
By the Bendixson criterion, there are no periodic orbits in the interior of $T$. Hence the alpha-limit set must contain an equilibrium. The only equilibria in $T$ are $p_0$ and $p_1$.

The chosen orbit approaches $p_1$ as $t\to\infty$, and it is not the constant orbit at $p_1$. Hence its alpha-limit cannot also be $p_1$. Therefore the alpha-limit is $p_0$.

Thus there exists an orbit such that
$$
\lim_{t\to -\infty}(u(t),v(t))=p_0,
\qquad
\lim_{t\to \infty}(u(t),v(t))=p_1.
$$
This is a heteroclinic orbit from $p_0$ to $p_1$, and it approaches $p_1$ along a center manifold.

#problem
Consider the boundary value problem
$$
\varepsilon y''+a(x)y'+b(x)y=0,
\qquad
0<x<1,
$$
$$
y(0)=y_0,
\qquad
y(1)=y_1,
$$
where $a(x)>0$ for $x\in[0,1]$. Using WKB theory, show that a leading order asymptotic expansion for the solution is given by
$$
y(x)\sim C_1e^{-\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}
+
\frac{C_2}{a(x)}
e^{\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_0^x a(s)\,ds},
$$
where
$$
C_1=y_1e^{\int_0^1 (b(s)/a(s))\,ds},
\qquad
C_2=a(0)(y_0-C_1).
$$

#proof
We look for WKB solutions of the form
$$
y(x)=e^{S(x)/\varepsilon}A(x).
$$
Substituting into
$$
\varepsilon y''+a(x)y'+b(x)y=0
$$
and collecting the leading order terms gives
$$
(S')^2+a(x)S'=0.
$$
Thus
$$
S'=0
\qquad\text{or}\qquad
S'=-a(x).
$$
These two roots give a slow mode and a fast boundary-layer mode.

First consider the slow mode $S'=0$. Then $y(x)=A(x)$ to leading order. Substituting into the equation and keeping the leading order terms gives
$$
a(x)A'(x)+b(x)A(x)=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{A'(x)}{A(x)}=-\frac{b(x)}{a(x)},
$$
and hence
$$
A(x)=C_1e^{-\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}.
$$
So the slow mode is
$$
y_s(x)=C_1e^{-\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}.
$$

Now consider the fast mode
$$
S(x)=-\int_0^x a(s)\,ds.
$$
Write
$$
y_f(x)=e^{-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_0^x a(s)\,ds}B(x).
$$
Substituting this into the differential equation gives
$$
\varepsilon B''-aB'+(b-a')B=0.
$$
At leading order,
$$
-aB'+(b-a')B=0.
$$
Thus
$$
\frac{B'}{B}=\frac{b}{a}-\frac{a'}{a}.
$$
Therefore
$$
B(x)=\frac{C_2}{a(x)}e^{\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}.
$$
The fast mode is therefore
$$
y_f(x)=\frac{C_2}{a(x)}e^{\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_0^x a(s)\,ds}.
$$

Combining the two leading order modes gives
$$
y(x)\sim C_1e^{-\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}
+
\frac{C_2}{a(x)}e^{\int_0^x (b(s)/a(s))\,ds-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\int_0^x a(s)\,ds}.
$$

Since $a(x)>0$ on $[0,1]$, we have
$$
\int_0^1 a(s)\,ds>0.
$$
Therefore the fast mode is exponentially small at $x=1$. Hence the boundary condition at $x=1$ gives
$$
y_1\sim C_1e^{-\int_0^1 (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}.
$$
Thus
$$
C_1=y_1e^{\int_0^1 (b(s)/a(s))\,ds}.
$$
At $x=0$, the exponential factors are $1$, so
$$
y_0\sim C_1+\frac{C_2}{a(0)}.
$$
Therefore
$$
C_2=a(0)(y_0-C_1).
$$
This gives the desired leading order asymptotic expansion.

#problem
Consider the initial value problem
$$
\dot u=f(u),
\qquad
u(0)=u_0, \tag{2}
$$
where $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R^n$ is locally Lipschitz. That is, there exist $K,\delta>0$ such that
$$
|f(u)-f(v)|\le K|u-v|
$$
for all $u,v\in B_\delta(u_0)=\{u\in\mathbb R^n:|u-u_0|<\delta\}$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Consider the map $T$ defined by
$$
(Tu)(t)=u_0+\int_0^t f(u(s))\,ds,
$$
and take $\varepsilon<\min\{1/K,\delta/M\}$ where
$$
M=\sup_{u\in B_\delta(u_0)}|f(u)|.
$$
Show that $T$ is a contraction on the space
$$
\mathcal B=\{u\in C^0([-\varepsilon,\varepsilon],\mathbb R^n):
\sup_{t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}|u(t)-u_0|<\delta\},
$$
where $C^0([-\varepsilon,\varepsilon],\mathbb R^n)$ denotes the space of continuous functions with the supremum norm
$$
\|u\|=\sup_{t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]}|u(t)|.
$$
What does this imply about solutions to the initial value problem $(2)$? You may use the contraction mapping theorem without proof.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Now suppose $f\in C^1(\mathbb R^n)$ and that the maximal interval of existence of the solution to the initial value problem $(2)$ is $(-\infty,\beta)$ where $\beta<\infty$. Show that $|u(t)|$ is unbounded as $t\to\beta$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** First we show that $T$ maps $\mathcal B$ into itself. Let $u\in\mathcal B$. Then for all $t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$, the values $u(t)$ lie in $B_\delta(u_0)$. Hence
$$
|f(u(t))|\le M.
$$
Therefore
$$
|(Tu)(t)-u_0|
=\left|\int_0^t f(u(s))\,ds\right|
\le \int_0^{|t|} M\,ds
\le \varepsilon M.
$$
Since
$$
\varepsilon<\frac{\delta}{M},
$$
we get
$$
|(Tu)(t)-u_0|<\delta.
$$
Thus $Tu\in\mathcal B$.

Now let $u,v\in\mathcal B$. Then
$$
|(Tu)(t)-(Tv)(t)|
=\left|\int_0^t \bigl(f(u(s))-f(v(s))\bigr)\,ds\right|.
$$
Using the Lipschitz condition,
$$
|(Tu)(t)-(Tv)(t)|
\le \int_0^{|t|} K|u(s)-v(s)|\,ds
\le K\varepsilon \|u-v\|.
$$
Taking the supremum over $t\in[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$ gives
$$
\|Tu-Tv\|\le K\varepsilon \|u-v\|.
$$
Since
$$
\varepsilon<\frac{1}{K},
$$
we have $K\varepsilon<1$. Therefore $T$ is a contraction.

By the contraction mapping theorem, $T$ has a unique fixed point in $\mathcal B$. A fixed point satisfies
$$
u(t)=u_0+\int_0^t f(u(s))\,ds.
$$
Differentiating gives
$$
\dot u(t)=f(u(t)),
\qquad
u(0)=u_0.
$$
Therefore the initial value problem has a unique local solution on $[-\varepsilon,\varepsilon]$.

Strictly speaking, the contraction mapping theorem applies directly to a closed complete ball. One may replace the strict inequality in the definition of $\mathcal B$ by $\le\delta$; the above estimates still show that $T$ maps the closed ball into the strict ball.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Suppose, toward a contradiction, that $|u(t)|$ is bounded as $t\to\beta$. Then there exists $R>0$ such that
$$
|u(t)|\le R
$$
for all $t<\beta$ sufficiently close to $\beta$.

Since $f\in C^1(\mathbb R^n)$, it is continuous. Hence $f$ is bounded on the compact ball
$$
\overline{B_R(0)}.
$$
So there exists $M_R>0$ such that
$$
|f(z)|\le M_R
$$
for all $z\in\overline{B_R(0)}$.

For $s,t$ sufficiently close to $\beta$, we have
$$
|u(t)-u(s)|
=\left|\int_s^t f(u(r))\,dr\right|
\le M_R|t-s|.
$$
Thus $u(t)$ is Cauchy as $t\to\beta^-$. Therefore there exists $u_\beta\in\mathbb R^n$ such that
$$
\lim_{t\to\beta^-}u(t)=u_\beta.
$$

By the local existence theorem applied at the initial condition $u(\beta)=u_\beta$, there is a solution on some interval $[\beta,\beta+\eta)$ for some $\eta>0$. Gluing this solution to the old solution on $(-\infty,\beta)$ extends $u$ beyond $\beta$, contradicting the maximality of $(-\infty,\beta)$.

Hence the assumption was false. Therefore
$$
|u(t)|\to\infty
$$
along some sequence as $t\to\beta^-$. Equivalently, $|u(t)|$ is unbounded as $t\to\beta$.

#problem
Numerical ODE problem.

Consider a linear multistep scheme of the form
$$
w_{n+1}=a_1w_n+a_2w_{n-1}+h\bigl(b_0f(t_{n+1},w_{n+1})+b_1f(t_n,w_n)\bigr),
\qquad n\ge 1,
$$
$$
w_1=y_1,
\qquad
w_0=y_0,
$$
for solving the ODE
$$
y'=f(t,y),
\qquad
0<t\le T,
\qquad
y(0)=y_0.
$$
Here, $h=T/N$ is the time step, $N$ is the total number of time steps, $t_n=nh$, and $w_n$ is the numerical approximation to $y(t)$ at $t=t_n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Find the equation for the error $e_n=y_n-w_n$, that describes how the error propagates in time, and describe the meaning of each of the terms in the equation.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** What is the highest order of accuracy this method can attain? Determine the coefficients $a_1,a_2,b_0,b_1$ that make the scheme reach this order of accuracy, assuming $y_1$ is a sufficiently accurate approximation of $y(h)$. Hint: The scheme is $p$th order accurate if the test functions $y(t)=1,t,t^2,\dots,t^p$, and $f(t,y(t))=0,1,2t,\dots,pt^{p-1}$ satisfy the scheme exactly with $h=1$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** How do you determine if the method is stable? Hint: Do not forget that this is a multistep method.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Define A-stability for a numerical scheme for first order ODEs. Is the optimally accurate scheme you found A-stable?

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let
$$
y_n=y(t_n),
\qquad
e_n=y_n-w_n.
$$
Define the local truncation residual by
$$
\tau_{n+1}
=
y_{n+1}-a_1y_n-a_2y_{n-1}
-h\bigl(b_0f(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})+b_1f(t_n,y_n)\bigr).
$$
Subtracting the numerical scheme from the exact equation with residual $\tau_{n+1}$ gives
$$
e_{n+1}
=a_1e_n+a_2e_{n-1}
+h b_0\bigl[f(t_{n+1},y_{n+1})-f(t_{n+1},w_{n+1})\bigr]
+h b_1\bigl[f(t_n,y_n)-f(t_n,w_n)\bigr]
+\tau_{n+1}.
$$
The terms $a_1e_n+a_2e_{n-1}$ describe propagation of past errors. The two terms involving $f(t,y)-f(t,w)$ describe how nonlinear dependence on $y$ amplifies or damps error. The term $\tau_{n+1}$ is the local truncation error generated by inserting the exact solution into the numerical method.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** We use the order conditions with $h=1$. The method is
$$
y_{n+1}=a_1y_n+a_2y_{n-1}+b_0y'_{n+1}+b_1y'_n.
$$

For $y(t)=1$, we get
$$
1=a_1+a_2. \tag{3}
$$
For $y(t)=t$, we get
$$
n+1=a_1n+a_2(n-1)+b_0+b_1.
$$
Using $(3)$, this gives
$$
b_0+b_1=1+a_2. \tag{4}
$$
For $y(t)=t^2$, we get
$$
(n+1)^2=a_1n^2+a_2(n-1)^2+2b_0(n+1)+2b_1n.
$$
Equating the constant terms after using $(3)$ gives
$$
4b_0+2b_1+a_1=4. \tag{5}
$$
For $y(t)=t^3$, we get
$$
(n+1)^3=a_1n^3+a_2(n-1)^3+3b_0(n+1)^2+3b_1n^2.
$$
This yields
$$
12b_0+3b_1+a_1=8. \tag{6}
$$
Solving $(3)$ through $(6)$ gives
$$
a_1=\frac45,
\qquad
a_2=\frac15,
\qquad
b_0=\frac25,
\qquad
b_1=\frac45.
$$
There are four free coefficients, and these conditions give exactness through degree $3$. The next condition, for $y(t)=t^4$, is not satisfied by these coefficients. Therefore the highest possible order is
$$
p=3.
$$
The third-order method is
$$
w_{n+1}=\frac45 w_n+\frac15 w_{n-1}
+h\left(\frac25 f(t_{n+1},w_{n+1})+\frac45 f(t_n,w_n)\right).
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Since this is a multistep method, stability is determined by a root condition.

For zero-stability, we consider the first characteristic polynomial
$$
\rho(\xi)=\xi^2-a_1\xi-a_2.
$$
The method is zero-stable if every root of $\rho$ satisfies
$$
|\xi|\le 1,
$$
and every root with $|\xi|=1$ is simple.

For the optimally accurate method,
$$
\rho(\xi)=\xi^2-\frac45\xi-\frac15
=(\xi-1)\left(\xi+\frac15\right).
$$
The roots are
$$
\xi=1,
\qquad
\xi=-\frac15.
$$
The root $1$ is simple, and the other root has modulus less than $1$. Hence the optimally accurate method is zero-stable.

For absolute stability, one applies the method to the test equation
$$
y'=\lambda y,
$$
and sets
$$
z=h\lambda.
$$
Then the recurrence becomes
$$
(1-b_0z)w_{n+1}-(a_1+b_1z)w_n-a_2w_{n-1}=0.
$$
The method is absolutely stable for a given $z$ if all roots $\xi$ of
$$
(1-b_0z)\xi^2-(a_1+b_1z)\xi-a_2=0
$$
satisfy $|\xi|\le 1$, with unit-modulus roots simple.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** A numerical method is A-stable if, for the test equation
$$
y'=\lambda y,
$$
its region of absolute stability contains the whole closed left half-plane
$$
\{z=h\lambda:\operatorname{Re}z\le 0\}.
$$
Equivalently, whenever $\operatorname{Re}\lambda\le 0$, the numerical solution is stable for every step size $h>0$.

For the optimally accurate method, the stability polynomial is
$$
\left(1-\frac25z\right)\xi^2-
\left(\frac45+\frac45z\right)\xi-\frac15=0.
$$
Multiplying by $5$ gives
$$
(5-2z)\xi^2-(4+4z)\xi-1=0.
$$
Take
$$
z=-10.
$$
Then $\operatorname{Re}z<0$, but the polynomial becomes
$$
25\xi^2+36\xi-1=0.
$$
Its roots are
$$
\xi=\frac{-36\pm\sqrt{36^2+100}}{50}
=\frac{-18\pm\sqrt{349}}{25}.
$$
The root
$$
\frac{-18-\sqrt{349}}{25}
$$
has modulus greater than $1$. Therefore the method is not absolutely stable at $z=-10$.

Hence the optimally accurate scheme is not A-stable.

#problem
Least squares problem.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let $A$ be a real $m\times n$ matrix. State the singular value decomposition (SVD) of $A$. Briefly present an algorithm to solve the overdetermined system $(m>n)$ using the SVD.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** What are the advantages and disadvantages of using the SVD to solve overdetermined systems?

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Perform the SVD on the matrix
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Compute the pseudo-inverse of $A$, the Moore-Penrose pseudo-inverse. Leave in factored form.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(e)** Find the minimal length, least squares solution of the problem
$$
Ax=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
2 \\
3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The singular value decomposition of a real $m\times n$ matrix $A$ is
$$
A=U\Sigma V^T,
$$
where $U$ is an $m\times m$ orthogonal matrix, $V$ is an $n\times n$ orthogonal matrix, and $\Sigma$ is an $m\times n$ diagonal matrix whose nonnegative diagonal entries
$$
\sigma_1\ge \sigma_2\ge \cdots \ge 0
$$
are the singular values of $A$.

To solve an overdetermined system
$$
Ax\approx b
$$
in the least squares sense, compute
$$
A=U\Sigma V^T.
$$
Then the minimal length least squares solution is
$$
x=A^+b,
$$
where
$$
A^+=V\Sigma^+U^T.
$$
Here $\Sigma^+$ is obtained by replacing each nonzero singular value $\sigma_i$ by $1/\sigma_i$ and transposing the rectangular diagonal matrix.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Advantages of the SVD method:

It is numerically stable, especially for ill-conditioned matrices. It reveals the numerical rank of $A$. It works even when $A$ is rank deficient. It gives the minimal length least squares solution automatically.

Disadvantages:

It is more computationally expensive than methods such as QR factorization or normal equations. For very large problems, computing a full SVD can be too costly in both time and memory.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Let
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1 \\
1 & 1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
This matrix has rank $1$. We can write
$$
A=\sqrt6\,u_1v_1^T,
$$
where
$$
u_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt3}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
1 \\
1
\end{pmatrix},
\qquad
v_1=\frac{1}{\sqrt2}
\begin{pmatrix}
1 \\
1
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Thus the only nonzero singular value is
$$
\sigma_1=\sqrt6.
$$
A possible choice of orthogonal matrices is
$$
U=
\begin{pmatrix}
\frac{1}{\sqrt3} & \frac{1}{\sqrt2} & \frac{1}{\sqrt6} \\

#problem
Eigenvalue problem.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix. Prove Gershgorin's theorem, which states: For $i=1,\dots,n$, let
$$
R_i=\sum_{\substack{j=1\\ j\neq i}}^n |a_{ij}|.
$$
Every eigenvalue of $A$ falls within at least one of the closed discs in the complex plane with center $a_{ii}$ and radius $R_i$. Hint: Let
$$
Ax=\lambda x \tag{7}
$$
and assume that the largest component of $x$ in absolute value is $x_k$. Consider the $k$th equation of $(7)$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Consider the matrix
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & -1 & 0 & 0 & 0 \\
-1 & 2 & -1 & 0 & 0 \\
0 & -1 & 2 & -1 & 0 \\
0 & 0 & -1 & 2 & -1 \\
0 & 0 & 0 & -1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Show that $A$ is positive definite. Hint: Use Gershgorin's theorem to show that $A$ is positive semi-definite. Then consider the equation $Ax=0$, where the first component of $x$ equals $1$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Let
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & -1 & 0 \\
-1 & 2 & -1 \\
0 & -1 & 2
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Find the eigenvalues of $A$ and verify that Gershgorin's theorem holds.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let $\lambda$ be an eigenvalue of $A$, and let $x\neq 0$ be a corresponding eigenvector. Choose $k$ such that
$$
|x_k|=\max_{1\le i\le n}|x_i|.
$$
Then $x_k\neq 0$.

The $k$th component of
$$
Ax=\lambda x
$$
is
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n a_{kj}x_j=\lambda x_k.
$$
Thus
$$
(\lambda-a_{kk})x_k=\sum_{j\neq k}a_{kj}x_j.
$$
Taking absolute values gives
$$
|\lambda-a_{kk}|\,|x_k|
\le
\sum_{j\neq k}|a_{kj}|\,|x_j|.
$$
Since $|x_j|\le |x_k|$ for every $j$, we get
$$
|\lambda-a_{kk}|\,|x_k|
\le
\sum_{j\neq k}|a_{kj}|\,|x_k|.
$$
Because $x_k\neq 0$, we may divide by $|x_k|$ and obtain
$$
|\lambda-a_{kk}|
\le
\sum_{j\neq k}|a_{kj}|=R_k.
$$
Therefore $\lambda$ lies in the Gershgorin disc centered at $a_{kk}$ with radius $R_k$. Hence every eigenvalue lies in at least one Gershgorin disc.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** The matrix $A$ is real symmetric, so all of its eigenvalues are real.

The Gershgorin discs are centered at $2$. For the first and last rows, the radius is $1$, and for the middle three rows, the radius is $2$. Hence all eigenvalues lie in
$$
[1,3]\cup[0,4]=[0,4].
$$
Since the eigenvalues are real, all eigenvalues are nonnegative. Therefore $A$ is positive semi-definite.

It remains to show that $0$ is not an eigenvalue. Suppose
$$
Ax=0.
$$
If $x_1=0$, then the first equation gives
$$
2x_1-x_2=0,
$$
so $x_2=0$. Then the second equation gives $x_3=0$, and continuing gives $x=0$.

Thus any nonzero solution of $Ax=0$ would have $x_1\neq 0$. Scaling, assume
$$
x_1=1.
$$
The first equation gives
$$
2x_1-x_2=0,
$$
so
$$
x_2=2.
$$
The second equation gives
$$
-x_1+2x_2-x_3=0,
$$
so
$$
x_3=3.
$$
Continuing in this way gives
$$
x_4=4,
\qquad
x_5=5.
$$
But the last equation is
$$
-x_4+2x_5=0.
$$
Substituting $x_4=4$ and $x_5=5$ gives
$$
-4+10=6\neq 0,
$$
a contradiction. Therefore $Ax=0$ has no nonzero solution, so $0$ is not an eigenvalue.

Hence all eigenvalues are strictly positive. Therefore $A$ is positive definite.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** For
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
2 & -1 & 0 \\
-1 & 2 & -1 \\
0 & -1 & 2
\end{pmatrix},
$$
the characteristic polynomial is
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)
=
\det
\begin{pmatrix}
2-\lambda & -1 & 0 \\
-1 & 2-\lambda & -1 \\
0 & -1 & 2-\lambda
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Expanding gives
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)
=(2-\lambda)\bigl((2-\lambda)^2-1\bigr)-(2-\lambda).
$$
Thus
$$
\det(A-\lambda I)
=(2-\lambda)\bigl((2-\lambda)^2-2\bigr).
$$
Therefore the eigenvalues are
$$
\lambda=2,
\qquad
\lambda=2-\sqrt2,
\qquad
\lambda=2+\sqrt2.
$$

Now compute the Gershgorin discs. The first and third rows have center $2$ and radius $1$, so they give
$$
[1,3].
$$
The second row has center $2$ and radius $2$, so it gives
$$
[0,4].
$$
Thus Gershgorin's theorem says that all eigenvalues lie in
$$
[0,4].
$$
Indeed,
$$
2-\sqrt2\in[0,4],
\qquad
2\in[0,4],
\qquad
2+\sqrt2\in[0,4].
$$
Therefore Gershgorin's theorem is verified.

#problem
Let $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$ be given by
$$
g(v)=v[1+\min\{v,0\}].
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Show that $g$ is convex, continuously differentiable, and satisfies
$$
g(v)\ge \max\{v,|v|-1\}.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Deduce that any arc $x$ admissible for the problem
$$
\min J(x)=\int_0^1\bigl(x^2+g(x')\bigr)\,dt
$$
subject to
$$
x\in AC[0,1],
\qquad
x(0)=0,
\qquad
x(1)=1,
$$
satisfies
$$
J(x)>1.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Show that the functions
$$
x_i(t)=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0\le t\le 1-1/i, \\
i[t-1+1/i], & 1-1/i<t\le 1,
\end{cases}
$$
satisfy
$$
\lim_{i\to\infty}J(x_i)=1.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Conclude that the problem defined in **(b)** admits no solution.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Since
$$
\min\{v,0\}=
\begin{cases}
v, & v<0, \\
0, & v\ge 0,
\end{cases}
$$
we have
$$
g(v)=
\begin{cases}
v+v^2, & v<0, \\
v, & v\ge 0.
\end{cases}
$$
For $v<0$,
$$
g'(v)=1+2v.
$$
For $v>0$,
$$
g'(v)=1.
$$
At $v=0$, the left derivative is $1$ and the right derivative is also $1$. Hence $g\in C^1(\mathbb R)$.

Also, $g''(v)=2$ for $v<0$ and $g''(v)=0$ for $v>0$, and $g'$ is nondecreasing across $v=0$. Therefore $g$ is convex.

Now we prove
$$
g(v)\ge \max\{v,|v|-1\}.
$$
If $v\ge 0$, then
$$
g(v)=v.
$$
Also $|v|-1=v-1\le v$. Hence
$$
g(v)=v=\max\{v,|v|-1\}.
$$

If $v<0$, then
$$
g(v)=v+v^2\ge v.
$$
Also
$$
g(v)-(|v|-1)
=v+v^2-(-v-1)
=v^2+2v+1
=(v+1)^2\ge 0.
$$
Thus $g(v)\ge |v|-1$. Hence
$$
g(v)\ge \max\{v,|v|-1\}.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $x$ be admissible. Since $g(v)\ge v$ for all $v$, we have
$$
g(x'(t))\ge x'(t)
$$
for almost every $t$. Therefore
$$
J(x)
=\int_0^1\bigl(x(t)^2+g(x'(t))\bigr)\,dt
\ge
\int_0^1 x(t)^2\,dt+
\int_0^1 x'(t)\,dt.
$$
Because $x\in AC[0,1]$, the fundamental theorem of calculus gives
$$
\int_0^1 x'(t)\,dt=x(1)-x(0)=1.
$$
Thus
$$
J(x)\ge 1+\int_0^1 x(t)^2\,dt.
$$
Since $x(1)=1$ and $x$ is continuous, $x$ cannot be identically zero. Hence
$$
\int_0^1 x(t)^2\,dt>0.
$$
Therefore
$$
J(x)>1.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** For
$$
x_i(t)=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0\le t\le 1-1/i, \\
i[t-1+1/i], & 1-1/i<t\le 1,
\end{cases}
$$
we have
$$
x_i'(t)=
\begin{cases}
0, & 0<t<1-1/i, \\
i, & 1-1/i<t<1.
\end{cases}
$$
Since $i>0$, we have
$$
g(i)=i.
$$
Thus
$$
J(x_i)=\int_{1-1/i}^1 \left(i^2[t-1+1/i]^2+i\right)dt.
$$
Let
$$
s=t-1+\frac1i.
$$
Then $s$ runs from $0$ to $1/i$. Hence
$$
J(x_i)=\int_0^{1/i}(i^2s^2+i)\,ds.
$$
Therefore
$$
J(x_i)
=i^2\frac{(1/i)^3}{3}+i\frac1i
=\frac{1}{3i}+1.
$$
Taking $i\to\infty$, we get
$$
\lim_{i\to\infty}J(x_i)=1.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** From **(b)**, every admissible arc satisfies
$$
J(x)>1.
$$
From **(c)**, there exists an admissible sequence $x_i$ such that
$$
J(x_i)\to 1.
$$
Therefore
$$
\inf J=1,
$$
but no admissible $x$ satisfies $J(x)=1$. Hence the problem admits no solution.

#problem
Assume $f\in L^2(\Omega)$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove the dual variational principle
$$
\min_{v\in H_0^1(\Omega)}
\int_\Omega\left(\frac12|\nabla v|^2-fv\right)dx
=
\max_{\substack{\sigma\in L^2(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)\\ \operatorname{div}\sigma=f}}
-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Write out Euler-Lagrange equations for both formulations.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let
$$
I(v)=\int_\Omega\left(\frac12|\nabla v|^2-fv\right)dx,
\qquad
v\in H_0^1(\Omega).
$$
Let $\sigma\in L^2(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)$ satisfy
$$
\operatorname{div}\sigma=f
$$
in the sense of distributions. Since $v\in H_0^1(\Omega)$, integration by parts gives
$$
\int_\Omega fv\,dx
=
\int_\Omega v\operatorname{div}\sigma\,dx
=-\int_\Omega \sigma\cdot\nabla v\,dx.
$$
Therefore
$$
I(v)
=\int_\Omega\left(\frac12|\nabla v|^2+\sigma\cdot\nabla v\right)dx.
$$
Complete the square:
$$
\frac12|\nabla v|^2+\sigma\cdot\nabla v
=
\frac12|\nabla v+\sigma|^2-\frac12|\sigma|^2.
$$
Hence
$$
I(v)
=
\frac12\int_\Omega |\nabla v+\sigma|^2\,dx
-
\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx
\ge
-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$
Thus, for every admissible $v$ and every admissible $\sigma$,
$$
I(v)\ge -\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$
Taking the minimum over $v$ and the maximum over $\sigma$ gives the weak duality inequality
$$
\min_{v\in H_0^1(\Omega)}I(v)
\ge
\max_{\operatorname{div}\sigma=f}
-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$

Now let $u\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ be the minimizer of $I$. Its Euler-Lagrange equation is
$$
-\Delta u=f
$$
in the weak sense. Define
$$
\sigma=-\nabla u.
$$
Then
$$
\operatorname{div}\sigma=-\Delta u=f.
$$
Thus $\sigma$ is admissible for the dual problem. Also,
$$
\nabla u+\sigma=0.
$$
Therefore equality holds in the completed-square inequality, and
$$
I(u)=-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$
Hence
$$
\min_{v\in H_0^1(\Omega)}
\int_\Omega\left(\frac12|\nabla v|^2-fv\right)dx
=
\max_{\substack{\sigma\in L^2(\Omega;\mathbb R^n)\\ \operatorname{div}\sigma=f}}
-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** For the primal problem, the Euler-Lagrange equation is
$$
\int_\Omega \nabla u\cdot\nabla \varphi\,dx
=
\int_\Omega f\varphi\,dx
$$
for every $\varphi\in H_0^1(\Omega)$. Equivalently,
$$
-\Delta u=f
\quad\text{in }\Omega,
\qquad
u=0
\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega.
$$

For the dual problem, introduce a Lagrange multiplier $u\in H_0^1(\Omega)$ for the constraint
$$
\operatorname{div}\sigma=f.
$$
The Lagrangian is
$$
\mathcal L(\sigma,u)
=-\frac12\int_\Omega |\sigma|^2\,dx
+
\int_\Omega u(\operatorname{div}\sigma-f)\,dx.
$$
Taking variations in $\sigma$ gives
$$
\sigma=-\nabla u.
$$
Taking variations in $u$ gives the constraint
$$
\operatorname{div}\sigma=f.
$$
Thus the Euler-Lagrange system for the dual formulation is
$$
\sigma=-\nabla u,
\qquad
\operatorname{div}\sigma=f.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
-\Delta u=f,
\qquad
u=0\text{ on }\partial\Omega.
$$

#problem
For each of the given Lagrangians, find the Hamiltonian and solve the Hamiltonian system.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)**
$$
L(t,x,v)=(v+kx)^2,
\qquad
k\neq 0.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**
$$
L(t,x,v)=e^{-x}\sqrt{1+v^2}.
$$

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The momentum is
$$
p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}=2(v+kx).
$$
Thus
$$
v=\frac{p}{2}-kx.
$$
The Hamiltonian is
$$
H(t,x,p)=pv-L(t,x,v).
$$
Since
$$
v+kx=\frac{p}{2},
$$
we get
$$
H=p\left(\frac{p}{2}-kx\right)-\left(\frac{p}{2}\right)^2
=\frac{p^2}{4}-kxp.
$$
Therefore
$$
H(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{4}-kxp.
$$

Hamilton's equations are
$$
\dot x=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}
=\frac{p}{2}-kx,
$$
$$
\dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}
=kp.
$$
Solving the second equation gives
$$
p(t)=Ce^{kt}.
$$
Then
$$
\dot x+kx=\frac{C}{2}e^{kt}.
$$
Multiplying by the integrating factor $e^{kt}$,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\bigl(e^{kt}x(t)\bigr)
=\frac{C}{2}e^{2kt}.
$$
Since $k\neq 0$,
$$
e^{kt}x(t)=\frac{C}{4k}e^{2kt}+D.
$$
Hence
$$
x(t)=\frac{C}{4k}e^{kt}+De^{-kt}.
$$
Thus the Hamiltonian solutions are
$$
p(t)=Ce^{kt},
\qquad
x(t)=\frac{C}{4k}e^{kt}+De^{-kt},
$$
where $C,D$ are constants.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** The momentum is
$$
p=\frac{\partial L}{\partial v}
=e^{-x}\frac{v}{\sqrt{1+v^2}}.
$$
Thus
$$
p^2=e^{-2x}\frac{v^2}{1+v^2}.
$$
Solving for $v$ gives
$$
v=\frac{p}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}},
$$
where the sign agrees with the sign of $p$.

The Hamiltonian is
$$
H=pv-L.
$$
Since
$$
1+v^2=\frac{e^{-2x}}{e^{-2x}-p^2},
$$
we have
$$
L=e^{-x}\sqrt{1+v^2}
=\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}}.
$$
Also
$$
pv=\frac{p^2}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}}.
$$
Therefore
$$
H
=\frac{p^2-e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}}
=-\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
H(x,p)=-\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}.
$$

Hamilton's equations are
$$
\dot x=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}
=\frac{p}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}},
$$
$$
\dot p=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}
=-\frac{e^{-2x}}{\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}}.
$$
Since $H$ is independent of $t$, it is constant along solutions. Let
$$
\sqrt{e^{-2x}-p^2}=c,
\qquad
c>0.
$$
Then
$$
e^{-2x}=p^2+c^2.
$$
The equation for $p$ becomes
$$
\dot p=-\frac{p^2+c^2}{c}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{dp}{p^2+c^2}=-\frac{dt}{c}.
$$
Integrating,
$$
\arctan\left(\frac{p}{c}\right)=C_0-t.
$$
Thus
$$
p(t)=c\tan(C_0-t).
$$
Since
$$
e^{-2x}=p^2+c^2=c^2\sec^2(C_0-t),
$$
we get
$$
e^{-x}=c\sec(C_0-t).
$$
Hence
$$
x(t)=\log\left(\frac{\cos(C_0-t)}{c}\right),
$$
on any interval where $\cos(C_0-t)>0$. Therefore the Hamiltonian solutions are
$$
p(t)=c\tan(C_0-t),
\qquad
x(t)=\log\left(\frac{\cos(C_0-t)}{c}\right),
$$
where $c>0$ and $C_0$ are constants.
