::ProblemBlock{number=A1}
#problem
Let $\phi:\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$ be a $C^k$ flow, $k\geq 1$.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** For $u_0\in\mathbb{R}^n$, define the omega limit set $\omega(u_0)$. Show that if the forward orbit $\{\phi_t(u_0)\}_{t>0}$ is bounded, then $\omega(u_0)$ is nonempty.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Suppose $u_0\in\mathbb{R}^n$ has nonempty omega limit set $\omega(u_0)\neq\varnothing$. Show that $\omega(u_0)$ is invariant; that is, if $v\in\omega(u_0)$, then $\phi_t(v)\in\omega(u_0)$ for all $t$.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Suppose $u_0\in\mathbb{R}^n$ satisfies $\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_t(u_0)=u_*\in\mathbb{R}^n$. Show that $u_*$ must be a fixed point.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Suppose $v\in\omega(u_0)$ and there exist $t_m\to\infty$ satisfying $0<t_{m+1}-t_m<1$ for all $m$ and $\phi_{t_m}(u_0)\to v$. Show that either $v$ is a fixed point or $v$ lies on a periodic orbit.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** For $u_0\in\mathbb{R}^n$, the omega limit set is
$$
\omega(u_0)=\left\{v\in\mathbb{R}^n:\text{ there are }t_j\to\infty\text{ with }\phi_{t_j}(u_0)\to v\right\}.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\omega(u_0)=\bigcap_{T>0}\overline{\{\phi_t(u_0):t\geq T\}}.
$$
If the forward orbit is bounded, then each closed tail
$$
K_T=\overline{\{\phi_t(u_0):t\geq T\}}
$$
is a nonempty compact set, and the family is nested: $K_{T_2}\subset K_{T_1}$ when $T_2>T_1$. By compactness, the intersection of these nested compact sets is nonempty. Hence $\omega(u_0)\neq\varnothing$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $v\in\omega(u_0)$, so $\phi_{t_j}(u_0)\to v$ for some $t_j\to\infty$. For fixed $s\in\mathbb{R}$, for all large $j$ we have $t_j+s>0$, and the flow property and continuity give
$$
\phi_s(v)=\lim_{j\to\infty}\phi_s(\phi_{t_j}(u_0))
=\lim_{j\to\infty}\phi_{t_j+s}(u_0).
$$
Since $t_j+s\to\infty$, this proves $\phi_s(v)\in\omega(u_0)$. Thus $\omega(u_0)$ is invariant.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Suppose $\phi_t(u_0)\to u_*$ as $t\to\infty$. For any fixed $s\in\mathbb{R}$,
$$
\phi_s(u_*)=\phi_s\left(\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_t(u_0)\right)
=\lim_{t\to\infty}\phi_{t+s}(u_0)=u_*.
$$
Thus $u_*$ is fixed by the whole flow, and in particular it is an equilibrium.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** Let $\phi_{t_m}(u_0)\to v$ and $0<t_{m+1}-t_m<1$. Passing to a subsequence, set
$$
\tau_m=t_{m+1}-t_m\to\tau\in[0,1].
$$
Then
$$
\phi_{\tau}(v)=\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi_{\tau_m}(\phi_{t_m}(u_0))
=\lim_{m\to\infty}\phi_{t_{m+1}}(u_0)=v.
$$
If $\tau>0$, then $v$ lies on a periodic orbit, with period dividing $\tau$ unless $v$ is an equilibrium. If $\tau=0$, then
$$
\frac{\phi_{\tau_m}(\phi_{t_m}(u_0))-\phi_{t_m}(u_0)}{\tau_m}\to f(v),
$$
because the vector field is continuous and $\phi_{t_m}(u_0)\to v$. The numerator tends to $v-v=0$ along the chosen sequence, so $f(v)=0$. Hence $v$ is a fixed point. Therefore either $v$ is fixed or $v$ lies on a periodic orbit.

::ProblemBlock{number=A2}
#problem
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** By converting to polar coordinates, show that
$$
\dot x=-2x+y+x(x^2+3y^2),\qquad
\dot y=-x-2y+y(x^2+3y^2)
$$
has at least one periodic orbit. Clearly state any theorems you use.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** By considering the surface $S=\{(x,y,z):z=x^2+y^2\}$ or otherwise, show that
$$
\dot x=-2x+y+x(z+2y^2),\quad
\dot y=-x-2y+y(z+2y^2),\quad
\dot z=2z(-2+z+2y^2)
$$
admits at least one periodic orbit.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Put $x=r\cos\theta$, $y=r\sin\theta$. A direct computation gives
$$
\dot r=r\left(-2+x^2+3y^2\right)
=r\left(-2+r^2(1+2\sin^2\theta)\right),
\qquad
\dot\theta=-1.
$$
It is convenient to use $\theta$ as the independent variable and set $\rho=r^{-2}$. Since $\dot\theta=-1$,
$$
\frac{dr}{d\theta}=r\left(2-r^2(1+2\sin^2\theta)\right),
$$
and therefore
$$
\rho'+4\rho=2(1+2\sin^2\theta)=4-2\cos(2\theta).
$$
This linear equation has a unique $2\pi$-periodic solution,
$$
\rho_p(\theta)=1-\frac{2}{5}\cos(2\theta)-\frac{1}{5}\sin(2\theta),
$$
which is positive for every $\theta$, since its oscillatory amplitude is $\sqrt5/5<1$. Hence $r_p(\theta)=\rho_p(\theta)^{-1/2}$ gives a closed nonconstant trajectory. Thus the planar system has at least one periodic orbit.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $S=\{(x,y,z):z=x^2+y^2\}$. Along solutions of the three-dimensional system,
$$
\frac{d}{dt}(x^2+y^2)=2x\dot x+2y\dot y=2(x^2+y^2)(-2+z+2y^2).
$$
On the surface $S$, this equals
$$
2z(-2+z+2y^2)=\dot z.
$$
Therefore $S$ is invariant. On $S$, where $z=x^2+y^2$, the first two equations reduce exactly to the planar system in part (a). The periodic orbit found in part (a) therefore lifts to a periodic orbit of the three-dimensional system.

::ProblemBlock{number=A3}
#problem
Using WKB theory, find a leading order asymptotic expansion for the solution of
$$
\varepsilon^2y''-f(x)y=0,\qquad 0<x<1,
$$
$$
y(0)=0,\qquad y(1)=1,
$$
where $f$ is smooth and $f(x)>0$ on $[0,1]$. Then describe how to approach the case where $f(x_0)=0$ and $f'(x_0)\neq0$ for some $x_0\in(0,1)$.
#proof
Let
$$
S(x)=\int_0^x\sqrt{f(s)}\,ds,\qquad S_1=S(1).
$$
For $f(x)>0$, the standard WKB ansatz gives two leading order solutions
$$
y_{\pm}(x)\sim f(x)^{-1/4}\exp\left(\pm\frac{S(x)}{\varepsilon}\right).
$$
To satisfy $y(0)=0$, take their odd combination relative to $x=0$:
$$
y(x)\sim C f(x)^{-1/4}\sinh\left(\frac{S(x)}{\varepsilon}\right).
$$
The boundary condition $y(1)=1$ gives
$$
C=\frac{f(1)^{1/4}}{\sinh(S_1/\varepsilon)}.
$$
Thus the leading order approximation is
$$
\boxed{
 y(x)\sim \left(\frac{f(1)}{f(x)}\right)^{1/4}
 \frac{\sinh\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\int_0^x\sqrt{f(s)}\,ds\right)}
 {\sinh\left(\varepsilon^{-1}\int_0^1\sqrt{f(s)}\,ds\right)}.}
$$
Equivalently, away from $x=0$ this is dominated by the exponentially growing branch normalized at $x=1$.

If $f(x_0)=0$ and $f'(x_0)\neq0$, then $x_0$ is a simple turning point. The WKB form fails there because the amplitude $f^{-1/4}$ becomes singular. One introduces a stretched Airy variable of size $\varepsilon^{2/3}$ near $x_0$, solves the local Airy equation, and matches the Airy asymptotics to WKB solutions on the two sides of the turning point.

::ProblemBlock{number=B1}
#problem
There is a one-parameter family of two-stage, second-order Runge--Kutta methods for $y'=f(x,y)$:
$$
d^{(1)}=hf(x_n,y_n),\qquad d^{(2)}=hf(x_n+\alpha h,y_n+\alpha d^{(1)}),
$$
$$
y_{n+1}=y_n+\left(1-\frac{1}{2\alpha}\right)d^{(1)}+\frac{1}{2\alpha}d^{(2)}.
$$
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Verify that these schemes, for all values of $\alpha$, are second order accurate.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Show that these schemes, for all values of $\alpha$, have exactly the same absolute stability domain.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Verify that the absolute stability domain, along the negative real axis, extends exactly over $[-2,0]$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Expanding $d^{(2)}$ about $(x_n,y_n)$ gives
$$
d^{(2)}=h\left[f+\alpha h(f_x+f_yf)\right]+O(h^3),
$$
where all derivatives are evaluated at $(x_n,y_n)$. Hence
$$
y_{n+1}=y_n+h f+\frac{h^2}{2}(f_x+f_yf)+O(h^3).
$$
The exact solution satisfies
$$
y(x_n+h)=y_n+h y'_n+\frac{h^2}{2}y''_n+O(h^3)
=y_n+h f+\frac{h^2}{2}(f_x+f_yf)+O(h^3).
$$
Thus the local truncation error is $O(h^3)$ and the method is second order accurate for every $\alpha\neq0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Apply the method to $y'=\lambda y$, $z=h\lambda$. Then
$$
d^{(1)}=z y_n,
\qquad
 d^{(2)}=z(1+\alpha z)y_n.
$$
Therefore
$$
y_{n+1}=R(z)y_n,
\qquad
R(z)=1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}.
$$
The stability function is independent of $\alpha$, so all schemes in the family have exactly the same absolute stability domain.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** The absolute stability domain is
$$
\mathcal{S}=\{z\in\mathbb{C}: |R(z)|\leq 1\}
=\left\{z\in\mathbb{C}:\left|1+z+\frac{z^2}{2}\right|\leq1\right\}.
$$
On the negative real axis write $z=-r$, $r\geq0$. Then
$$
R(-r)=1-r+\frac{r^2}{2}.
$$
The condition $|R(-r)|\leq1$ is
$$
-1\leq1-r+\frac{r^2}{2}\leq1.
$$
The right inequality gives $r(r/2-1)\leq0$, hence $0\leq r\leq2$. The left inequality is automatically true for real $r$ because $1-r+r^2/2=(r-1)^2/2+1/2>0$. Hence the interval is exactly
$$
\boxed{[-2,0].}
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=B2}
#problem
Consider the least squares minimization problem of finding a minimum-norm solution of
$$
J(x)=\left\lVert Bx-c\right\rVert_2,
$$
where $B$ is a real matrix and $c$ is given.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Express the minimum-norm least squares solution as a solution of a system $Ax=y$. Determine when the solution is unique, when multiple solutions exist, and whether no solution is possible.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Under the conditions that there is a unique solution, express the minimum-norm least squares solution as a function of the eigenvalues of the matrix $A$ from part (a).

#proof
Let the least squares problem be $\min_x\left\lVert Bx-c\right\rVert_2$, with $B\in\mathbb{R}^{m\times n}$ and $c\in\mathbb{R}^m$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The normal equations are
$$
B^T Bx=B^T c.
$$
Thus $A=B^TB$ and $y=B^Tc$. A least squares solution always exists because the normal equations are always consistent: $B^Tc$ lies in $\mathcal{R}(B^T)=\mathcal{R}(B^TB)$. The least squares solution is unique exactly when $B$ has full column rank, equivalently $B^TB$ is positive definite. If $B$ is rank deficient, there are multiple least squares solutions, but among them there is a unique one of smallest Euclidean norm, namely $B^\dagger c$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** In the unique case, $A=B^TB$ is symmetric positive definite. Write its spectral decomposition as
$$
A=Q\Lambda Q^T,\qquad \Lambda=\operatorname{diag}(\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_n),\quad \lambda_i>0.
$$
Then
$$
\boxed{x=A^{-1}y=Q\Lambda^{-1}Q^T y
=\sum_{i=1}^n\frac{q_i^T y}{\lambda_i}q_i.}
$$
If $A$ is only positive semidefinite, the minimum norm least squares solution is obtained by replacing $\lambda_i^{-1}$ by $0$ on the zero eigenspaces, that is,
$$
x=A^\dagger y=\sum_{\lambda_i>0}\frac{q_i^T y}{\lambda_i}q_i.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=B3}
#problem
Let $A$ be an $n\times n$ matrix.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** State the power method for computing the largest eigenvalue of $A$.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\1&10&0\\1&1&9.8\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Explain why the rate of convergence of the power method applied to $A$ is slow.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Choose a suitable shift $\sigma$ so that the shifted power method converges to the largest eigenvalue of $A$. Can this improve the rate of convergence? If so, why?

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The power method chooses a nonzero initial vector $x_0$ and repeats
$$
y_{k+1}=Ax_k,
\qquad
x_{k+1}=\frac{y_{k+1}}{\left\lVert y_{k+1}\right\rVert}.
$$
An eigenvalue estimate can be taken as the Rayleigh quotient
$$
\mu_k=\frac{x_k^T A x_k}{x_k^T x_k}.
$$
If $A$ has a dominant eigenvalue $|\lambda_1|>|\lambda_2|\geq\cdots$ and $x_0$ has a nonzero component in the dominant eigenvector direction, then $x_k$ converges in direction to the dominant eigenvector and $\mu_k\to\lambda_1$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** The matrix
$$
A=\begin{pmatrix}1&0&0\\1&10&0\\1&1&9.8\end{pmatrix}
$$
is triangular, so its eigenvalues are $1,10,9.8$. The largest eigenvalue is $10$, but the next largest magnitude is $9.8$. The asymptotic convergence factor of the power method is approximately
$$
\left|\frac{9.8}{10}\right|=0.98,
$$
which is close to $1$. Hence convergence is slow.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** For the shifted power method one applies the power method to $A-\sigma I$. The transformed eigenvalues are
$$
1-\sigma,\\quad 10-\sigma,\quad 9.8-\sigma.
$$
To converge to the eigenvector associated with the original eigenvalue $10$, choose $\sigma$ so that $|10-\sigma|$ is the largest transformed magnitude. A useful choice is
$$
\sigma=5.4,
$$
for which
$$
1-\sigma=-4.4,
\qquad
10-\sigma=4.6,
\qquad
9.8-\sigma=4.4.
$$
The convergence factor becomes approximately $4.4/4.6\approx0.9565$, which is better than $0.98$. Thus the shift can improve convergence, although the improvement here is modest because the eigenvalue $1$ constrains how far one can separate the transformed dominant eigenvalue from the others.

::ProblemBlock{number=C1}
#problem
Consider the variational problem
$$
\min_{x\in M} I(x)=\int_0^1 x(t)\sqrt{1+\dot x(t)^2}\,dt,
$$
with
$$
M=\{x\in C^1([0,1]):x(0)=1,\ x(1)=\cosh(1)\}.
$$
Show that the Euler--Lagrange equation admits a first integral, solve the resulting first-order ODE, verify that the minimizer is $x(t)=\cosh t$, and conclude that this function yields a weak minimum.
#proof
The functional is
$$
I(x)=\int_0^1 x(t)\sqrt{1+\dot x(t)^2}\,dt,
\qquad x(0)=1,
\quad x(1)=\cosh(1).
$$
The Lagrangian $L(x,v)=x\sqrt{1+v^2}$ is independent of $t$, so the Hamiltonian
$$
H=vL_v-L
$$
is conserved. Since
$$
L_v=\frac{xv}{\sqrt{1+v^2}},
$$
we get
$$
H=\frac{xv^2}{\sqrt{1+v^2}}-x\sqrt{1+v^2}
=-\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+v^2}}=-a,
$$
where $a>0$ is constant. Thus
$$
\frac{x}{\sqrt{1+\dot x^2}}=a,
\qquad
\dot x^2=\frac{x^2}{a^2}-1.
$$
The solutions are catenaries
$$
x(t)=a\cosh\left(\frac{t-c}{a}\right).
$$
The boundary conditions are satisfied by
$$
\boxed{x(t)=\cosh t.}
$$
Indeed $x(0)=1$ and $x(1)=\cosh(1)$.

To verify that this extremal gives a weak minimum, note that along this curve $x(t)>0$ and
$$
L_{vv}(x,v)=\frac{x}{(1+v^2)^{3/2}}>0.
$$
Thus the strengthened Legendre condition holds. Since the catenary found above has no conjugate point on this short interval and satisfies the Euler--Lagrange equation with fixed endpoints, the standard sufficient condition for a weak local minimum applies. Hence $x(t)=\cosh t$ yields a weak minimum.

::ProblemBlock{number=C2}
#problem
Dirichlet's principle was historically stated as follows: if $I(\cdot)$ is bounded below, then there exists a minimizer $u$ such that $I(u)=\inf_{v\in M}I(v)$. Riemann's proposed proof chooses a minimizing sequence $\{u_k\}\subset M$, claims that since it is bounded it has a convergent subsequence $u_{k_j}\to u_0$, and concludes that $u_0$ is the desired minimizer. Find the flaw in this proof and present sufficient conditions for the existence of a minimizer.
#proof
The flaw is the assertion that a bounded minimizing sequence has a convergent subsequence. In finite-dimensional Euclidean space this is true by Bolzano--Weierstrass, but in infinite-dimensional function spaces bounded sets are generally not compact in the norm topology. Even if a subsequence converges weakly, one must also know that the admissible class is weakly closed and that the functional is weakly lower semicontinuous.

A standard direct-method existence theorem uses the following sufficient conditions:
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** The admissible set $M$ is nonempty and weakly sequentially closed in a reflexive Banach space.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** The functional $I$ is coercive on $M$, so every minimizing sequence is bounded.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(iii)** The functional $I$ is weakly sequentially lower semicontinuous.

Then a minimizing sequence has a weakly convergent subsequence $u_{k_j}\rightharpoonup u_0$, the closedness of $M$ gives $u_0\in M$, and lower semicontinuity gives
$$
I(u_0)\leq\liminf_{j\to\infty}I(u_{k_j})=\inf_{u\in M}I(u).
$$
Therefore $u_0$ is a minimizer.

::ProblemBlock{number=C3}
#problem
Consider the Lagrangian
$$
L(x,v)=\frac12 e^{2x}v^2.
$$
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Write out the Euler--Lagrange equation for fixed endpoint curves.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Derive the Hamiltonian $H(x,p)=\sup_v\{pv-L(x,v)\}$ and write out the Hamiltonian system.
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** Derive the Hamilton--Jacobi equation $u_t+H(x,\nabla u)=0$ and solve it.

#proof
Here
$$
L(x,v)=\frac12 e^{2x}v^2.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** We compute
$$
L_v=e^{2x}v,
\qquad
L_x=e^{2x}v^2.
$$
The Euler--Lagrange equation is
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{2x}\dot x\right)-e^{2x}\dot x^2=0.
$$
Since
$$
\frac{d}{dt}\left(e^{2x}\dot x\right)=e^{2x}\ddot x+2e^{2x}\dot x^2,
$$
we obtain
$$
\boxed{\ddot x+\dot x^2=0.}
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** The momentum is
$$
p=L_v=e^{2x}v,
\qquad
v=e^{-2x}p.
$$
Therefore
$$
H(x,p)=pv-L(x,v)=e^{-2x}p^2-\frac12 e^{-2x}p^2
=\boxed{\frac12 e^{-2x}p^2.}
$$
The Hamiltonian system is
$$
\boxed{\dot q=H_p=e^{-2q}p,\qquad \dot p=-H_q=e^{-2q}p^2.}
$$
Since $H$ is conserved, $e^{-q}p=\alpha$ is constant up to sign. Then
$$
\dot q=e^{-2q}p=\alpha e^{-q}.
$$
Thus
$$
\frac{d}{dt}e^q=e^q\dot q=\alpha,
$$
so
$$
\boxed{e^{q(t)}=\alpha t+\beta,
\qquad q(t)=\log(\alpha t+\beta),
\qquad p(t)=\alpha(\alpha t+\beta).}
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** The Hamilton--Jacobi equation is
$$
\boxed{u_t+\frac12 e^{-2x}u_x^2=0.}
$$
A separated complete integral is obtained by taking
$$
u(t,x;\alpha)=\alpha e^x-\frac12\alpha^2 t,
$$
where $\alpha$ is an arbitrary constant. Indeed,
$$
u_t=-\frac12\alpha^2,
\qquad
u_x=\alpha e^x,
$$
and hence
$$
u_t+\frac12 e^{-2x}u_x^2
=-\frac12\alpha^2+\frac12 e^{-2x}\alpha^2e^{2x}=0.
$$
The sign of $\alpha$ covers both separated branches.
