::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)**  State the Axiom of Completeness for $\mathbb{R}$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**  Prove that a monotone increasing sequence of real numbers converges if and only if it is bounded above.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The Axiom of Completeness says that every nonempty subset $S\subset \mathbb{R}$ that is bounded above has a least upper bound in $\mathbb{R}$. That is, there exists $\sup S\in \mathbb{R}$ such that
$$
s\le \sup S \quad \text{for all } s\in S,
$$
and if $u$ is any upper bound for $S$, then
$$
\sup S\le u.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $\{a_n\}$ be monotone increasing.

First suppose that $a_n\to L$. Then every convergent sequence is bounded. In particular, there exists $N$ such that
$$
|a_n-L|<1
$$
for all $n\ge N$. Hence for $n\ge N$,
$$
a_n<L+1.
$$
The finitely many terms $a_1,\dots,a_{N-1}$ are also bounded above. Thus the whole sequence is bounded above.

Conversely, suppose $\{a_n\}$ is bounded above. Let
$$
S=\{a_n:n\in \mathbb{N}\}.
$$
Then $S$ is nonempty and bounded above, so by completeness it has a supremum. Let
$$
L=\sup S.
$$
We claim that $a_n\to L$.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $L$ is the least upper bound, $L-\varepsilon$ is not an upper bound for $S$. Therefore there exists $N$ such that
$$
a_N>L-\varepsilon.
$$
Since the sequence is increasing, for all $n\ge N$,
$$
a_n\ge a_N>L-\varepsilon.
$$
Also, since $L$ is an upper bound for $S$,
$$
a_n\le L
$$
for all $n$. Hence for all $n\ge N$,
$$
L-\varepsilon<a_n\le L,
$$
so
$$
|a_n-L|<\varepsilon.
$$
Thus $a_n\to L$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $\{f_n(x)\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a sequence of continuous functions defined on $[a,b]$. Suppose
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(x)
$$
converges pointwise on $[a,b)$, but
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(b)
$$
diverges. Show that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(x)
$$
does not converge uniformly on $[a,b)$.

#proof
Assume, for contradiction, that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(x)
$$
converges uniformly on $[a,b)$.

Then the partial sums are uniformly Cauchy on $[a,b)$. Thus for every $\varepsilon>0$, there exists $N$ such that for all $m>n\ge N$,
$$
\sup_{x\in [a,b)}\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m} f_k(x)\right|<\varepsilon.
$$
Therefore, for all $x\in [a,b)$,
$$
\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m} f_k(x)\right|<\varepsilon.
$$

For fixed $m$ and $n$, the function
$$
x\mapsto \sum_{k=n+1}^{m} f_k(x)
$$
is continuous on $[a,b]$, because it is a finite sum of continuous functions. Taking the limit as $x\to b^-$ gives
$$
\left|\sum_{k=n+1}^{m} f_k(b)\right|\le \varepsilon.
$$
Thus the numerical series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(b)
$$
is Cauchy, hence converges. This contradicts the hypothesis that it diverges.

Therefore
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} f_n(x)
$$
does not converge uniformly on $[a,b)$.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be a bounded sequence of real numbers. Suppose that
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} (a_{2n}+2a_n)=0.
$$
Prove that
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty} a_n=0.
$$

#proof
Let
$$
r_n=a_{2n}+2a_n.
$$
By hypothesis,
$$
r_n\to 0.
$$
Since $\{a_n\}$ is bounded, there exists $M>0$ such that
$$
|a_n|\le M
$$
for all $n$.

From
$$
r_n=a_{2n}+2a_n,
$$
we have
$$
a_n=\frac{r_n-a_{2n}}{2}.
$$
Iterating this identity gives, for any integer $K\ge 1$,
$$
a_n=\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\left(-\frac12\right)^j\frac{r_{2^j n}}{2}+\left(-\frac12\right)^K a_{2^K n}.
$$
Hence
$$
|a_n|\le \sum_{j=0}^{K-1} \frac{|r_{2^j n}|}{2^{j+1}}+\frac{M}{2^K}.
$$

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Choose $K$ so large that
$$
\frac{M}{2^K}<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
Since $r_n\to 0$, there exists $N$ such that
$$
|r_m|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}
$$
for all $m\ge N$. Then for all $n\ge N$, every $2^j n\ge N$, so
$$
|a_n|\le \sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{\varepsilon/2}{2^{j+1}}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}.
$$
Since
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{K-1}\frac{1}{2^{j+1}}<1,
$$
we get
$$
|a_n|<\frac{\varepsilon}{2}+\frac{\varepsilon}{2}=\varepsilon.
$$
Therefore $a_n\to 0$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Suppose a sequence of real-valued functions $\{f_n\}$, where $f_n:\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$, is given such that for all $n\in \mathbb{N}$ we have $f_n(0)=0$, and for any $x,y\in \mathbb{R}$,
$$
|f_n(x)-f_n(y)|\le \max\{|x|,|y|\}\,|x-y|.
$$
Prove that the sequence $\{f_n\}$ has a subsequence that converges pointwise on $\mathbb{R}$.

#proof
First we prove local uniform boundedness and local equicontinuity.

Fix $R>0$ and suppose $x,y\in [-R,R]$. Then
$$
|f_n(x)-f_n(y)|\le R|x-y|.
$$
Thus the family $\{f_n\}$ is equicontinuous on $[-R,R]$.

Also, since $f_n(0)=0$, taking $y=0$ gives
$$
|f_n(x)|=|f_n(x)-f_n(0)|\le \max\{|x|,0\}|x-0|=|x|^2.
$$
Therefore, for $x\in [-R,R]$,
$$
|f_n(x)|\le R^2.
$$
So $\{f_n\}$ is uniformly bounded on $[-R,R]$.

By the Arzela-Ascoli theorem, from $\{f_n\}$ we can extract a subsequence that converges uniformly on $[-1,1]$. From that subsequence, extract a further subsequence that converges uniformly on $[-2,2]$. Continuing inductively, for each $m\in \mathbb{N}$ we obtain a subsequence that converges uniformly on $[-m,m]$.

Now take the diagonal subsequence. More explicitly, if the $m$-th subsequence is denoted by
$$
\{f_{n_k^{(m)}}\}_{k=1}^{\infty},
$$
with each subsequence taken from the previous one, define
$$
g_k=f_{n_k^{(k)}}.
$$
Then for each fixed $m$, all but finitely many terms of $\{g_k\}$ lie in the $m$-th subsequence, so $\{g_k\}$ converges uniformly on $[-m,m]$.

Since every real number belongs to some interval $[-m,m]$, it follows that $\{g_k\}$ converges pointwise on $\mathbb{R}$. Thus $\{f_n\}$ has a pointwise convergent subsequence on $\mathbb{R}$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Suppose $\{a_n\}$ and $\{b_n\}$ are monotone strictly decreasing sequences of positive real numbers converging to zero. Prove that if there exists a limit
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_{n+1}-a_n}{b_{n+1}-b_n}=A,
$$
then
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{a_n}{b_n}=A.
$$

#proof
Since $\{a_n\}$ and $\{b_n\}$ are strictly decreasing and converge to zero, define
$$
\alpha_n=a_n-a_{n+1}>0,
\qquad
\beta_n=b_n-b_{n+1}>0.
$$
Then
$$
\frac{a_{n+1}-a_n}{b_{n+1}-b_n}
=
\frac{-(a_n-a_{n+1})}{-(b_n-b_{n+1})}
=
\frac{\alpha_n}{\beta_n}.
$$
Thus
$$
\frac{\alpha_n}{\beta_n}\to A.
$$

Because $a_n\to 0$ and $b_n\to 0$, we can write the tails as
$$
a_n=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty} (a_k-a_{k+1})=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\alpha_k
$$
and
$$
b_n=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty} (b_k-b_{k+1})=\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{a_n}{b_n}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\alpha_k}{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k}
=
\frac{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k(\alpha_k/\beta_k)}{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k}.
$$

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $\alpha_k/\beta_k\to A$, there exists $N$ such that for all $k\ge N$,
$$
\left|\frac{\alpha_k}{\beta_k}-A\right|<\varepsilon.
$$
Then for $n\ge N$,
$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{b_n}-A\right|
=
\left|\frac{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k((\alpha_k/\beta_k)-A)}{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k}\right|.
$$
Since all $\beta_k>0$, we get
$$
\left|\frac{a_n}{b_n}-A\right|
\le
\frac{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k\varepsilon}{\sum_{k=n}^{\infty}\beta_k}
=\varepsilon.
$$
Thus
$$
\frac{a_n}{b_n}\to A.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Denote
$$
U\subset \mathbb{R}^2,
\qquad
U=\{(x,y):y>|x|\}.
$$
Is it true that the map
$$
f(x,y)=\left(\int_x^y e^{t^2}\,dt,\int_x^y e^{-t^2}\,dt\right)
$$
is a local diffeomorphism near every point $(x,y)\in U$? Explain your answer.

#proof
Yes. We compute the Jacobian determinant.

Write
$$
f(x,y)=(F_1(x,y),F_2(x,y)),
$$
where
$$
F_1(x,y)=\int_x^y e^{t^2}\,dt,
\qquad
F_2(x,y)=\int_x^y e^{-t^2}\,dt.
$$
By the fundamental theorem of calculus,
$$
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial x}=-e^{x^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial F_1}{\partial y}=e^{y^2},
$$
and
$$
\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial x}=-e^{-x^2},
\qquad
\frac{\partial F_2}{\partial y}=e^{-y^2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
Df(x,y)=
\begin{pmatrix}
-e^{x^2} & e^{y^2}\\
-e^{-x^2} & e^{-y^2}
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Its determinant is
$$
\det Df(x,y)
=
-e^{x^2-y^2}+e^{y^2-x^2}
=
e^{y^2-x^2}-e^{x^2-y^2}.
$$

If $(x,y)\in U$, then $y>|x|$. Hence $y>0$ and
$$
y^2>x^2.
$$
Thus
$$
e^{y^2-x^2}>1
\qquad \text{and} \qquad
0<e^{x^2-y^2}<1.
$$
So
$$
\det Df(x,y)>0.
$$
In particular, the Jacobian determinant is nonzero at every point of $U$.

By the inverse function theorem, $f$ is a local diffeomorphism near every point $(x,y)\in U$.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let
$$
\Sigma=\left\{(x,y,z)\in \mathbb{R}^3:\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}=1\right\}.
$$
Compute the integral
$$
\int_{\Sigma} x^3\,dy\,dz+y^3\,dz\,dx+z^3\,dx\,dy.
$$

#proof
Assume that $\Sigma$ is oriented by the outward normal. The given integral is the flux integral of the vector field
$$
F(x,y,z)=(x^3,y^3,z^3).
$$
Indeed,
$$
F\cdot n\,dS=x^3\,dy\,dz+y^3\,dz\,dx+z^3\,dx\,dy.
$$

Let $E$ be the solid ellipsoid
$$
E=\left\{(x,y,z):\frac{x^2}{a^2}+\frac{y^2}{b^2}+\frac{z^2}{c^2}\le 1\right\}.
$$
By the divergence theorem,
$$
\int_{\Sigma} x^3\,dy\,dz+y^3\,dz\,dx+z^3\,dx\,dy
=
\iiint_E \operatorname{div}F\,dV.
$$
Now
$$
\operatorname{div}F=3x^2+3y^2+3z^2.
$$
Hence
$$
\iiint_E \operatorname{div}F\,dV
=3\iiint_E (x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dV.
$$

Use the change of variables
$$
x=au,
\qquad
y=bv,
\qquad
z=cw.
$$
Then $E$ maps to the unit ball
$$
B=\{(u,v,w):u^2+v^2+w^2\le 1\},
$$
and the Jacobian is
$$
abc.
$$
Therefore
$$
\iiint_E x^2\,dV
=abc\,a^2\iiint_B u^2\,du\,dv\,dw.
$$
By symmetry,
$$
\iiint_B u^2\,du\,dv\,dw
=
\iiint_B v^2\,du\,dv\,dw
=
\iiint_B w^2\,du\,dv\,dw.
$$
Also,
$$
\iiint_B (u^2+v^2+w^2)\,du\,dv\,dw
=
\int_0^1 r^2\cdot 4\pi r^2\,dr
=4\pi\int_0^1 r^4\,dr
=\frac{4\pi}{5}.
$$
Thus
$$
\iiint_B u^2\,du\,dv\,dw=\frac{1}{3}\cdot \frac{4\pi}{5}=\frac{4\pi}{15}.
$$
Similarly,
$$
\iiint_E y^2\,dV=abc\,b^2\frac{4\pi}{15},
\qquad
\iiint_E z^2\,dV=abc\,c^2\frac{4\pi}{15}.
$$
Therefore
$$
3\iiint_E (x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dV
=3abc\frac{4\pi}{15}(a^2+b^2+c^2).
$$
Hence
$$
\int_{\Sigma} x^3\,dy\,dz+y^3\,dz\,dx+z^3\,dx\,dy
=
\frac{4\pi abc}{5}(a^2+b^2+c^2).
$$
If the opposite orientation is used, the answer has the opposite sign.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Prove or disprove that
$$
f(x,y)=\ln(1+x^2+y^2)+\sin(x^2)
$$
is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

#proof
The statement is false. We disprove uniform continuity by constructing two sequences of points whose distances go to zero while the corresponding function values stay separated.

Let
$$
p_n=(x_n,0),
\qquad
q_n=(z_n,0),
$$
where
$$
x_n=\sqrt{2\pi n+\frac{\pi}{2}},
\qquad
z_n=\sqrt{2\pi n+\frac{3\pi}{2}}.
$$
Then
$$
|p_n-q_n|=|z_n-x_n|
=
\frac{z_n^2-x_n^2}{z_n+x_n}
=
\frac{\pi}{z_n+x_n}\to 0.
$$

Now compute the difference of the function values. Since
$$
\sin(x_n^2)=\sin\left(2\pi n+\frac{\pi}{2}\right)=1
$$
and
$$
\sin(z_n^2)=\sin\left(2\pi n+\frac{3\pi}{2}\right)=-1,
$$
we have
$$
f(q_n)-f(p_n)
=
\ln(1+z_n^2)-\ln(1+x_n^2)-2.
$$
But
$$
\ln(1+z_n^2)-\ln(1+x_n^2)
=
\ln\left(\frac{1+2\pi n+3\pi/2}{1+2\pi n+\pi/2}\right)\to 0.
$$
Therefore
$$
f(q_n)-f(p_n)\to -2.
$$
In particular,
$$
|f(q_n)-f(p_n)|\to 2,
$$
while
$$
|p_n-q_n|\to 0.
$$
This contradicts uniform continuity. Hence $f$ is not uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $(M,d)$ be a bounded metric space that has the following property: for any sequence $\{x_n\}\subset M$ with
$$
d(x_n,x_{n+1})\le \frac{1}{n},
$$
there exists a convergent subsequence. Does it imply that $(M,d)$ is compact? Prove or give a counterexample.

#proof
No. We give a counterexample.

Let $M$ be any infinite set, and define the discrete metric
$$
d(x,y)=
\begin{cases}
0, & x=y,\\
1, & x\ne y.
\end{cases}
$$
Then $(M,d)$ is bounded, since
$$
d(x,y)\le 1
$$
for all $x,y\in M$.

We check the stated property. Suppose $\{x_n\}\subset M$ satisfies
$$
d(x_n,x_{n+1})\le \frac{1}{n}
$$
for all $n$. For every $n\ge 2$,
$$
\frac{1}{n}<1.
$$
Since in the discrete metric the distance between two distinct points is exactly $1$, the inequality
$$
d(x_n,x_{n+1})\le \frac{1}{n}<1
$$
forces
$$
x_n=x_{n+1}
$$
for every $n\ge 2$. Thus the sequence is eventually constant. Therefore it has a convergent subsequence.

So $(M,d)$ satisfies the given property.

However, $(M,d)$ is not compact. Since $M$ is infinite, choose a sequence $\{y_n\}$ of distinct points in $M$. In the discrete metric, no subsequence of $\{y_n\}$ can converge, because a convergent sequence in the discrete metric must eventually be constant. Hence $(M,d)$ is not sequentially compact, and therefore not compact.

Thus the stated property does not imply compactness.
::
