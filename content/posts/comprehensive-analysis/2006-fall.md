::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Analyze the convergence behavior of each of the following infinite series. This means: determine whether the series converges or diverges; if it converges, determine whether it converges absolutely or conditionally; if it diverges, determine whether or not the sequence of terms converges to $0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
$$
\frac12-\frac13+\frac1{2^2}-\frac1{3^2}+\frac1{2^3}-\frac1{3^3}+\cdots+\frac1{2^n}-\frac1{3^n}+\cdots
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
$$
\frac{1}{2(\log 2)^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{3(\log 3)^{3/2}}+\frac{1}{4(\log 4)^{3/2}}+\cdots+\frac{1}{n(\log n)^{3/2}}+\cdots
$$

Justify your answers.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
The series can be written as
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^n}-\frac{1}{3^n}\right).
$$
Its absolute series is bounded by
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^n}+\frac{1}{3^n}\right).
$$
Both
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{2^n}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{3^n}
$$
are convergent geometric series. Hence the given series is absolutely convergent.

In fact,
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(\frac{1}{2^n}-\frac{1}{3^n}\right)
=
1-\frac12
=
\frac12,
$$
but the main conclusion is that the series converges absolutely.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
The series is positive, so ordinary convergence and absolute convergence are the same. Consider
$$
\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n(\log n)^{3/2}}.
$$
Use the integral test with
$$
f(x)=\frac{1}{x(\log x)^{3/2}}, \qquad x\ge 2.
$$
The function $f$ is positive and decreasing for $x\ge 2$. Moreover,
$$
\int_2^{\infty}\frac{dx}{x(\log x)^{3/2}}
=
\int_{\log 2}^{\infty}u^{-3/2}\,du
=
\left[-2u^{-1/2}\right]_{\log 2}^{\infty}
=
\frac{2}{\sqrt{\log 2}}
<
\infty.
$$
Therefore the series converges. Since all terms are positive, it converges absolutely.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Throughout this problem let $f:\mathbb R^n\to\mathbb R$ be a function with continuous first partial derivatives at each point of $\mathbb R^n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Prove that $f$ is continuous at each point of $\mathbb R^n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
Suppose that $f$ has exactly one critical point, namely at a certain point $x_0\in\mathbb R^n$. Suppose further that
$$
\lim_{\lVert x\rVert\to+\infty}f(x)=+\infty.
$$
Prove or disprove: the function $f$ takes on its global minimum at the point $x_0$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Fix $a\in\mathbb R^n$. Since the first partial derivatives of $f$ are continuous at each point, they are bounded on some small closed box
$$
Q=\{a+h: |h_j|\le r,\ j=1,\dots,n\}
$$
around $a$. Thus there is a constant $M>0$ such that
$$
\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_j}(x)\right|\le M
$$
for all $x\in Q$ and all $j=1,\dots,n$.

Let $h=(h_1,\dots,h_n)$ be small enough so that $a+h\in Q$. Move from $a$ to $a+h$ one coordinate at a time. By the one-variable mean value theorem applied in each coordinate direction,
$$
|f(a+h)-f(a)|
\le
M\sum_{j=1}^n |h_j|.
$$
As $h\to 0$, the right-hand side tends to $0$. Hence
$$
\lim_{h\to 0}f(a+h)=f(a),
$$
so $f$ is continuous at $a$. Since $a$ was arbitrary, $f$ is continuous on $\mathbb R^n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
The statement is true.

Because
$$
\lim_{\lVert x\rVert\to+\infty}f(x)=+\infty,
$$
we may choose $R>0$ so large that $x_0\in B_R(0)$ and
$$
f(x)>f(x_0)
$$
whenever $\lVert x\rVert\ge R$.

The closed ball $\overline{B_R(0)}$ is compact, and by part **(a)**, $f$ is continuous. Therefore $f$ attains a minimum on $\overline{B_R(0)}$ at some point $z\in\overline{B_R(0)}$.

The point $z$ cannot lie on the boundary $\lVert z\rVert=R$, because on that boundary we have
$$
f(z)>f(x_0),
$$
while $x_0$ lies inside the ball. Hence $z$ is an interior point of $\overline{B_R(0)}$. Since $f$ has a local minimum at $z$ and is differentiable, we must have
$$
\nabla f(z)=0.
$$
Thus $z$ is a critical point. By assumption, the only critical point is $x_0$. Therefore
$$
z=x_0.
$$
So $f$ attains its global minimum at $x_0$.

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Prove or disprove: if $f:[a,b]\to\mathbb R$ is a continuous real-valued function such that
$$
\int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx=0,
$$
then $f(x)=0$ for all $x\in[a,b]$.

#proof
The statement is true.

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that there exists $c\in[a,b]$ such that
$$
f(c)\ne 0.
$$
Then
$$
|f(c)|>0.
$$
Since $f$ is continuous, $|f|$ is continuous. Therefore there exists an interval $I\subset [a,b]$ containing $c$ and having positive length such that
$$
|f(x)|\ge \frac{|f(c)|}{2}
$$
for all $x\in I$.

Hence
$$
\int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx
\ge
\int_I |f(x)|\,dx
\ge
\frac{|f(c)|}{2}|I|
>
0,
$$
which contradicts the assumption that
$$
\int_a^b |f(x)|\,dx=0.
$$
Therefore no such $c$ exists, and so
$$
f(x)=0
$$
for all $x\in[a,b]$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $f$ be a real-valued function with domain $\mathbb R$, and let $A$ and $y$ be elements of $\mathbb R$. Prove that if
$$
\limsup_{x\to y} f(x)=A,
$$
then there is a sequence $(x_n)$ in $\mathbb R$ such that
$$
y=\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n
$$
and
$$
A=\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_n).
$$

#proof
We use the standard definition
$$
\limsup_{x\to y} f(x)
=
\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}\sup\{f(x):0<|x-y|<\delta\}.
$$
For $\delta>0$, define
$$
M(\delta)=\sup\{f(x):0<|x-y|<\delta\}.
$$
Then $M(\delta)$ decreases as $\delta\downarrow 0$, and
$$
\lim_{\delta\downarrow 0}M(\delta)=A.
$$

For each $n$, choose $\delta_n>0$ such that
$$
0<\delta_n<\frac1n
$$
and
$$
M(\delta_n)<A+\frac1n.
$$
By the definition of supremum, there exists $x_n$ satisfying
$$
0<|x_n-y|<\delta_n
$$
and
$$
f(x_n)>M(\delta_n)-\frac1n.
$$
Since $M(\delta_n)\ge A$, we get
$$
f(x_n)>A-\frac1n.
$$
Also, because $x_n$ lies in the punctured $\delta_n$-neighborhood of $y$,
$$
f(x_n)\le M(\delta_n)<A+\frac1n.
$$
Thus
$$
A-\frac1n<f(x_n)<A+\frac1n.
$$
Therefore
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f(x_n)=A.
$$
Also,
$$
|x_n-y|<\delta_n<\frac1n,
$$
so
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=y.
$$
This proves the claim.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Suppose that $(a_n)$ is an infinite sequence of positive real numbers, and for each positive integer $n$ let $p_n$ denote the finite product
$$
p_n=a_1a_2\cdots a_n.
$$
The infinite product
$$
a_1a_2\cdots a_n\cdots
$$
is said to be convergent if $(p_n)$ converges to a nonzero finite value $p$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Show that the infinite product
$$
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right)
$$
is convergent.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
Estimate the value of the infinite product in part **(a)** correct to one decimal place. Justify your estimate.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Let
$$
p_N=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$
Since every factor is greater than $1$, the sequence $(p_N)$ is increasing. We prove it is bounded above.

Taking logarithms,
$$
\log p_N
=
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\log\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$
For $t\ge 0$,
$$
\log(1+t)\le t.
$$
Therefore
$$
\log p_N
\le
\sum_{n=1}^{N}\frac1{n^2}
\le
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}
<
\infty.
$$
Thus $(\log p_N)$ is increasing and bounded above, so it converges to some finite number $L$. Hence
$$
p_N=e^{\log p_N}\to e^L.
$$
Since $L\ge 0$, the limit $e^L$ is positive and finite. Therefore
$$
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right)
$$
converges.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
A convenient exact evaluation comes from Euler's product formula
$$
\frac{\sinh \pi}{\pi}
=
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$
Thus
$$
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right)
=
\frac{\sinh \pi}{\pi}
\approx
3.6760779.
$$
Therefore the value correct to one decimal place is
$$
3.7.
$$

One can also justify the decimal estimate directly from partial products. For example, if
$$
P_N=\prod_{n=1}^{N}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right),
$$
then
$$
1
\le
\frac{\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right)}{P_N}
\le
\exp\left(\sum_{n=N+1}^{\infty}\frac1{n^2}\right)
\le
e^{1/N}.
$$
With $N=200$, direct computation gives
$$
P_{200}\approx 3.657789,
$$
and hence
$$
3.657789
<
\prod_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(1+\frac1{n^2}\right)
<
3.657789e^{1/200}
<
3.677.
$$
This also gives the one-decimal estimate $3.7$.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Prove, from the definition of Riemann integrability, that if $f$ and $g$ are Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, then $fg$ is also Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$.

#proof
Since $f$ and $g$ are Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$, they are bounded. Choose constants $M_f,M_g\ge 0$ such that
$$
|f(x)|\le M_f,
\qquad
|g(x)|\le M_g
$$
for all $x\in[a,b]$.

For a subinterval $I\subset[a,b]$, let
$$
\omega(f,I)=\sup_I f-\inf_I f
$$
denote the oscillation of $f$ on $I$. If $x,y\in I$, then
$$
|f(x)g(x)-f(y)g(y)|
\le
|f(x)|\,|g(x)-g(y)|+|g(y)|\,|f(x)-f(y)|.
$$
Therefore
$$
|f(x)g(x)-f(y)g(y)|
\le
M_f\omega(g,I)+M_g\omega(f,I).
$$
Taking the supremum over $x,y\in I$, we get
$$
\omega(fg,I)
\le
M_f\omega(g,I)+M_g\omega(f,I).
$$

Let $\varepsilon>0$. Since $f$ is Riemann integrable, there exists a partition $P_f$ such that
$$
\sum_{I\in P_f}\omega(f,I)|I|
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_g+1)}.
$$
Since $g$ is Riemann integrable, there exists a partition $P_g$ such that
$$
\sum_{I\in P_g}\omega(g,I)|I|
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_f+1)}.
$$
Let $P$ be a common refinement of $P_f$ and $P_g$. Refining a partition does not increase the corresponding upper-minus-lower sum, so
$$
\sum_{I\in P}\omega(f,I)|I|
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_g+1)}
$$
and
$$
\sum_{I\in P}\omega(g,I)|I|
<
\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_f+1)}.
$$
Using the oscillation estimate,
$$
\begin{aligned}
U(fg,P)-L(fg,P)
&=
\sum_{I\in P}\omega(fg,I)|I| \\
&\le
M_f\sum_{I\in P}\omega(g,I)|I|
+
M_g\sum_{I\in P}\omega(f,I)|I| \\
&<
M_f\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_f+1)}
+
M_g\frac{\varepsilon}{2(M_g+1)} \\
&<
\varepsilon.
\end{aligned}
$$
Thus, for every $\varepsilon>0$, there is a partition $P$ such that
$$
U(fg,P)-L(fg,P)<\varepsilon.
$$
By the Riemann criterion for integrability, $fg$ is Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Consider the sequence
$$
(x_0,x_1,x_2,\dots)
$$
defined recursively by
$$
x_0=1,
$$
and
$$
x_{n+1}=\tanh(x_n), \qquad n=0,1,2,\dots.
$$
Determine the convergence properties of this sequence. That is, determine whether it is convergent; if it is convergent, determine its limit; if it is not convergent, determine how it diverges. Justify your answer.

Recall that
$$
\tanh x=\frac{\sinh x}{\cosh x}=\frac{e^x-e^{-x}}{e^x+e^{-x}}.
$$

#proof
We first note that
$$
x_0=1>0.
$$
For $x>0$, we have
$$
0<\tanh x< x.
$$
Indeed, $\tanh x>0$ for $x>0$, and the inequality $\tanh x<x$ follows from the fact that
$$
\phi(x)=x-\tanh x
$$
satisfies
$$
\phi(0)=0
$$
and
$$
\phi'(x)=1-\operatorname{sech}^2 x=\tanh^2 x>0
$$
for $x>0$.

Therefore, if $x_n>0$, then
$$
0<x_{n+1}<x_n.
$$
By induction, the sequence $(x_n)$ is positive and strictly decreasing. Hence it is bounded below by $0$, so it converges. Let
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}x_n=L.
$$
Then $L\ge 0$. Passing to the limit in
$$
x_{n+1}=\tanh(x_n)
$$
and using continuity of $\tanh$, we obtain
$$
L=\tanh L.
$$
For $L>0$, we have $\tanh L<L$, so the equality $L=\tanh L$ is impossible. Thus
$$
L=0.
$$
Therefore the sequence converges monotonically decreasing to $0$.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Consider the sequence of functions $f_n:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, $n=1,2,3,\dots$, given by
$$
f_n(x)=\frac{n}{n+x^2}, \qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Show that this sequence converges pointwise on $\mathbb R$ to some limit function $g:\mathbb R\to\mathbb R$, and determine the formula for $g$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
Determine whether the sequence converges uniformly to the function $g$ obtained in part **(a)**. Justify your answer.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
Fix $x\in\mathbb R$. Then
$$
f_n(x)=\frac{n}{n+x^2}
=
\frac{1}{1+x^2/n}.
$$
As $n\to\infty$,
$$
\frac{x^2}{n}\to 0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x)=1.
$$
Thus the pointwise limit function is
$$
g(x)=1,
\qquad x\in\mathbb R.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
We check whether
$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|f_n(x)-1|
$$
tends to $0$. Since
$$
|f_n(x)-1|
=
1-\frac{n}{n+x^2}
=
\frac{x^2}{n+x^2},
$$
we have
$$
\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}|f_n(x)-1|
=
\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\frac{x^2}{n+x^2}
=
1.
$$
The supremum is not attained at a finite $x$, but it is approached as $|x|\to\infty$.

Since the supremum is $1$ for every $n$, it does not tend to $0$. Therefore $f_n$ does not converge uniformly to $g$ on $\mathbb R$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $f:B_1\to(0,\infty)$ be a continuous function, with values in the positive real numbers, defined on the standard closed unit ball $B_1$ in $\mathbb R^n$.

Determine the convergence properties of the infinite series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nf(x)}.
$$
That is, determine whether the series converges pointwise on $B_1$; and if it does, determine whether the convergence is uniform, and compute the formula for the function to which the series converges.

Justify your answer.

#proof
Because $B_1$ is compact and $f$ is continuous, $f$ attains its minimum on $B_1$. Since $f$ takes values in $(0,\infty)$, this minimum is positive. Thus there exists $m>0$ such that
$$
f(x)\ge m
$$
for all $x\in B_1$.

For each fixed $x\in B_1$, the series is geometric:
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nf(x)}
=
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\left(e^{-f(x)}\right)^n.
$$
Since $f(x)>0$, we have
$$
0<e^{-f(x)}<1.
$$
Therefore the pointwise sum is
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nf(x)}
=
\frac{e^{-f(x)}}{1-e^{-f(x)}}
=
\frac{1}{e^{f(x)}-1}.
$$

Now we prove uniform convergence. Since $f(x)\ge m$,
$$
0<e^{-nf(x)}\le e^{-nm}
$$
for all $x\in B_1$ and all $n$. The numerical series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nm}
$$
is a convergent geometric series. Hence, by the Weierstrass $M$-test,
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-nf(x)}
$$
converges uniformly on $B_1$.

Thus the series converges pointwise and uniformly on $B_1$, and its sum is
$$
\frac{1}{e^{f(x)}-1}.
$$
