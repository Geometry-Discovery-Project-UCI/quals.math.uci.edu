#problem
Show that for every $N\in \mathbb{N}$,
$$
\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{1}{k}\ge \log(N+1).
$$

#proof
For each $k=1,\dots,N$ and each $x\in [k,k+1]$, we have
$$
x\ge k,
$$
so
$$
\frac{1}{x}\le \frac{1}{k}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_k^{k+1}\frac{1}{x}\,dx\le \frac{1}{k}.
$$
Summing from $k=1$ to $N$, we obtain
$$
\log(N+1)
=
\int_1^{N+1}\frac{1}{x}\,dx
=
\sum_{k=1}^N \int_k^{k+1}\frac{1}{x}\,dx
\le
\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{1}{k}.
$$
Hence
$$
\sum_{k=1}^N \frac{1}{k}\ge \log(N+1).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $f$ be continuous on $[-1,1]$ and suppose that
$$
\int_{-1}^1 f(x)x^{2n}\,dx=0
$$
for all integers $n\ge 0$. Show that $f(0)=0$.

#proof
Define
$$
F(x)=f(x)+f(-x),\qquad 0\le x\le 1.
$$
Then $F$ is continuous on $[0,1]$. Since $x^{2n}$ is even,
$$
0=\int_{-1}^1 f(x)x^{2n}\,dx
=
\int_0^1 \bigl(f(x)+f(-x)\bigr)x^{2n}\,dx
=
\int_0^1 F(x)x^{2n}\,dx.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_0^1 F(x)P(x^2)\,dx=0
$$
for every polynomial $P$.

Now define
$$
G(y)=F(\sqrt{y}),\qquad 0\le y\le 1.
$$
Then $G$ is continuous on $[0,1]$. By the Weierstrass approximation theorem, there are polynomials $P_j$ such that
$$
P_j(y)\to G(y)
$$
uniformly on $[0,1]$. Hence
$$
P_j(x^2)\to G(x^2)=F(x)
$$
uniformly on $[0,1]$.

Since
$$
\int_0^1 F(x)P_j(x^2)\,dx=0
$$
for all $j$, taking the limit gives
$$
\int_0^1 F(x)^2\,dx=0.
$$
Thus $F(x)=0$ for all $x\in[0,1]$. In particular,
$$
F(0)=f(0)+f(0)=2f(0)=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
f(0)=0.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $K_1,K_2\subset \ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ be compact. Define
$$
S=\{v\in \ell^2(\mathbb{N})\mid v=v_1+v_2
\text{ for some } v_1\in K_1,\ v_2\in K_2\}.
$$
Prove that $S$ is compact.

#proof
The product $K_1\times K_2$ is compact. Define
$$
T:K_1\times K_2\to \ell^2(\mathbb{N})
$$
by
$$
T(v_1,v_2)=v_1+v_2.
$$
The map $T$ is continuous because addition is continuous in the Hilbert space
$\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$.

By definition,
$$
S=T(K_1\times K_2).
$$
Since the continuous image of a compact set is compact, $S$ is compact.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $f:(0,\infty)\to(0,\infty)$ be twice differentiable with $f''<0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
  Prove that $f$ is increasing.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**  Prove that $f$ is uniformly continuous.

#proof
Since $f''<0$, the derivative $f'$ is strictly decreasing.

Suppose for contradiction that there exists $x_0>0$ such that
$$
f'(x_0)<0.
$$
Since $f'$ is decreasing, for every $x\ge x_0$ we have
$$
f'(x)\le f'(x_0)<0.
$$
Thus, for $x\ge x_0$,
$$
f(x)\le f(x_0)+f'(x_0)(x-x_0).
$$
The right-hand side tends to $-\infty$ as $x\to\infty$, contradicting the assumption
that $f(x)>0$ for all $x>0$.

Therefore
$$
f'(x)\ge 0
$$
for all $x>0$. If $f'(x_0)=0$ for some $x_0$, then since $f'$ is strictly decreasing,
we would have $f'(x)<0$ for all $x>x_0$, again a contradiction. Hence
$$
f'(x)>0
$$
for all $x>0$. Therefore $f$ is increasing.

Since $f$ is increasing and positive, the limit
$$
L=\lim_{x\to 0^+} f(x)
$$
exists and is finite. Define $\widetilde f$ on $[0,1]$ by
$$
\widetilde f(0)=L,
\qquad
\widetilde f(x)=f(x)\quad \text{for }0<x\le 1.
$$
Then $\widetilde f$ is continuous on the compact interval $[0,1]$, so it is uniformly continuous on $[0,1]$.

On $[1,\infty)$, since $f'$ is decreasing, we have
$$
0<f'(x)\le f'(1)
$$
for all $x\ge 1$. Hence $f$ is Lipschitz on $[1,\infty)$:
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\le f'(1)|x-y|
$$
for all $x,y\ge 1$.

Combining the uniform continuity on $[0,1]$ with the Lipschitz estimate on $[1,\infty)$,
we conclude that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $(0,\infty)$.

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Which of the following statements are true? Explain your answer.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** 
If $(M,d)$ is a metric space, and $f:M\to M$ is a map such that for any
distinct $x,y\in M$ one has
$$
d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y),
$$
then $f$ has exactly one fixed point in $M$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** 
If $(M,d)$ is a complete metric space, and $f:M\to M$ is a map such that
$$
d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y)
$$
for any distinct $x,y\in M$, then $f$ has exactly one fixed point in $M$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)** 
If $(M,d)$ is a bounded metric space, and $f:M\to M$ is a map such that
$$
d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y)
$$
for any distinct $x,y\in M$, then $f$ has exactly one fixed point in $M$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)** 
 If $(M,d)$ is a compact metric space, and $f:M\to M$ is a map such that
$$
d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y)
$$
for any distinct $x,y\in M$, then $f$ has exactly one fixed point in $M$.


#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)**  **False**

Take
$$
M=(0,1)
$$
with the usual metric and
$$
f(x)=\frac{x}{2}.
$$
Then for $x\ne y$,
$$
d(f(x),f(y))=\frac{1}{2}d(x,y)<d(x,y).
$$
But $f$ has no fixed point in $M$, since $x=x/2$ implies $x=0$, and $0\notin M$.

<br>

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**   **False**

Let
$$
M=[0,\infty)
$$
with the metric
$$
d(x,y)=|\log(1+x)-\log(1+y)|.
$$
This metric space is complete because $x\mapsto \log(1+x)$ is an isometry from
$M$ onto $[0,\infty)$ with the usual metric.

Define
$$
f(x)=x+1.
$$
If $x<y$, then
$$
d(f(x),f(y))
=
\log\frac{y+2}{x+2}
<
\log\frac{y+1}{x+1}
=
d(x,y).
$$
Thus $f$ strictly decreases distances between distinct points. But $f$ has no fixed point, since
$$
x+1=x
$$
has no solution.

<br>

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(c)**  **False**

The same example as in part **(a) works**. The space $(0,1)$ is bounded, and
$f(x)=x/2$ strictly decreases distances, but it has no fixed point in $(0,1)$.


<br>


<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(d)**  **True**

 
Let $(M,d)$ be compact and suppose
$$
d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y)
$$
whenever $x\ne y$.

First, $f$ is continuous. Indeed, if $x_n\to x$, then
$$
d(f(x_n),f(x))\le d(x_n,x)\to 0.
$$

Define
$$
g(x)=d(x,f(x)).
$$
Since $f$ is continuous, $g$ is continuous. Since $M$ is compact, $g$ attains a minimum at some $x_0\in M$.

If $g(x_0)>0$, then $x_0\ne f(x_0)$, so
$$
g(f(x_0))
=
d(f(x_0),f(f(x_0)))
<
d(x_0,f(x_0))
=
g(x_0),
$$
contradicting the minimality of $g(x_0)$. Hence
$$
g(x_0)=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
x_0=f(x_0),
$$
so a fixed point exists.

The fixed point is unique. If $x$ and $y$ are two distinct fixed points, then
$$
d(x,y)=d(f(x),f(y))<d(x,y),
$$
which is impossible. Hence $f$ has exactly one fixed point.

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $N\in C^\infty(\mathbb{R}\times(0,\infty))$ satisfy
$$
N(p+a,t+a^2)+N(p-a,t+a^2)\ge 2N(p,t)
$$
for all $a,p\in\mathbb{R}$ and $t>0$. Show that
$$
\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^2 N}{\partial p^2}(p,t)
+
\frac{\partial N}{\partial t}(p,t)
\ge 0.
$$

#proof
Fix $p\in\mathbb{R}$ and $t>0$. For small $a$, define
$$
F(a)=N(p+a,t+a^2)+N(p-a,t+a^2)-2N(p,t).
$$
By assumption,
$$
F(a)\ge 0
$$
for all sufficiently small $a$, and
$$
F(0)=0.
$$
Thus $a=0$ is a minimum of $F$, so
$$
F''(0)\ge 0.
$$

Using Taylor expansion at $(p,t)$, we have
$$
N(p+a,t+a^2)
=
N(p,t)+aN_p(p,t)+a^2N_t(p,t)+\frac{a^2}{2}N_{pp}(p,t)+o(a^2),
$$
and
$$
N(p-a,t+a^2)
=
N(p,t)-aN_p(p,t)+a^2N_t(p,t)+\frac{a^2}{2}N_{pp}(p,t)+o(a^2).
$$
Adding these and subtracting $2N(p,t)$ gives
$$
F(a)
=
a^2\bigl(N_{pp}(p,t)+2N_t(p,t)\bigr)+o(a^2).
$$
Since $F(a)\ge 0$, dividing by $a^2$ and letting $a\to 0$ gives
$$
N_{pp}(p,t)+2N_t(p,t)\ge 0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{1}{2}N_{pp}(p,t)+N_t(p,t)\ge 0.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $Q$ be the square with vertices $(1,1),(1,-1),(-1,1),(-1,-1)$, and let $C$ be its boundary oriented counterclockwise. Evaluate
$$
\int_C \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy.
$$

#proof
The vector field
$$
\left(\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2},\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\right)
$$
is singular at $(0,0)$, and the square $Q$ contains $(0,0)$.

Let $C_\varepsilon$ be the circle of radius $\varepsilon$ centered at the origin, oriented counterclockwise. On $C_\varepsilon$, write
$$
x=\varepsilon \cos t,\qquad y=\varepsilon\sin t,
$$
where $0\le t\le 2\pi$. Then
$$
dx=-\varepsilon\sin t\,dt,
\qquad
dy=\varepsilon\cos t\,dt.
$$
Hence
$$
\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
=
\sin^2 t\,dt+\cos^2 t\,dt
=
dt.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_{C_\varepsilon}
\frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
=
\int_0^{2\pi}dt
=
2\pi.
$$

On the region between $C$ and $C_\varepsilon$, the vector field is smooth and has curl $0$. Therefore the integral over the outer boundary equals the integral over the small circle:
$$
\int_C \frac{-y}{x^2+y^2}\,dx+\frac{x}{x^2+y^2}\,dy
=
2\pi.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Calculate
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-3x^2+2xy-3y^2}\,dx\,dy.
$$

#proof
We write
$$
-3x^2+2xy-3y^2
=
-\begin{pmatrix}x&y\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & -1\\
-1 & 3
\end{pmatrix}
\begin{pmatrix}
x\\
y
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Let
$$
A=
\begin{pmatrix}
3 & -1\\
-1 & 3
\end{pmatrix}.
$$
Then
$$
\det A=9-1=8.
$$
Using the standard Gaussian integral formula
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^n} e^{-x^T A x}\,dx
=
\frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\sqrt{\det A}},
$$
for positive definite $A$, we get
$$
\int_{\mathbb{R}^2} e^{-3x^2+2xy-3y^2}\,dx\,dy
=
\frac{\pi}{\sqrt{8}}
=
\frac{\pi}{2\sqrt{2}}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Give an example of a path connected bounded complete metric space that is not separable.

#proof
Let $A$ be an uncountable set. Define
$$
X=\{o\}\cup (A\times (0,1]).
$$
Think of $X$ as a hedgehog with one spine for each $\alpha\in A$, all joined at the point $o$.

Define a metric $d$ on $X$ by
$$
d(o,(\alpha,t))=t,
$$
and
$$
d((\alpha,s),(\beta,t))
=
\begin{cases}
|s-t|, & \alpha=\beta,\\
s+t, & \alpha\ne \beta.
\end{cases}
$$

The space is bounded because
$$
d(x,y)\le 2
$$
for all $x,y\in X$.

It is path connected: any point $(\alpha,t)$ can be joined to $o$ by the path
$$
\gamma(r)=(\alpha,(1-r)t),\qquad 0\le r<1,
$$
with $\gamma(1)=o$. Therefore any two points can be joined by going from the first point to $o$ and then from $o$ to the second point.

The space is complete. Indeed, let $(x_n)$ be a Cauchy sequence. If the sequence visits infinitely many different spines while staying away from $o$, then distances between some terms would be bounded below by a positive number, contradicting the Cauchy property. Hence either $x_n\to o$, or eventually all $x_n$ lie in one spine. In the latter case, completeness follows from the completeness of $[0,1]$.

Finally, $X$ is not separable. For each $\alpha\in A$, the point $(\alpha,1)$ belongs to $X$. If $\alpha\ne \beta$, then
$$
d((\alpha,1),(\beta,1))=2.
$$
Thus $X$ contains an uncountable set of points separated from each other by distance $2$. Therefore no countable subset can be dense in $X$.

Hence $X$ is path connected, bounded, complete, and not separable.
