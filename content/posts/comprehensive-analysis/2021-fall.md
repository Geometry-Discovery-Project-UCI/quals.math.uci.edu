::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $f:
\mathbb{R}\to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous function satisfying
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\ge |x-y|
$$
for all $x,y\in \mathbb{R}$. Prove that $f$ is surjective.

#proof
First observe that $f$ is injective. Indeed, if $f(x)=f(y)$, then
$$
0=|f(x)-f(y)|\ge |x-y|,
$$
so $x=y$.

A continuous injective function on an interval is strictly monotone. Hence $f$ is either strictly increasing or strictly decreasing on $\mathbb{R}$.

Suppose first that $f$ is strictly increasing. For $x>0$, using the given inequality with $y=0$, we have
$$
|f(x)-f(0)|\ge x.
$$
Since $f$ is increasing, $f(x)>f(0)$, so
$$
 f(x)-f(0)\ge x.
$$
Therefore
$$
 f(x)\ge f(0)+x\to +\infty
$$
as $x\to +\infty$.

Similarly, for $x<0$, since $f(x)<f(0)$, we have
$$
 f(0)-f(x)\ge -x,
$$
and hence
$$
 f(x)\le f(0)+x\to -\infty
$$
as $x\to -\infty$.

Thus $f$ is continuous and its values tend to $-\infty$ in one direction and $+\infty$ in the other direction. By the intermediate value theorem, every real number is attained by $f$.

If $f$ is strictly decreasing, the same argument gives
$$
 f(x)\to -\infty \quad \text{as } x\to +\infty
$$
and
$$
 f(x)\to +\infty \quad \text{as } x\to -\infty.
$$
Again, the intermediate value theorem implies that $f$ is surjective.

Therefore $f$ is surjective.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Show that the sequence $\{a_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ defined recursively by
$$
a_1>0,\qquad a_n=\sqrt{5a_{n-1}-6}\quad \text{for } n\ge 2,
$$
converges, and find its limit.

#proof
As written, the recurrence defines a real sequence only when the expression under the square root is nonnegative at every step. We use this fact below.

The fixed points of the recurrence are the solutions of
$$
L=\sqrt{5L-6}.
$$
Squaring gives
$$
L^2=5L-6,
$$
so
$$
(L-2)(L-3)=0.
$$
Thus the only possible limits are
$$
L=2 \quad \text{or} \quad L=3.
$$

We first note that, if the sequence is defined as a real sequence for all $n$, then necessarily
$$
a_1\ge 2.
$$
Indeed, suppose $a_1<2$. Since the sequence is defined for all $n$, we must have $a_n\ge 6/5$ for all $n$. For $6/5\le x<2$,
$$
\sqrt{5x-6}<x,
$$
because this inequality is equivalent to
$$
5x-6<x^2,
$$
or
$$
0<(x-2)(x-3),
$$
which is true for $x<2$. Hence $a_n$ would be decreasing and bounded below by $6/5$, so it would converge to some $L<2$. Passing to the limit in the recurrence would give
$$
L=\sqrt{5L-6},
$$
so $L=2$ or $L=3$, a contradiction. Therefore $a_1\ge 2$.

If $a_1=2$, then
$$
a_2=\sqrt{10-6}=2,
$$
and so $a_n=2$ for all $n$. Hence
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=2.
$$

Now suppose $a_1>2$.

If $2<a_1<3$, then for $2<x<3$,
$$
\sqrt{5x-6}>x
$$
because
$$
5x-6>x^2
$$
is equivalent to
$$
(x-2)(x-3)<0.
$$
Also, if $x<3$, then
$$
\sqrt{5x-6}<3.
$$
Thus if $2<a_n<3$, then
$$
2<a_n<a_{n+1}<3.
$$
So $\{a_n\}$ is increasing and bounded above by $3$, hence convergent.

If $a_1=3$, then $a_n=3$ for all $n$.

If $a_1>3$, then for $x>3$,
$$
\sqrt{5x-6}<x
$$
and
$$
\sqrt{5x-6}>3.
$$
Thus $\{a_n\}$ is decreasing and bounded below by $3$, hence convergent.

In all cases with $a_1>2$, the limit $L$ satisfies
$$
L=\sqrt{5L-6},
$$
and since $a_n>2$, the limit cannot be $2$. Therefore
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=3.
$$

Thus, for a real sequence defined for all $n$, the answer is
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=
\begin{cases}
2, & a_1=2,\\
3, & a_1>2.
\end{cases}
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is continuous on $[a,b]$ and differentiable on $(a,b)$. Assume that
$$
f(a)=f(b)
$$
and
$$
|f'(x)|\le 1
$$
for all $x\in (a,b)$. Prove that
$$
|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|\le \frac{b-a}{2}
$$
for any $x_1,x_2\in [a,b]$.

#proof
Since $f$ is continuous on the compact interval $[a,b]$, it attains its maximum and minimum. Let
$$
M=\max_{[a,b]} f, \qquad m=\min_{[a,b]} f.
$$
It is enough to prove
$$
M-m\le \frac{b-a}{2},
$$
because then, for any $x_1,x_2\in [a,b]$,
$$
|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|\le M-m\le \frac{b-a}{2}.
$$

Choose $u,v\in [a,b]$ such that
$$
f(u)=m,\qquad f(v)=M.
$$
Assume without loss of generality that $u\le v$. By the mean value theorem and the bound $|f'|\le 1$,
$$
M-m=|f(v)-f(u)|\le v-u.
$$

On the other hand, since $f(a)=f(b)$,
$$
M-m=f(v)-f(u)
=\bigl(f(v)-f(b)\bigr)+\bigl(f(a)-f(u)\bigr).
$$
Therefore, again using the mean value theorem,
$$
M-m\le |f(v)-f(b)|+|f(a)-f(u)|
\le (b-v)+(u-a).
$$
Hence
$$
M-m\le v-u
$$
and
$$
M-m\le (b-v)+(u-a).
$$
Adding these two inequalities gives
$$
2(M-m)\le (v-u)+(b-v)+(u-a)=b-a.
$$
Thus
$$
M-m\le \frac{b-a}{2}.
$$
Therefore
$$
|f(x_1)-f(x_2)|\le \frac{b-a}{2}
$$
for all $x_1,x_2\in [a,b]$.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)**  TRUE or FALSE: If a bounded function $f:[a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ is Riemann integrable, then it is continuous on $[a,b]$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**  TRUE or FALSE: If a bounded function $f:[a,b]\to \mathbb{R}$ has uncountably many discontinuities, then it is not Riemann integrable on $[a,b]$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)**  The statement is false.

For example, define
$$
f(x)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x=c,\\
0, & x\ne c,
\end{cases}
$$
where $c\in [a,b]$ is fixed. This function is bounded and is discontinuous at $c$. However, it is Riemann integrable, and
$$
\int_a^b f(x)\,dx=0.
$$
Indeed, for every partition, the lower sum is $0$, and by choosing a partition with a very small interval containing $c$, the upper sums can be made arbitrarily small. Hence $f$ is Riemann integrable but not continuous.

Therefore **(a)** is false.

<br>

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)**   The statement is also false.

Let $C\subset [0,1]$ be the standard Cantor set, and define
$$
f(x)=\mathbf{1}_C(x)=
\begin{cases}
1, & x\in C,\\
0, & x\notin C.
\end{cases}
$$
The Cantor set $C$ is uncountable, closed, has empty interior, and has Lebesgue measure zero.

The function $\mathbf{1}_C$ is discontinuous at every point of $C$. Indeed, if $x\in C$, then arbitrarily close to $x$ there are points outside $C$, so the function values near $x$ include both $1$ and $0$.

If $x\notin C$, then since $C$ is closed, there is a small open interval around $x$ disjoint from $C$. On that interval, $\mathbf{1}_C=0$, so the function is continuous at $x$.

Thus the set of discontinuities of $\mathbf{1}_C$ is exactly $C$, which is uncountable. Nevertheless, since $C$ has measure zero, $\mathbf{1}_C$ is Riemann integrable and
$$
\int_0^1 \mathbf{1}_C(x)\,dx=0.
$$
For a general interval $[a,b]$, one may use an affine copy of the Cantor set inside $[a,b]$.

Therefore (b) is false.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $f:\mathbb{R}^2\to \mathbb{R}$ be a continuous function such that $f^{-1}(E)\subset \mathbb{R}^2$ is bounded whenever $E\subset \mathbb{R}$ is bounded. Prove that $f$ attains either a maximum or a minimum value.

#proof
The hypothesis implies that the sets
$$
f^{-1}([-C,C])
$$
are bounded for every $C>0$.

Thus, for every $C>0$, there exists $R_C>0$ such that
$$
\|x\|>R_C \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |f(x)|>C.
$$
In other words, $|f(x)|\to \infty$ as $\|x\|\to \infty$.

We now show that, in fact, $f(x)\to +\infty$ at infinity or $f(x)\to -\infty$ at infinity.

Take $C=1$. There exists $R_1>0$ such that
$$
\|x\|>R_1 \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |f(x)|>1.
$$
The exterior region
$$
\{x\in \mathbb{R}^2: \|x\|>R_1\}
$$
is connected. Its image under the continuous function $f$ is therefore connected. But it lies in
$$
(-\infty,-1)\cup (1,\infty),
$$
which has two connected components. Hence the image must lie entirely in one of these components.

Therefore either
$$
f(x)>1 \quad \text{for all } \|x\|>R_1,
$$
or
$$
f(x)<-1 \quad \text{for all } \|x\|>R_1.
$$

Suppose first that
$$
f(x)>1 \quad \text{for all } \|x\|>R_1.
$$
Let $C>|f(0)|$. Choose $R\ge R_1$ so large that
$$
\|x\|>R \quad \Longrightarrow \quad |f(x)|>C.
$$
Since $\|x\|>R$ also implies $\|x\|>R_1$, we have $f(x)>1$ there. Therefore
$$
\|x\|>R \quad \Longrightarrow \quad f(x)>C>|f(0)|\ge f(0).
$$
Thus no global minimum can occur outside the closed disk
$$
\overline{B_R(0)}.
$$
Since $f$ is continuous and $\overline{B_R(0)}$ is compact, $f$ attains a minimum on $\overline{B_R(0)}$. This minimum is also the global minimum on $\mathbb{R}^2$.

The other case is similar. If
$$
f(x)<-1 \quad \text{for all } \|x\|>R_1,
$$
then choosing $C>|f(0)|$ and $R$ large enough gives
$$
\|x\|>R \quad \Longrightarrow \quad f(x)<-C<-|f(0)|\le f(0).
$$
So no global maximum can occur outside $\overline{B_R(0)}$. By compactness, $f$ attains a maximum on $\overline{B_R(0)}$, and hence on all of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

Therefore $f$ attains either a maximum or a minimum value.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $f:[0,1]\times [0,1]\to \mathbb{R}$ be continuous, and define $g:[0,1]\to \mathbb{R}$ by
$$
g(x)=\max\{f(x,y):y\in [0,1]\}.
$$
Prove that $g$ is continuous.

#proof
Since $f$ is continuous on the compact set $[0,1]\times [0,1]$, it is uniformly continuous.

Let $\varepsilon>0$. By uniform continuity, there exists $\delta>0$ such that whenever
$$
|x-x_0|<\delta
$$
and $y\in [0,1]$, we have
$$
|f(x,y)-f(x_0,y)|<\varepsilon.
$$

Fix $x,x_0\in [0,1]$ with $|x-x_0|<\delta$.

For every $y\in [0,1]$,
$$
f(x,y)\le f(x_0,y)+\varepsilon\le g(x_0)+\varepsilon.
$$
Taking the maximum over $y$ gives
$$
g(x)\le g(x_0)+\varepsilon.
$$

Similarly, for every $y\in [0,1]$,
$$
f(x_0,y)\le f(x,y)+\varepsilon\le g(x)+\varepsilon.
$$
Taking the maximum over $y$ gives
$$
g(x_0)\le g(x)+\varepsilon.
$$

Therefore
$$
|g(x)-g(x_0)|\le \varepsilon.
$$
Thus $g$ is continuous on $[0,1]$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Suppose $M$ is a connected metric space which is also locally pathwise connected, meaning that every point $x\in M$ has an open neighborhood $U(x)\subset M$ that is pathwise connected. Does it imply that $M$ is pathwise connected? Prove or give a counterexample.

#proof
Yes. Under this hypothesis, $M$ is pathwise connected.

Fix a point $p\in M$, and let $C$ be the path component of $p$. Thus
$$
C=\{x\in M: \text{there is a path in } M \text{ from } p \text{ to } x\}.
$$
We show that $C$ is both open and closed in $M$.

First, let $x\in C$. Since $M$ is locally pathwise connected in the stated sense, there exists an open pathwise connected neighborhood $U$ of $x$. For any $y\in U$, there is a path inside $U$ from $x$ to $y$. Since $x\in C$, there is also a path from $p$ to $x$. Concatenating these paths gives a path from $p$ to $y$. Hence $y\in C$. Therefore
$$
U\subset C,
$$
so $C$ is open.

Next, let $x\in M\setminus C$. Again, choose an open pathwise connected neighborhood $U$ of $x$. If $U$ met $C$, say $y\in U\cap C$, then there would be a path from $p$ to $y$, and also a path inside $U$ from $y$ to $x$. Concatenating them would give a path from $p$ to $x$, contradicting $x\notin C$. Thus
$$
U\subset M\setminus C,
$$
so $M\setminus C$ is open. Therefore $C$ is closed.

Thus $C$ is a nonempty subset of $M$ that is both open and closed. Since $M$ is connected, we must have
$$
C=M.
$$
Therefore every point of $M$ can be joined to $p$ by a path, and $M$ is pathwise connected.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let
$$
S=\{(x,y,z)\in \mathbb{R}^3: x^2+y^2+z^2=1\}
$$
denote the unit sphere in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Evaluate the surface integral
$$
\iint_S (3x^2+4y+5z)\,dA.
$$

#proof
By symmetry of the unit sphere,
$$
\iint_S y\,dA=0
$$
and
$$
\iint_S z\,dA=0.
$$
Therefore
$$
\iint_S (3x^2+4y+5z)\,dA
=3\iint_S x^2\,dA.
$$

By symmetry again,
$$
\iint_S x^2\,dA
=\iint_S y^2\,dA
=\iint_S z^2\,dA.
$$
Since on $S$ we have
$$
x^2+y^2+z^2=1,
$$
it follows that
$$
3\iint_S x^2\,dA
=\iint_S (x^2+y^2+z^2)\,dA
=\iint_S 1\,dA.
$$
The area of the unit sphere is
$$
4\pi.
$$
Hence
$$
3\iint_S x^2\,dA=4\pi.
$$
Therefore
$$
\iint_S (3x^2+4y+5z)\,dA=4\pi.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let
$$
f(x)=\exp\bigl(\sin(\|x\|^{2021})-\|x\|\bigr), \qquad x\in \mathbb{R}^n,
$$
where $\|x\|$ is the standard Euclidean norm of $x\in \mathbb{R}^n$. Prove that $f$ is uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.

#proof
Define
$$
h(r)=\exp\bigl(\sin(r^{2021})-r\bigr), \qquad r\ge 0.
$$
Then
$$
f(x)=h(\|x\|).
$$

We claim that $h$ is Lipschitz on $[0,\infty)$. For $r>0$,
$$
h'(r)=\exp\bigl(\sin(r^{2021})-r\bigr)\bigl(2021r^{2020}\cos(r^{2021})-1\bigr).
$$
Hence
$$
|h'(r)|\le e^{1-r}\bigl(2021r^{2020}+1\bigr).
$$
The function
$$
e^{1-r}\bigl(2021r^{2020}+1\bigr)
$$
is bounded on $[0,\infty)$, because it is continuous and tends to $0$ as $r\to\infty$. Therefore there exists a constant $C>0$ such that
$$
|h'(r)|\le C
$$
for all $r>0$.

By the mean value theorem, for all $r,s\ge 0$,
$$
|h(r)-h(s)|\le C|r-s|.
$$
Thus $h$ is Lipschitz.

Now for any $x,y\in \mathbb{R}^n$,
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|
=|h(\|x\|)-h(\|y\|)|
\le C\bigl|\|x\|-\|y\|\bigr|.
$$
By the reverse triangle inequality,
$$
\bigl|\|x\|-\|y\|\bigr|\le \|x-y\|.
$$
Therefore
$$
|f(x)-f(y)|\le C\|x-y\|.
$$
So $f$ is Lipschitz on $\mathbb{R}^n$, and hence uniformly continuous on $\mathbb{R}^n$.
