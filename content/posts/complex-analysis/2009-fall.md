# 2009 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
For $\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0$, find the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)\beta(\beta+1)\cdots(\beta+n-1)}
{n!\gamma(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+n-1)}
z^n.
$$

#proof
Write the coefficient as
$$
c_n=\frac{(\alpha)_n(\beta)_n}{n!(\gamma)_n},
$$
where $(a)_n=a(a+1)\cdots(a+n-1)$ is the rising factorial.

Then
$$
\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}
=\frac{(\alpha+n)(\beta+n)}{(n+1)(\gamma+n)}.
$$
As $n\to\infty$,
$$
\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\to 1.
$$
Therefore, by the ratio test, the power series has radius of convergence
$$
1.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Prove or disprove: there is a holomorphic function $f$ on the unit disk $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
\{z\in D(0,1): f^{(k)}(z)=0\text{ for some nonnegative integer }k\}=(-1,1).
$$

#proof
No such holomorphic function exists.

For each $k\geq 0$, the zero set of $f^{(k)}$ is either discrete in $D(0,1)$ or all of $D(0,1)$.

If no derivative $f^{(k)}$ is identically zero, then the set
$$
\bigcup_{k=0}^{\infty}\{z:f^{(k)}(z)=0\}
$$
is a countable union of discrete sets. In particular, it cannot contain the whole interval $(-1,1)$, which has accumulation points inside the disk.

If some derivative $f^{(m)}$ is identically zero, then all higher derivatives are also identically zero. Since the problem allows nonnegative integers $k$, this would make every point of $D(0,1)$ belong to the displayed set, not just the interval $(-1,1)$.

Both cases are impossible. Hence no such holomorphic function exists.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let
$$
L=\{z=x+iy:y=2\}.
$$
Assume $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ is entire and that for every $z\in L$, one has $f(z)\in L$. If
$$
f(0)=i,
$$
find $f(4i)$.

#proof
Set
$$
g(z)=f(z)-2i.
$$
Then $g$ is entire, and $g$ is real-valued on the line $L$.

Reflection across the line $y=2$ is
$$
z\mapsto \overline z+4i.
$$
By the Schwarz reflection principle,
$$
g(\overline z+4i)=\overline{g(z)}.
$$
Taking $z=0$, we get
$$
g(4i)=\overline{g(0)}.
$$
Since
$$
g(0)=f(0)-2i=i-2i=-i,
$$
we have
$$
g(4i)=i.
$$
Therefore
$$
f(4i)=g(4i)+2i=3i.
$$
Thus
$$
f(4i)=3i.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $\{f_\alpha\}_{\alpha\in A}$ be a family of holomorphic functions on $D(0,1)$ such that for every $z\in D(0,1)$ and every $\alpha$,
$$
\operatorname{Im} f_\alpha(z)\neq (\operatorname{Re} f_\alpha(z))^2.
$$
Prove that $\{f_\alpha\}$ is a normal family.

#proof
The forbidden set
$$
\{u+iv:v=u^2\}
$$
contains at least two distinct complex numbers, for example $0$ and $1+i$.

Thus every function $f_\alpha$ omits the two values
$$
0
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
1+i.
$$
By Montel's theorem for families omitting two fixed complex values, the family is normal in the sense that every sequence has a subsequence converging locally uniformly to a holomorphic function or to $\infty$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let
$$
f:\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}\to \mathbb C\setminus D(0,1)
$$
be holomorphic. Prove that $f$ is constant.

#proof
Since $f$ takes values outside the unit disk, $f$ never vanishes and
$$
|f(z)|\geq 1.
$$
Therefore
$$
g(z)=\frac1{f(z)}
$$
is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$ and satisfies
$$
|g(z)|\leq 1.
$$

The singularities of $g$ at $0$ and $1$ are removable, because $g$ is bounded near each of them. Hence $g$ extends to a bounded entire function on $\mathbb C$.

By Liouville's theorem, $g$ is constant. Therefore $f=1/g$ is constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is a polynomial such that all of its zeros are inside the unit disk. Prove that all zeros of $f'$ are also inside the unit disk.

#proof
By the Gauss-Lucas theorem, every zero of $f'$ lies in the convex hull of the zeros of $f$.

The unit disk is convex. Since all zeros of $f$ lie inside the unit disk, their convex hull is also contained inside the unit disk. Therefore every zero of $f'$ lies inside the unit disk.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Find
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{1-\cos(2x)}{x^2}\,dx.
$$

#proof
Integrate by parts. Let
$$
u=1-\cos(2x),
\qquad
dv=\frac{dx}{x^2}.
$$
Then
$$
du=2\sin(2x)\,dx,
\qquad
v=-\frac1x.
$$
The boundary term
$$
-\frac{1-\cos(2x)}{x}
$$
vanishes at both $0$ and $\infty$. Hence
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{1-\cos(2x)}{x^2}\,dx
=2\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(2x)}{x}\,dx.
$$
Using
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(ax)}{x}\,dx=\frac{\pi}{2}
\qquad (a>0),
$$
we get
$$
2\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(2x)}{x}\,dx
=2\cdot \frac{\pi}{2}
=\pi.
$$
Therefore
$$
\displaystyle
\int_0^\infty \frac{1-\cos(2x)}{x^2}\,dx=\pi.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Does there exist a sequence of holomorphic functions $\{f_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
f_n(z)\to \frac1z
$$
uniformly on
$$
\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|=1/2\}?
$$

#proof
No.

If such a sequence existed, then uniform convergence on the circle $|z|=1/2$ would imply
$$
\int_{|z|=1/2} f_n(z)\,dz
\to
\int_{|z|=1/2}\frac1z\,dz.
$$
But each $f_n$ is holomorphic in the disk, so by Cauchy's theorem,
$$
\int_{|z|=1/2} f_n(z)\,dz=0.
$$
On the other hand,
$$
\int_{|z|=1/2}\frac1z\,dz=2\pi i.
$$
This is a contradiction. Therefore no such sequence exists.
::
