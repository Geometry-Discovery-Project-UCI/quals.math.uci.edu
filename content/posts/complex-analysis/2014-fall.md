# 2014 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $f$ be an entire holomorphic function such that
$$
f(z)\notin \mathbb R
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$, where $\mathbb R$ is the real line in the complex plane. Prove or disprove: $f$ is constant.

#proof
The statement is true.

Since $f(\mathbb C)$ does not meet the real axis and $\mathbb C$ is connected, the imaginary part of $f$ has one fixed sign. Thus either
$$
\operatorname{Im} f(z)>0\quad\text{for all }z,
$$
or
$$
\operatorname{Im} f(z)<0\quad\text{for all }z.
$$
In either case, $\operatorname{Im} f$ is a harmonic function on the whole plane that is bounded from one side.

A harmonic function on $\mathbb C$ that is bounded above or bounded below is constant. Therefore $\operatorname{Im} f$ is constant. If
$$
f=u+iv
$$
and $v$ is constant, then the Cauchy-Riemann equations give
$$
u_x=v_y=0,
\qquad
u_y=-v_x=0.
$$
Hence $u$ is constant too. Therefore $f$ is constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Evaluate the real integral
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\log x}{1+x^4}\,dx.
$$

#proof
For $0<a<4$, define
$$
F(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x^4}\,dx.
$$
The standard beta-integral formula gives
$$
F(a)=\frac{\pi}{4}\csc\left(\frac{\pi a}{4}\right).
$$
Differentiating with respect to $a$,
$$
F'(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}\log x}{1+x^4}\,dx.
$$
Thus the desired integral is $F'(1)$. Since
$$
F'(a)
=-\frac{\pi^2}{16}
\csc\left(\frac{\pi a}{4}\right)
\cot\left(\frac{\pi a}{4}\right),
$$
we get
$$
F'(1)
=-\frac{\pi^2}{16}\csc\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)\cot\left(\frac{\pi}{4}\right)
=-\frac{\pi^2}{16}\sqrt2.
$$
Therefore
$$
\displaystyle
\int_0^\infty \frac{\log x}{1+x^4}\,dx
=-\frac{\pi^2\sqrt2}{16}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $f$ be entire holomorphic such that
$$
f(x+ix)\in \mathbb R
$$
for all $x\in\mathbb R$. If
$$
f(2)=1-i,
$$
find $f(2i)$.

#proof
The line $x+ix$ is the line through the origin with angle $\pi/4$. Reflection across this line is
$$
z\mapsto i\overline z.
$$
Since $f$ is entire and real-valued on this line, the Schwarz reflection principle gives
$$
f(i\overline z)=\overline{f(z)}.
$$
Taking $z=2$, we have
$$
i\overline{2}=2i.
$$
Therefore
$$
f(2i)=\overline{f(2)}
=\overline{1-i}
=1+i.
$$
Thus
$$
f(2i)=1+i.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $h(x)$ be a twice differentiable function on $[-1,1]$ such that
$$
h(0)=h'(0)=0,
\qquad
h''(0)\neq 0.
$$
Prove that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} h\left(\frac1n\right)z^n
$$
defines a holomorphic function on $D(0,1)$ which is continuous on $\overline{D(0,1)}$.

#proof
Since $h$ is twice differentiable at $0$ and
$$
h(0)=h'(0)=0,
$$
Taylor's formula at $0$ gives
$$
h(t)=O(t^2)
\qquad (t\to 0).
$$
Therefore
$$
h\left(\frac1n\right)=O\left(\frac1{n^2}\right).
$$
So there is a constant $C$ such that
$$
\left|h\left(\frac1n\right)\right|\leq \frac{C}{n^2}
$$
for all sufficiently large $n$.

For $|z|\leq 1$,
$$
\left|h\left(\frac1n\right)z^n\right|
\leq \left|h\left(\frac1n\right)\right|
\leq \frac{C}{n^2}
$$
for all large $n$. Since $\sum 1/n^2$ converges, the Weierstrass $M$-test shows that the series converges uniformly on $\overline{D(0,1)}$.

Each term is continuous on $\overline{D(0,1)}$ and holomorphic on $D(0,1)$. The uniform limit on the closed disk is continuous there, and the locally uniform convergence inside the disk implies that the limit is holomorphic in $D(0,1)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $D$ be a bounded domain in $\mathbb C$ with piecewise $C^1$ boundary. Let $f$ be holomorphic in $D$, continuous on $\overline D$, and suppose all zeros of $f$ are
$$
z_1,\ldots,z_n\in D,
$$
counting multiplicity. Let $g$ be holomorphic in $D$ and continuous on $\overline D$. Evaluate
$$
\int_{\partial D} g(z)\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz.
$$

#proof
Near a zero $z_j$ of $f$ of multiplicity $m_j$, write
$$
f(z)=(z-z_j)^{m_j}h_j(z),
$$
where $h_j$ is holomorphic and $h_j(z_j)\neq 0$. Then
$$
\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}
=\frac{m_j}{z-z_j}+\frac{h_j'(z)}{h_j(z)}.
$$
Thus
$$
g(z)\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}
$$
has residue
$$
m_jg(z_j)
$$
at $z_j$.

Since the zeros are listed counting multiplicity, the residue theorem gives
$$
\int_{\partial D} g(z)\frac{f'(z)}{f(z)}\,dz
=2\pi i\sum_{j=1}^{n} g(z_j).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let
$$
D=\{z\in\mathbb C: |z|<1,\ \operatorname{Re}z>0,\ \operatorname{Im}z>0\}.
$$
Construct a conformal holomorphic map from $D$ onto the unit disk $D(0,1)$.

#proof
The domain $D$ is the quarter unit disk in the first quadrant.

First use
$$
w=z^2.
$$
This maps $D$ conformally onto the upper half of the unit disk:
$$
\{w: |w|<1,\ \operatorname{Im}w>0\}.
$$

Next apply
$$
T(w)=i\frac{1-w}{1+w}.
$$
This maps the unit disk to the upper half-plane. On the upper half-disk, it maps the boundary diameter $(-1,1)$ to the positive imaginary axis and the semicircular boundary to the positive real axis. Hence it maps the upper half-disk onto the first quadrant.

Squaring maps the first quadrant onto the upper half-plane:
$$
\eta=T(w)^2.
$$
Finally, the Cayley transform
$$
C(\eta)=\frac{\eta-i}{\eta+i}
$$
maps the upper half-plane conformally onto the unit disk.

Therefore one conformal map from $D$ onto $D(0,1)$ is
$$

F(z)=
\frac{\left(i\frac{1-z^2}{1+z^2}\right)^2-i}
{\left(i\frac{1-z^2}{1+z^2}\right)^2+i}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $D$ be a simply connected domain in $\mathbb C$ and $z_0\in D$. Let $F$ be the set of all functions
$$
f:D\to D(0,1)
$$
such that:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** $f(z_0)=0$;

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** $f'(z_0)>0$;

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(iii)** $f$ is one-to-one.

Prove that $F$ is not empty.

#proof
As stated, the problem needs the usual assumption that $D\neq\mathbb C$. If $D=\mathbb C$, then any bounded entire function is constant by Liouville's theorem, so no one-to-one map $D\to D(0,1)$ exists.

Assume now that $D$ is a proper simply connected domain. By the Riemann mapping theorem, there exists a conformal bijection
$$
\phi:D\to D(0,1).
$$
Let
$$
a=\phi(z_0)\in D(0,1).
$$
The disk automorphism
$$
M_a(w)=\frac{w-a}{1-\overline a w}
$$
maps $a$ to $0$. Hence
$$
g=M_a\circ \phi
$$
is one-to-one, maps $D$ into $D(0,1)$, and satisfies
$$
g(z_0)=0.
$$

Now $g'(z_0)\neq 0$. Choose $\theta\in\mathbb R$ so that
$$
e^{i\theta}g'(z_0)>0.
$$
Then
$$
f=e^{i\theta}g
$$
still maps $D$ one-to-one into $D(0,1)$, satisfies $f(z_0)=0$, and has
$$
f'(z_0)>0.
$$
Thus $f\in F$, so $F$ is not empty.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $u(z)$ be harmonic in
$$
D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}
$$
such that
$$
\lim_{z\to 0}\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}=0.
$$
Prove that $u$ can be extended to be harmonic in $D(0,1)$.

#proof
An isolated singularity of a harmonic function has the form
$$
u(z)=c\log|z|+v(z)
$$
near $0$, where $v$ is harmonic in a full neighborhood of $0$. The constant $c$ is the coefficient of the logarithmic singularity.

Dividing by $\log|z|$, we get
$$
\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}
=c+\frac{v(z)}{\log|z|}.
$$
Since $v$ is harmonic near $0$, it is bounded near $0$. Also
$$
\log|z|\to -\infty
\qquad (z\to 0).
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{v(z)}{\log|z|}\to 0.
$$
The hypothesis says
$$
\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}\to 0,
$$
so necessarily $c=0$.

Thus the logarithmic singularity is absent, and $u=v$ near $0$. Hence $u$ extends harmonically across $0$ to all of $D(0,1)$.
::
