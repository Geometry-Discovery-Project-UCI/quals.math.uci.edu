::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Find the line integral
$$
\int_\gamma z\sin z\,dz,
$$
where $\gamma$ is the curve from $-1$ to $1$ taken along a semicircle.

#proof

The integrand $z\sin z$ is entire, so the integral is independent of path. A primitive is
$$
F(z)=\sin z-z\cos z,
$$
because
$$
F'(z)=\cos z-\cos z+z\sin z=z\sin z.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_\gamma z\sin z\,dz
=F(1)-F(-1).
$$
Now
$$
F(1)=\sin 1-\cos 1,
$$
and
$$
F(-1)=\sin(-1)-(-1)\cos(-1)=-\sin 1+\cos 1.
$$
Thus
$$
\int_\gamma z\sin z\,dz=2\sin 1-2\cos 1.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

A domain $\Omega$ in $\mathbb C$ is said to be holomorphically simply connected if for any holomorphic function $f$ on $\Omega$ and any simple closed piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma$ in $\Omega$, one has
$$
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=0.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that
$$
\Omega=\{z=x+iy\in\mathbb C:y>x\}
$$
is holomorphically simply connected.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Prove that $\Omega=D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ is not holomorphically simply connected.

#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The domain
$$
\Omega=\{x+iy:y>x\}
$$
is an open half-plane. In particular, it is convex. Every convex domain is simply connected, and every holomorphic function on a simply connected domain has a primitive.

Thus, if $f$ is holomorphic on $\Omega$, there exists a holomorphic function $F$ on $\Omega$ such that
$$
F'=f.
$$
For every closed piecewise $C^1$ curve $\gamma$ in $\Omega$,
$$
\int_\gamma f(z)\,dz=\int_\gamma F'(z)\,dz=0.
$$
Therefore $\Omega$ is holomorphically simply connected.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let
$$
\Omega=D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}.
$$
The function
$$
f(z)=\frac1z
$$
is holomorphic on $\Omega$. Let $\gamma(t)=\frac12 e^{it}$ for $0\le t\le 2\pi$. Then $\gamma$ is a simple closed piecewise $C^1$ curve in $\Omega$, and
$$
\int_\gamma \frac1z\,dz=2\pi i\ne 0.
$$
Therefore $D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ is not holomorphically simply connected.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that the series
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{n(1+i)z}
$$
converges to a holomorphic function in a neighborhood of the point $z_0=i$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** If
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} e^{n(1+i)z}
$$
is represented as a power series
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_n(z-i)^n,
$$
what is its radius of convergence?

#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** This is a geometric series with ratio
$$
q(z)=e^{(1+i)z}.
$$
It converges precisely when
$$
|q(z)|<1.
$$
Writing $z=x+iy$, we have
$$
(1+i)z=(x-y)+i(x+y),
$$
so
$$
|e^{(1+i)z}|=e^{x-y}.
$$
Thus the series converges when
$$
x-y<0,
$$
or equivalently $y>x$.

Since $z_0=i$ satisfies $1>0$, the point $i$ lies in this half-plane. Hence the series converges uniformly on compact subsets of a neighborhood of $i$, and its sum is holomorphic there.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Where the geometric series converges,
$$
f(z)=\frac{1}{1-e^{(1+i)z}}.
$$
This meromorphic function has singularities when
$$
e^{(1+i)z}=1.
$$
Equivalently,
$$
(1+i)z=2\pi ik,
\qquad k\in\mathbb Z,
$$
so
$$
z=\frac{2\pi ik}{1+i}=\pi k(1+i).
$$
The singularity nearest to $i$ occurs at $z=0$, whose distance from $i$ is
$$
|i-0|=1.
$$
All other singularities $\pi k(1+i)$ are farther away. Therefore the radius of convergence of the Taylor series about $i$ is
$$
1.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Find an explicit conformal mapping of the domain
$$
U=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z-i|<\sqrt2\ \text{and}\ |z+i|<\sqrt2\}
$$
onto the unit disk.

#proof

The boundary circles
$$
|z-i|=\sqrt2
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
|z+i|=\sqrt2
$$
meet at $z=1$ and $z=-1$. Use the Mobius map
$$
w=\frac{z-1}{z+1},
$$
which sends $1$ to $0$ and $-1$ to $\infty$. Therefore the two boundary circles, both passing through $\pm1$, are mapped to two straight lines through the origin.

One checks that these lines are
$$
\arg w=\frac{3\pi}{4}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
\arg w=\frac{5\pi}{4}.
$$
The point $z=0\in U$ maps to $w=-1$, so $U$ is mapped to the sector
$$
\frac{3\pi}{4}<\arg w<\frac{5\pi}{4}.
$$
Rotate this sector by defining
$$
\zeta=e^{-3\pi i/4}w.
$$
Then the sector becomes
$$
0<\arg \zeta<\frac{\pi}{2}.
$$
Squaring maps this sector conformally onto the upper half-plane:
$$
\eta=\zeta^2.
$$
Finally, the Cayley transform
$$
\phi(\eta)=\frac{\eta-i}{\eta+i}
$$
maps the upper half-plane onto the unit disk.

Thus an explicit conformal map $U\to D(0,1)$ is
$$

\Phi(z)=
\frac{
\left(e^{-3\pi i/4}\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right)^2-i
}{
\left(e^{-3\pi i/4}\frac{z-1}{z+1}\right)^2+i
}.

$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

If $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ is an entire function, define a sequence of functions $f^{(n)}$ by
$$
f^{(1)}=f,\qquad f^{(2)}=f\circ f,\qquad f^{(3)}=f\circ f\circ f,\quad \ldots
$$
Prove or disprove: for any entire function $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$, the sequence of restrictions
$$
f^{(n)}|_{D(0,1)}
$$
forms a normal family.

#proof

The statement is false.

Take
$$
f(z)=2z.
$$
Then
$$
f^{(n)}(z)=2^n z
$$
up to the harmless indexing convention. Consider the family
$$
\{2^n z\}_{n\ge 1}
$$
on $D(0,1)$.

This family is not normal. For example, by Marty's criterion, a family of holomorphic functions is normal only if the spherical derivatives are locally bounded. The spherical derivative of $2^n z$ at $0$ is
$$
\frac{|(2^n z)'|}{1+|2^n z|^2}\bigg|_{z=0}=2^n,
$$
which is unbounded as $n\to\infty$.

Therefore the iterates of an arbitrary entire function need not form a normal family on the unit disk.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Suppose that $u:\mathbb C\to\mathbb R$ is a harmonic function such that $u|_{\mathbb R}=0$. Does it imply that $u\equiv0$?

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Suppose that $u:\mathbb C\to\mathbb R$ is a harmonic function such that $u|_{\partial D(0,1)}=0$. Does it imply that $u\equiv0$?

#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** No. Take
$$
u(x+iy)=y.
$$
This function is harmonic on $\mathbb C$, and on the real axis $y=0$, so
$$
u|_{\mathbb R}=0.
$$
But $u$ is not identically zero. Therefore the condition $u|_{\mathbb R}=0$ does not imply $u\equiv0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Yes. Since $u$ is harmonic on $\mathbb C$, it is harmonic on the closed unit disk. If
$$
u=0
$$
on $\partial D(0,1)$, then by uniqueness for the Dirichlet problem, or by the maximum and minimum principles,
$$
u=0
$$
throughout $D(0,1)$.

Harmonic functions are real analytic. Since $u$ vanishes on the open set $D(0,1)$, unique continuation implies that $u$ vanishes everywhere on $\mathbb C$. Thus
$$
u\equiv0.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $f$ be entire holomorphic such that
$$
|f(z)|^2=p_n(x,y),\qquad z=x+iy,
$$
where $p_n(x,y)$ is a polynomial in $x$ and $y$ of degree $n$. Prove that $f$ is a polynomial in $z$.

#proof

Since $p_n(x,y)$ is a polynomial of degree $n$, there is a constant $C>0$ such that
$$
|p_n(x,y)|\le C(1+|z|)^n
$$
for all sufficiently large $|z|$. Because
$$
|f(z)|^2=p_n(x,y),
$$
we get
$$
|f(z)|\le C_1(1+|z|)^{n/2}
$$
for some constant $C_1>0$.

Thus $f$ is an entire function of polynomial growth. We now use Cauchy's estimates. Write
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kz^k.
$$
For any $R>0$,
$$
|a_k|\le \frac{\max_{|z|=R}|f(z)|}{R^k}
\le \frac{C_1(1+R)^{n/2}}{R^k}.
$$
If $k>n/2$, then letting $R\to\infty$ gives
$$
a_k=0.
$$
Therefore all sufficiently high Taylor coefficients vanish. Hence $f$ is a polynomial in $z$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Let $f$ be holomorphic in
$$
D=D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}
$$
such that
$$
\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|\,d\theta\le 1
$$
for all $0<r<1$. Prove that $z=0$ is a removable singularity.

#proof

Since $f$ is holomorphic in the punctured disk, it has a Laurent expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty}a_mz^m
$$
for $0<|z|<1$.

We will show that all negative coefficients vanish. For $k\ge 1$,
$$
a_{-k}
=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=r} f(z)z^{k-1}\,dz.
$$
Put $z=re^{i\theta}$. Then $dz=ire^{i\theta}\,d\theta$, and
$$
|a_{-k}|
\le \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|r^k\,d\theta
\le \frac{r^k}{2\pi}.
$$
This holds for every $0<r<1$. Letting $r\to0$ gives
$$
a_{-k}=0.
$$
Thus all negative Laurent coefficients vanish, so the singularity at $0$ is removable.
::
