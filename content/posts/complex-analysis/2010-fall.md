# 2010 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Let $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ be distinct complex numbers contained in the disk $|z|<R$. Let $f$ be analytic in the closed disk $|z|\leq R$. Let
$$
Q(z)=(z-z_1)\cdots(z-z_n).
$$
Prove that
$$
P(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R} f(\zeta)
\frac{1-\frac{Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)}}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta
$$
is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$ having the same values as $f$ at $z_1,\ldots,z_n$.

#proof
For fixed $\zeta$ on $|\zeta|=R$, the expression
$$
\frac{1-\frac{Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)}}{\zeta-z}
=\frac{Q(\zeta)-Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)(\zeta-z)}
$$
is a polynomial in $z$ of degree at most $n-1$, because $Q(\zeta)-Q(z)$ vanishes when $z=\zeta$ and is divisible by $\zeta-z$.

Therefore $P(z)$ is an integral of polynomials in $z$ of degree at most $n-1$, so $P$ itself is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$.

Now evaluate $P$ at $z=z_j$. Since $Q(z_j)=0$,
$$
P(z_j)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z_j}\,d\zeta.
$$
By Cauchy's integral formula,
$$
P(z_j)=f(z_j).
$$
Thus $P$ interpolates $f$ at $z_1,\ldots,z_n$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Show that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^2+n^2}
$$
is a meromorphic function on $\mathbb C$.

#proof
The possible poles occur where
$$
z^2+n^2=0,
$$
that is,
$$
z=\pm in,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
Let $K$ be a compact subset of $\mathbb C$ avoiding all these points. For all sufficiently large $n$, uniformly for $z\in K$,
$$
|z^2+n^2|\geq \frac12 n^2.
$$
Hence
$$
\left|\frac{1}{z^2+n^2}\right|\leq \frac{2}{n^2}
$$
for all large $n$. Since $\sum 1/n^2$ converges, the series converges uniformly on $K$.

Therefore the sum is holomorphic away from the points $\pm in$. At each such point, only one term has a pole, and that pole is simple. Hence the sum is meromorphic on $\mathbb C$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $\mathcal F$ be a family of holomorphic functions on the unit disk such that for every $f\in\mathcal F$,
$$
\int_D |f(z)|(1-|z|)^2\,dA(z)\leq 1.
$$
Prove that $\mathcal F$ is a normal family.

#proof
It is enough to prove that $\mathcal F$ is locally uniformly bounded.

Fix a compact set $K\subset D(0,1)$. Choose $r<1$ such that $K\subset D(0,r)$, and choose $\rho>0$ so that for every $z\in K$, the disk $D(z,\rho)$ is contained in $D(0,(1+r)/2)$.

For $\zeta\in D(z,\rho)$,
$$
1-|\zeta|\geq 1-\frac{1+r}{2}=\frac{1-r}{2}.
$$
Since $|f|$ is subharmonic,
$$
|f(z)|\leq \frac{1}{\pi\rho^2}\int_{D(z,\rho)}|f(\zeta)|\,dA(\zeta).
$$
Using the lower bound for $(1-|\zeta|)^2$ on $D(z,\rho)$,
$$
\int_{D(z,\rho)}|f(\zeta)|\,dA(\zeta)
\leq \frac{4}{(1-r)^2}\int_D |f(\zeta)|(1-|\zeta|)^2\,dA(\zeta)
\leq \frac{4}{(1-r)^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
|f(z)|\leq \frac{4}{\pi\rho^2(1-r)^2}
$$
for all $z\in K$ and all $f\in\mathcal F$.

So $\mathcal F$ is locally uniformly bounded. By Montel's theorem, $\mathcal F$ is normal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Find an explicit conformal transformation of
$$
U=\{|z|>1\}\setminus[1,\infty)
$$
onto the unit disk.

#proof
First use
$$
w=\frac1z.
$$
This maps $U$ conformally onto the slit disk
$$
D(0,1)\setminus[0,1).
$$
Choose the square-root branch with argument in $(0,2\pi)$:
$$
\zeta=\sqrt w.
$$
This maps the slit disk onto the upper half unit disk
$$
\{\zeta:|\zeta|<1,\ \operatorname{Im}\zeta>0\}.
$$

Now use
$$
T(\zeta)=i\frac{1-\zeta}{1+\zeta},
$$
which maps the upper half unit disk onto the first quadrant. Squaring maps the first quadrant onto the upper half-plane, and
$$
C(\eta)=\frac{\eta-i}{\eta+i}
$$
maps the upper half-plane onto the unit disk.

Therefore one explicit conformal map is
$$

F(z)=
\frac{\left(i\frac{1-\sqrt{1/z}}{1+\sqrt{1/z}}\right)^2-i}
{\left(i\frac{1-\sqrt{1/z}}{1+\sqrt{1/z}}\right)^2+i},
$$
where the branch of $\sqrt{1/z}$ is chosen with argument in $(0,2\pi)$ after the slit is mapped to $[0,1)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Find, for $a>b>0$,
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}\,dx.
$$

#proof
Use partial fractions:
$$
\frac{1}{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}
=\frac{1}{a^2-b^2}
\left(\frac{1}{x^2+b^2}-\frac{1}{x^2+a^2}\right).
$$
The standard formula
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{x^2+c^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2c}e^{-c}
$$
gives
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}\,dx
=\frac{1}{a^2-b^2}
\left(\frac{\pi}{2b}e^{-b}-\frac{\pi}{2a}e^{-a}\right).
$$
Thus
$$
\displaystyle
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos x}{(x^2+a^2)(x^2+b^2)}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2(a^2-b^2)}
\left(\frac{e^{-b}}{b}-\frac{e^{-a}}{a}\right).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $U$ be an open subset of $\mathbb C$, let $f:U\to\mathbb C$, and let $z_0\in U$. Write $f=u+iv$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** Prove that if $f$ is complex differentiable at $z_0$, then $u,v$ satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations at $z_0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** Prove that if $f$ is complex differentiable and $f'(z)\neq 0$ in $U$, then $f$ is an orientation-preserving conformal map.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** Write $z_0=x_0+iy_0$. Taking the difference quotient along the real direction gives
$$
f'(z_0)=u_x(z_0)+iv_x(z_0).
$$
Taking it along the imaginary direction gives
$$
f'(z_0)=\frac{u_y(z_0)+iv_y(z_0)}{i}
=v_y(z_0)-iu_y(z_0).
$$
Equating real and imaginary parts,
$$
u_x=v_y,
\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$
These are the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** If $f'(z_0)\neq 0$, then near $z_0$,
$$
f(z_0+h)=f(z_0)+f'(z_0)h+o(h).
$$
Multiplication by the nonzero complex number $f'(z_0)$ is a rotation followed by a scaling. Such a linear map preserves angles and orientation. Therefore the differential of $f$ at each point is angle-preserving and orientation-preserving.

Hence $f$ is conformal and orientation-preserving wherever $f'\neq 0$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** State the mean value theorem for analytic functions and use the Cauchy integral formula to prove it.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** Prove that if $f=u+iv$ is analytic on an open subset $U\subset\mathbb C$, then $u$ and $v$ are harmonic.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(iii)** Let $U$ be an open subset of $\mathbb R^2$, and let $u:U\to\mathbb R$ be harmonic. Prove that if there is $p_0\in U$ such that
$$
u(p_0)=\inf_{x\in U}u(x),
$$
then $u$ is constant.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** If $f$ is analytic on a neighborhood of $\overline{D(a,r)}$, then
$$
f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+re^{i\theta})\,d\theta.
$$
Indeed, by Cauchy's integral formula,
$$
f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta-a|=r}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-a}\,d\zeta.
$$
With $\zeta=a+re^{i\theta}$, this becomes
$$
f(a)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(a+re^{i\theta})\,d\theta.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** Since $f$ is analytic, $u$ and $v$ satisfy the Cauchy-Riemann equations:
$$
u_x=v_y,
\qquad
u_y=-v_x.
$$
Differentiating,
$$
u_{xx}=v_{yx},
\qquad
u_{yy}=-v_{xy}.
$$
Since mixed partial derivatives agree,
$$
\Delta u=u_{xx}+u_{yy}=v_{yx}-v_{xy}=0.
$$
Similarly,
$$
\Delta v=v_{xx}+v_{yy}=0.
$$
Thus $u$ and $v$ are harmonic.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(iii)** A harmonic function satisfies the minimum principle. If $u$ attains its minimum at an interior point $p_0$, then $-u$ attains its maximum at $p_0$. By the maximum principle for harmonic functions, $-u$ is constant on the connected component of $U$ containing $p_0$, hence $u$ is constant there.

If $U$ is connected, this says $u$ is constant on all of $U$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n
$$
be a power series with radius of convergence $R=64$. Determine the region of convergence of the Laurent series
$$
\sum_{n=-\infty}^{-1}a_{|2n|}z^{3n}
+
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{3n}z^{2n}.
$$

#proof
Since the original radius of convergence is $64$,
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n}=\frac1{64}.
$$

For the positive-power part,
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{3n}z^{2n},
$$
the root test gives the limiting size
$$
|a_{3n}|^{1/n}|z|^2
\sim \left(\frac1{64}\right)^3|z|^2.
$$
Thus this part converges for
$$
|z|^2<64^3,
$$
or
$$
|z|<64^{3/2}=512.
$$

For the negative-power part, write $n=-k$, where $k\geq 1$. Then
$$
a_{|2n|}z^{3n}=a_{2k}z^{-3k}.
$$
The root test gives the limiting size
$$
|a_{2k}|^{1/k}|z|^{-3}
\sim \left(\frac1{64}\right)^2|z|^{-3}.
$$
Thus this part converges for
$$
|z|^3>64^2,
$$
or
$$
|z|>16.
$$

Therefore the Laurent series converges in the annulus
$$
16<|z|<512.
$$
::
