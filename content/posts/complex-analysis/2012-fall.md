::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Show that for $a>0$,
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos ax}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(1+a)}{4e^a}.
$$

#proof
Consider
$$
I=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iaz}}{(1+z^2)^2}\,dz.
$$
For $a>0$, close the contour in the upper half-plane. The only pole inside is the double pole at $z=i$.

Since
$$
(1+z^2)^2=(z-i)^2(z+i)^2,
$$
the residue at $z=i$ is
$$
\operatorname{Res}\left(\frac{e^{iaz}}{(z-i)^2(z+i)^2},i\right)
=\left.\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{e^{iaz}}{(z+i)^2}\right)\right|_{z=i}.
$$
Now
$$
\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{e^{iaz}}{(z+i)^2}\right)
=\frac{iae^{iaz}}{(z+i)^2}-\frac{2e^{iaz}}{(z+i)^3}.
$$
At $z=i$,
$$
e^{iai}=e^{-a},
\qquad
z+i=2i.
$$
Thus the residue is
$$
\frac{iae^{-a}}{(2i)^2}-\frac{2e^{-a}}{(2i)^3}
=-\frac{ia e^{-a}}4-\frac{2e^{-a}}{-8i}
=-\frac{i(a+1)e^{-a}}4.
$$
Therefore
$$
I=2\pi i\left(-\frac{i(a+1)e^{-a}}4\right)
=\frac{\pi(a+1)e^{-a}}2.
$$
Taking real parts and using evenness,
$$
I=2\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos ax}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx.
$$
Hence
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos ax}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(a+1)e^{-a}}4
=\frac{\pi(1+a)}{4e^a}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is analytic in an annulus
$$
A(0,r,R)=\{z\in\mathbb C:r<|z|<R\},
$$
and there exists a sequence of polynomials $p_n$ converging to $f$ uniformly on compact subsets of $A(0,r,R)$. Show that $f$ is analytic on the disk $D(0,R)$.

#proof
Write the Laurent expansion of $f$ in the annulus:
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_kz^k.
$$
We prove that all negative coefficients vanish.

Fix $\rho$ with $r<\rho<R$. For $m<0$,
$$
a_m=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=\rho}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta^{m+1}}\,d\zeta.
$$
Since $p_n\to f$ uniformly on $|\zeta|=\rho$,
$$
a_m=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=\rho}\frac{p_n(\zeta)}{\zeta^{m+1}}\,d\zeta.
$$
Each $p_n$ is a polynomial, so this integral is $0$ for every $m<0$. Hence $a_m=0$ for all $m<0$.

Thus the Laurent expansion has no principal part and is a power series
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kz^k
$$
with radius of convergence at least $R$. Therefore $f$ extends analytically to $D(0,R)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $P(z)$ be a polynomial. Assume that
$$
P(z)\neq 0
$$
for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$. Show that
$$
P'(z)\neq 0
$$
for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.

#proof
The intended statement is for nonconstant polynomials. A nonzero constant polynomial would have $P'\equiv 0$.

Assume $P$ is nonconstant. Since $P$ has no zeros in the right half-plane, every zero of $P$ lies in the closed left half-plane
$$
\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0\}.
$$
By the Gauss-Lucas theorem, every zero of $P'$ lies in the convex hull of the zeros of $P$.

The closed left half-plane is convex, so the convex hull of the zeros of $P$ is still contained in
$$
\{z:\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0\}.
$$
Therefore $P'$ has no zeros in the right half-plane. Hence
$$
P'(z)\neq 0
$$
whenever $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Determine the number of roots, counted with multiplicity, of
$$
2z^5-6z^2+z+1=0
$$
in the annulus
$$
A(0,1,2)=\{z\in\mathbb C:1<|z|<2\}.
$$

#proof
Let
$$
P(z)=2z^5-6z^2+z+1.
$$
On $|z|=2$,
$$
|2z^5|=64,
$$
while
$$
|-6z^2+z+1|\leq 24+2+1=27.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $P$ has the same number of zeros in $|z|<2$ as $2z^5$, namely $5$.

On $|z|=1$,
$$
|-6z^2|=6,
$$
while
$$
|2z^5+z+1|\leq 2+1+1=4.
$$
Again by Rouche's theorem, $P$ has the same number of zeros in $|z|<1$ as $-6z^2$, namely $2$.

The inequalities are strict, so there are no zeros on the boundary circles. Therefore the number of zeros in $1<|z|<2$ is
$$
5-2=3.
$$
Thus the answer is
$$
3.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $f$ be analytic on the upper half-plane and satisfy
$$
|f(z)|<1.
$$
Furthermore suppose $f(i)=0$. Give an upper bound for $|f'(i)|$ and state which functions realize the extremum.

#proof
Use the conformal map
$$
\phi(z)=\frac{z-i}{z+i},
$$
which maps the upper half-plane to the unit disk and sends $i$ to $0$. Its derivative is
$$
\phi'(z)=\frac{2i}{(z+i)^2},
$$
so
$$
|\phi'(i)|=\frac12.
$$

Let
$$
g=f\circ \phi^{-1}.
$$
Then $g:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ is holomorphic and $g(0)=0$. By the Schwarz lemma,
$$
|g'(0)|\leq 1.
$$
Since
$$
|g'(0)|=\frac{|f'(i)|}{|\phi'(i)|}=2|f'(i)|,
$$
we get
$$
|f'(i)|\leq \frac12.
$$

Equality holds exactly when
$$
g(w)=e^{i\theta}w.
$$
Thus the extremal functions are
$$

f(z)=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-i}{z+i},
\qquad \theta\in\mathbb R.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $f$ be an entire holomorphic function on $\mathbb C$ such that
$$
|f(e^z)|\leq |e^z|
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$. Prove that
$$
f(z)\equiv az
$$
for some constant $a$ with $|a|\leq 1$.

#proof
Since $e^z$ ranges over $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, the hypothesis is equivalent to
$$
|f(w)|\leq |w|
$$
for all $w\neq 0$.

By continuity,
$$
f(0)=0.
$$
Thus
$$
g(w)=\frac{f(w)}{w}
$$
has a removable singularity at $0$ and extends to an entire function. For $w\neq 0$,
$$
|g(w)|\leq 1.
$$
By Liouville's theorem, $g$ is constant. Therefore
$$
f(w)=aw
$$
for some constant $a$ with $|a|\leq 1$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $f$ be holomorphic in
$$
D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}.
$$
If
$$
\int_{D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}} |f(z)|^3\,dA(z)<\infty,
$$
then prove that $z=0$ is a removable singularity of $f$.

#proof
The isolated singularity at $0$ is either removable, a pole, or essential. We rule out the last two possibilities.

If $0$ were a pole of order $m\geq 1$, then near $0$,
$$
|f(z)|\sim C|z|^{-m}
$$
for some $C>0$. Hence
$$
|f(z)|^3\sim C^3|z|^{-3m}.
$$
In polar coordinates,
$$
\int_{|z|<\varepsilon}|f(z)|^3\,dA(z)
$$
would have the same convergence behavior as
$$
\int_0^\varepsilon r^{-3m}r\,dr
=\int_0^\varepsilon r^{1-3m}\,dr.
$$
This diverges for every integer $m\geq 1$.

An essential singularity is also impossible by the standard $L^p$ isolated-singularity theorem: if a holomorphic function on a punctured disk is in $L^p$ near the puncture, then the pole order must be strictly less than $2/p$. Here $p=3$, so any possible pole order would have to be less than $2/3$, forcing order $0$.

Therefore the singularity is removable.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Find the largest set in $\mathbb C$ where the Laurent series
$$
\sum_{j=-\infty}^{\infty}2^jz^{j^3}
$$
converges.

#proof
Split the series into its positive and negative tails.

For $j\to+\infty$, the terms are
$$
2^jz^{j^3}.
$$
Taking the $j$th root of the absolute value gives
$$
2|z|^{j^2}.
$$
This tends to $0$ if $|z|<1$, and it fails to tend to $0$ if $|z|\geq 1$. Hence the positive tail converges only for
$$
|z|<1.
$$

For $j=-k\to-\infty$, the terms are
$$
2^{-k}z^{-k^3}.
$$
Taking the $k$th root of the absolute value gives
$$
\frac12 |z|^{-k^2}.
$$
This tends to $0$ if $|z|>1$, and it fails to tend to $0$ if $|z|\leq 1$.

Thus the positive tail requires $|z|<1$, while the negative tail requires $|z|>1$. No complex number satisfies both conditions. Therefore the Laurent series converges nowhere:
$$
D=\varnothing.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Let $u$ be a real-valued harmonic function in $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. Show that
$$
u(z)=c\log|z|+\operatorname{Re} f(z)
$$
for some real constant $c$ and a holomorphic function $f$ on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$.

#proof
The only possible obstruction to having a global harmonic conjugate on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ is the period around a loop enclosing the origin.

Let
$$
c=\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{|z|=1}\frac{\partial u}{\partial n}\,ds.
$$
The function
$$
v(z)=u(z)-c\log|z|
$$
is harmonic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. The choice of $c$ makes the period of the conjugate differential of $v$ around the unit circle equal to $0$. Since every closed curve in $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ has winding number an integer multiple of the unit circle, all periods vanish.

Therefore $v$ has a single-valued harmonic conjugate $w$ on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. Hence
$$
f=v+iw
$$
is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ and
$$
\operatorname{Re}f=v.
$$
Thus
$$
u(z)=c\log|z|+\operatorname{Re}f(z),
$$
as required.
