# 2019 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Find all points where
$$
f(z)=\overline z^{\,2}+3z
$$
is analytic.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Let $D$ be a domain in $\mathbb C$ and let $f$ be a $C^1$ function on $D$ such that
$$
\int_{\partial D(z_0,r)} f(z)\,dz=0
$$
for every $z_0\in D$ and every $0<r<\delta(z_0)=\operatorname{dist}(z_0,\partial D)$. Prove that $f$ is holomorphic in $D$.

#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Write $z=x+iy$. Then
$$
f(z)=\overline z^{\,2}+3z=(x-iy)^2+3(x+iy).
$$
The Wirtinger derivative is
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial \overline z}=2\overline z.
$$
Thus the Cauchy-Riemann equations hold only when
$$
\overline z=0,
$$
that is, at $z=0$.

However, being analytic at a point means holomorphic in a neighborhood of that point. Since the Cauchy-Riemann equations fail in every punctured neighborhood of $0$, the function is holomorphic on no nonempty open set. Thus it is analytic nowhere.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Since $f$ is $C^1$, Green's theorem gives
$$
\int_{\partial D(z_0,r)} f(z)\,dz
=2i\iint_{D(z_0,r)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \overline z}\,dA.
$$
The left-hand side is zero for every sufficiently small disk. Therefore
$$
\iint_{D(z_0,r)}\frac{\partial f}{\partial \overline z}\,dA=0
$$
for every such disk.

Since $\partial f/\partial\overline z$ is continuous, this implies
$$
\frac{\partial f}{\partial\overline z}=0
$$
throughout $D$. Hence $f$ satisfies the Cauchy-Riemann equations and is holomorphic in $D$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Let $f:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ be holomorphic such that
$$
f(0)=5^{-20}.
$$
Give a sharp estimate for the number of zeros of $f$ in
$$
D(0,1/5).
$$

#proof

Let $a_1,\ldots,a_N$ be the zeros of $f$ in $D(0,1/5)$, counted with multiplicity. Jensen's formula and the bound $|f|\le1$ imply
$$
|f(0)|\le \prod_{j=1}^N |a_j|.
$$
Since $|a_j|<1/5$,
$$
\prod_{j=1}^N |a_j|<5^{-N}.
$$
But
$$
|f(0)|=5^{-20}.
$$
Therefore
$$
5^{-20}<5^{-N},
$$
so
$$
N<20.
$$
Thus
$$
N\le19
$$
for the open disk $D(0,1/5)$.

If the intended disk is the closed disk $|z|\le1/5$, then the sharp bound is $20$, achieved by a finite Blaschke product with twenty zeros at $|z|=1/5$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Given the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\left(\frac{2019+i}{2019-i}\right)^{n^2}
\left(\frac{z-2019}{z+2019}\right)^n,
$$
find:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(i)** all $z$ such that the series converges absolutely;

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(ii)** all $z$ such that the series converges.

#proof

Let
$$
\lambda=\frac{2019+i}{2019-i}.
$$
Then $|\lambda|=1$, and $\lambda$ is not a root of unity. Put
$$
w=\frac{z-2019}{z+2019}.
$$
The terms have modulus
$$
|\lambda^{n^2}w^n|=|w|^n.
$$
Thus the series converges absolutely exactly when
$$
|w|<1.
$$
This is equivalent to
$$
|z-2019|<|z+2019|,
$$
which is the right half-plane
$$
\operatorname{Re}z>0.
$$

If $|w|>1$, the terms do not tend to zero, so the series diverges. If $|w|=1$, then the terms are
$$
\lambda^{n^2}w^n,
$$
which do not tend to zero in general; in fact the phase is a nonconstant quadratic rotation and has no limit to zero. Hence the series does not converge on the boundary.

Therefore both absolute convergence and convergence occur exactly for
$$
\operatorname{Re}z>0.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Prove that
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi^3}{8}.
$$

#proof

For $0<a<2$, define
$$
I(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
The standard beta-integral gives
$$
I(a)=\frac{\pi}{2}\csc\left(\frac{\pi a}{2}\right).
$$
Differentiate twice under the integral sign:
$$
I''(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
At $a=1$,
$$
I''(1)=\int_0^\infty\frac{(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
Since
$$
I(a)=\frac{\pi}{2}\csc\left(\frac{\pi a}{2}\right),
$$
a direct differentiation gives
$$
I''(1)=\frac{\pi^3}{8}.
$$
Hence
$$

\int_0^\infty \frac{(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi^3}{8}.

$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

Prove or disprove: there exists a sequence of holomorphic functions $\{f_n\}$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
f_n(z)\to |z|^2
$$
uniformly on a nonempty open subset of $D(0,1)$.

#proof

This is impossible.

If $f_n$ converges uniformly on a nonempty open set $U$ to $|z|^2$, then by the Weierstrass theorem the limit must be holomorphic on $U$. But
$$
|z|^2=z\overline z
$$
is not holomorphic on any nonempty open set. Indeed,
$$
\frac{\partial}{\partial\overline z}|z|^2=z,
$$
which cannot vanish on a nonempty open set.

Therefore no such sequence exists.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Suppose $f$ is a nonconstant entire function satisfying
$$
|f(z)|\ge1
$$
whenever $|z|\ge10$. Prove that $f$ is a polynomial.

#proof

The inequality implies that $f$ has no zeros outside $D(0,10)$. Hence $f$ has only finitely many zeros in $\mathbb C$.

Let $P$ be the polynomial whose zeros, with multiplicity, are exactly the zeros of $f$. Then
$$
g=\frac{P}{f}
$$
is entire. Outside a large disk,
$$
|g(z)|\le |P(z)|.
$$
Thus $g$ has polynomial growth. By Cauchy's estimates, $g$ is a polynomial.

Since
$$
f=\frac{P}{g}
$$
is entire and rational, it must be a polynomial.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $f:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ be a proper holomorphic map, continuous on $\overline{D(0,1)}$. Prove that $f$ is a rational function.

#proof

A proper holomorphic self-map of the disk is a finite Blaschke product.

Indeed, properness implies that each value in $D(0,1)$ has finitely many preimages, counted with multiplicity. In particular, $f$ has finitely many zeros $a_1,\ldots,a_N$ in $D(0,1)$. Also, continuity on the closed disk and properness imply
$$
|f(e^{it})|=1
$$
on the boundary.

Let
$$
B(z)=\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{z-a_j}{1-\overline{a_j}z}
$$
with the zeros repeated according to multiplicity. Then $f/B$ is holomorphic and zero-free in the disk, continuous on the boundary, and has modulus $1$ on the boundary. By the maximum modulus principle applied to $f/B$ and its reciprocal,
$$
\frac{f}{B}
$$
is constant of modulus $1$.

Thus
$$
f(z)=c\prod_{j=1}^{N}\frac{z-a_j}{1-\overline{a_j}z},
\qquad |c|=1.
$$
This is rational.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that
$$
F(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
-\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}
$$
is entire.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Prove that for $z\notin\mathbb Z$,
$$
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
=\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}.
$$

#proof

The series
$$
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
$$
converges normally on compact subsets of $\mathbb C\setminus\mathbb Z$, since the terms are $O(k^{-2})$ uniformly on compact sets away from the integers. Hence it defines a meromorphic function with double poles at the integers.

At each integer, the principal part of
$$
\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}
$$
is the same as the principal part of the corresponding term in the series, namely
$$
\frac1{(z-m)^2}.
$$
Therefore the difference
$$
F(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
-\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}
$$
has removable singularities at every integer. Hence $F$ is entire.

Both terms are periodic with period $1$, so $F$ is entire and periodic. On a vertical strip, the two terms are bounded away from the removable singularities, and after removability $F$ is bounded on the whole strip. By periodicity, $F$ is bounded on $\mathbb C$. Liouville's theorem implies that $F$ is constant.

Finally, as $\operatorname{Im}z\to+\infty$, both
$$
\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
$$
and
$$
\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}
$$
tend to $0$. Therefore the constant is $0$, and
$$

\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}\frac1{(k+z)^2}
=\frac{\pi^2}{\sin^2(\pi z)}.

$$
::
