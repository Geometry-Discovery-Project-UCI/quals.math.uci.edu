# 2013 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Describe all entire functions $f:\mathbb C\to\mathbb C$ satisfying:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $f(1/n)=f(-1/n)=1/n^2$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$;

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** $f(1/n)=f(-1/n)=1/n^3$ for all $n\in\mathbb N$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The function $z^2$ has the required values:
$$
\left(\frac1n\right)^2=\left(-\frac1n\right)^2=\frac1{n^2}.
$$
If $f$ is another entire function with the same values, then
$$
f(z)-z^2
$$
has zeros at $z=1/n$ and $z=-1/n$ for all $n$. These zeros accumulate at $0$, which lies in the domain of analyticity. By the identity theorem,
$$
f(z)-z^2\equiv 0.
$$
Thus the only function is
$$
f(z)=z^2.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** There is no such entire function.

Indeed, the condition
$$
f(1/n)=f(-1/n)
$$
for all $n$ implies, by the identity theorem, that
$$
f(z)=f(-z)
$$
for all $z$. Hence $f$ is even, so there is an entire function $h$ such that
$$
f(z)=h(z^2).
$$
The value condition gives
$$
h\left(\frac1{n^2}\right)=\frac1{n^3}.
$$
Since $1/n^2\to 0$, we get $h(0)=0$. Also
$$
\frac{h(1/n^2)}{1/n^2}
=\frac{1/n^3}{1/n^2}
=\frac1n\to 0,
$$
so $h'(0)=0$. Therefore
$$
h(w)=O(w^2)
\qquad (w\to 0).
$$
But with $w=1/n^2$,
$$
h(w)=\frac1{n^3}=w^{3/2},
$$
and
$$
\frac{h(w)}{w^2}=n\to\infty,
$$
contradicting $h(w)=O(w^2)$. Thus no entire function satisfies part **(b)**.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is an entire function such that
$$
\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{|f(z)|}{|z|}=0.
$$
Prove that $f$ is constant.

#proof
The hypothesis says $f(z)=o(z)$ as $z\to\infty$. Let
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kz^k
$$
be the Taylor expansion at $0$.

Fix $\varepsilon>0$. For sufficiently large $R$,
$$
|f(z)|\leq \varepsilon |z|
$$
whenever $|z|=R$. By Cauchy's estimate,
$$
|a_k|\leq \frac{\max_{|z|=R}|f(z)|}{R^k}
\leq \varepsilon R^{1-k}.
$$
For $k=1$, this gives $|a_1|\leq \varepsilon$, so $a_1=0$ after letting $\varepsilon\to 0$.

For $k\geq 2$, letting $R\to\infty$ gives
$$
|a_k|\leq \varepsilon R^{1-k}\to 0,
$$
so $a_k=0$. Hence all coefficients except possibly $a_0$ vanish, and $f$ is constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Describe explicitly the automorphism group
$$
\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}).
$$

#proof
Every automorphism of $\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$ extends to a Möbius transformation of the Riemann sphere that permutes the three punctures
$$
0,\quad 1,\quad \infty.
$$
Conversely, every Möbius transformation that permutes these three points restricts to an automorphism of $\mathbb C\setminus\{0,1\}$.

Thus the automorphism group is isomorphic to $S_3$ and consists of the six maps
$$

z,\quad
1-z,\quad
\frac1z,\quad
\frac{1}{1-z},\quad
\frac{z}{z-1},\quad
\frac{z-1}{z}.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Evaluate
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{1+x^5}\,dx.
$$

#proof
For $0<a<5$,
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x^5}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{5}\csc\left(\frac{\pi a}{5}\right).
$$
Taking $a=1$ gives
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{1}{1+x^5}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{5}\csc\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\displaystyle
\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{1+x^5}
=\frac{\pi}{5}\csc\left(\frac{\pi}{5}\right).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Prove that
$$
u(x,y)=y\cos y\sinh x+x\sin y\cosh x
$$
is harmonic in $\mathbb R^2$ and find its harmonic conjugate.

#proof
Observe that
$$
z\sinh z=(x+iy)(\sinh x\cos y+i\cosh x\sin y).
$$
Its imaginary part is
$$
\operatorname{Im}(z\sinh z)
=x\cosh x\sin y+y\sinh x\cos y.
$$
This is exactly
$$
u(x,y)=y\cos y\sinh x+x\sin y\cosh x.
$$
Since $z\sinh z$ is entire, its real and imaginary parts are harmonic. Therefore $u$ is harmonic on $\mathbb R^2$.

If $F(z)=z\sinh z$, then $u=\operatorname{Im}F$. To get an analytic function whose real part is $u$, take
$$
-iF(z).
$$
Thus a harmonic conjugate of $u$ is
$$
v(x,y)=-\operatorname{Re}(z\sinh z).
$$
Now
$$
\operatorname{Re}(z\sinh z)
=x\sinh x\cos y-y\cosh x\sin y.
$$
Hence
$$

v(x,y)=-x\sinh x\cos y+y\cosh x\sin y+C,
$$
where $C$ is a real constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
How many solutions does
$$
z^4+3z^2+z+1=0
$$
have in the closed upper half unit disk?

#proof
Let
$$
P(z)=z^4+3z^2+z+1.
$$
On $|z|=1$, compare
$$
3z^2+z^4
$$
with
$$
z+1.
$$
For $z=e^{i\theta}$,
$$
|z^2(z^2+3)|^2=|z^2+3|^2
=10+6\cos 2\theta,
$$
while
$$
|z+1|^2=2+2\cos\theta.
$$
The difference is
$$
10+6\cos 2\theta-(2+2\cos\theta)
=2(6\cos^2\theta-\cos\theta+1)>0.
$$
Thus
$$
|z^4+3z^2|>|z+1|
$$
on $|z|=1$.

By Rouche's theorem, $P$ has the same number of zeros in $|z|<1$ as
$$
z^4+3z^2=z^2(z^2+3).
$$
This function has exactly two zeros in $|z|<1$, both at $z=0$ counting multiplicity. Hence $P$ has exactly two zeros in the unit disk.

The polynomial has real coefficients. Also it has no real zeros in $[-1,1]$, since
$$
x^4+3x^2+x+1>0
$$
on that interval. Therefore the two zeros in the unit disk occur as a conjugate pair, one in the upper half-plane and one in the lower half-plane.

Thus the closed upper half unit disk contains exactly
$$
1
$$
solution.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Assume $z=0$ is an essential singularity of a holomorphic function $f$. Show that it is also an essential singularity of $f^2,f^3$, and in fact of $f^n$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$.

#proof
Fix $n\in\mathbb N$. Suppose, for contradiction, that $f^n$ does not have an essential singularity at $0$. Then the isolated singularity of $f^n$ is either removable or a pole.

If $f^n$ has a removable singularity at $0$, then $f^n$ is bounded near $0$. Hence $f$ is bounded near $0$, because
$$
|f|=|f^n|^{1/n}.
$$
Thus $f$ would have a removable singularity at $0$, contradicting the assumption that $0$ is essential.

If $f^n$ has a pole at $0$, then
$$
|f^n(z)|\to\infty
\qquad (z\to 0),
$$
so
$$
|f(z)|\to\infty.
$$
Therefore $f$ would have a pole at $0$, again a contradiction.

Thus $f^n$ can be neither removable nor a pole at $0$. Hence $0$ is an essential singularity of $f^n$ for every $n\in\mathbb N$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$ and
$$
f(1/z)=f(z)
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$. Suppose further that $f$ is real on the unit circle. Show that $f$ is real for all real $z\neq 0$.

#proof
Since $f$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$, it has a Laurent expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty}a_nz^n
$$
valid on $\mathbb C\setminus\{0\}$.

The identity $f(1/z)=f(z)$ gives
$$
a_n=a_{-n}
$$
for every integer $n$. Therefore
$$
f(z)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n(z^n+z^{-n}).
$$

On the unit circle, $z=e^{it}$, so
$$
f(e^{it})
=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n(e^{int}+e^{-int})
=a_0+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n\cos(nt).
$$
Since $f(e^{it})$ is real for all $t$, the Fourier coefficients must be real. Hence each $a_n$ is real.

Now let $x\in\mathbb R\setminus\{0\}$. Since all $a_n$ are real and
$$
x^n+x^{-n}\in\mathbb R,
$$
we have
$$
f(x)=a_0+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_n(x^n+x^{-n})\in\mathbb R.
$$
Thus $f$ is real for every real $x\neq 0$.
::
