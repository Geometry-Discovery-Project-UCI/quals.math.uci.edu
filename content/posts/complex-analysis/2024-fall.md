::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Find all real-valued harmonic functions $u$ on the whole complex plane such that for all $z=x+iy\in\mathbb C$,
$$
u(z)\le x^3-3xy^2+x+y.
$$

#proof

Observe that
$$
x^3-3xy^2=\operatorname{Re}(z^3)
$$
and
$$
x+y=\operatorname{Re}((1-i)z).
$$
Thus
$$
v(z)=x^3-3xy^2+x+y
=\operatorname{Re}\left(z^3+(1-i)z\right)
$$
is harmonic on $\mathbb C$.

Then
$$
w=u-v
$$
is harmonic on $\mathbb C$ and satisfies
$$
w(z)\le0
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$. An entire harmonic function bounded above is constant. Therefore
$$
w\equiv C
$$
for some real constant $C\le0$.

Hence all such functions are
$$

u(x+iy)=x^3-3xy^2+x+y+C,\qquad C\le0.

$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Let
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{n^n}{(n!)^2}z^n.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Prove that $f$ is an entire function.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Evaluate
$$
\int_{|z|=1} f\left(\sin\frac1z\right)\,dz.
$$

#proof

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Let
$$
a_n=\frac{n^n}{(n!)^2}.
$$
Using Stirling's formula $n!\sim n^ne^{-n}\sqrt{2\pi n}$, we get
$$
a_n^{1/n}\sim \frac{n}{(n/e)^2}=\frac{e^2}{n}\to0.
$$
Hence
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n}=0,
$$
so the radius of convergence is infinite. Therefore $f$ is entire.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** By the residue theorem,
$$
\int_{|z|=1} f\left(\sin\frac1z\right)\,dz
=2\pi i\operatorname{Res}_{z=0} f\left(\sin\frac1z\right).
$$
Now
$$
f(w)=w+\sum_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{n^n}{(n!)^2}w^n,
$$
and
$$
\sin\frac1z=\frac1z-\frac1{3!z^3}+\frac1{5!z^5}-\cdots.
$$
The coefficient of $z^{-1}$ comes only from the first term $w$ in $f(w)$. All powers $w^n$ with $n\ge2$ have only powers $z^{-k}$ with $k\ge2$.

Thus the residue is $1$, and
$$

\int_{|z|=1} f\left(\sin\frac1z\right)\,dz=2\pi i.

$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Let $P$ be a polynomial of degree $n$, and let
$$
M(r,P)=\sup_{|z|=r}|P(z)|,\qquad r>0.
$$
Prove that $r\mapsto M(r,P)$ is increasing and that
$$
r\mapsto \frac{M(r,P)}{r^n}
$$
is decreasing.

#proof

The function $r\mapsto M(r,P)$ is increasing by the maximum modulus principle. Indeed, if $0<r_1<r_2$, then the maximum of $|P|$ on $\overline{D(0,r_2)}$ is attained on $|z|=r_2$, so
$$
M(r_1,P)\le M(r_2,P).
$$

For the second assertion, define the reciprocal polynomial
$$
Q(z)=z^nP(1/z).
$$
This is a polynomial of degree at most $n$. For $\rho>0$,
$$
M(\rho,Q)=\sup_{|z|=\rho}|z^nP(1/z)|
=\rho^n M(1/\rho,P).
$$
Since $M(\rho,Q)$ is increasing in $\rho$, if $0<r_1<r_2$ then $1/r_2<1/r_1$, so
$$
\left(\frac1{r_2}\right)^nM(r_2,P)
\le
\left(\frac1{r_1}\right)^nM(r_1,P).
$$
Equivalently,
$$
\frac{M(r_2,P)}{r_2^n}
\le
\frac{M(r_1,P)}{r_1^n}.
$$
Thus $M(r,P)/r^n$ is decreasing.

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Prove that
$$
f(z)=\int_0^1\frac{\cos t}{t-z}\,dt+\int_2^3\frac{\sin t}{t-z}\,dt
$$
admits a Laurent expansion in the annulus
$$
\{1<|z|<2\}
$$
and compute this expansion.

#proof

For $0\le t\le1$ and $|z|>1$,
$$
\frac1{t-z}
=-\frac1z\frac1{1-t/z}
=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}t^nz^{-n-1}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_0^1\frac{\cos t}{t-z}\,dt
=-\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left(\int_0^1 t^n\cos t\,dt\right)z^{-n-1}.
$$

For $2\le t\le3$ and $|z|<2$,
$$
\frac1{t-z}
=\frac1t\frac1{1-z/t}
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{t^{n+1}}.
$$
Thus
$$
\int_2^3\frac{\sin t}{t-z}\,dt
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left(\int_2^3\frac{\sin t}{t^{n+1}}\,dt\right)z^n.
$$

Combining the two expansions, for $1<|z|<2$,
$$

f(z)=
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left(\int_2^3\frac{\sin t}{t^{n+1}}\,dt\right)z^n
-
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\left(\int_0^1 t^n\cos t\,dt\right)z^{-n-1}.

$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

Let $a$ and $b$ be two complex numbers and assume that $b$ is not equal to any integer $\le0$. Show that the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}
\frac{a(a+1)\cdots(a+n)}{b(b+1)\cdots(b+n)}z^n
$$
is at least $1$ and can be infinity in some cases.

#proof

Let
$$
c_n=\frac{a(a+1)\cdots(a+n)}{b(b+1)\cdots(b+n)}.
$$
Since $b$ is not a non-positive integer, none of the denominator factors is zero.

If the numerator does not eventually vanish, then
$$
\left|\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}\right|
=\left|\frac{a+n+1}{b+n+1}\right|\to1.
$$
Therefore the radius of convergence is $1$.

If the numerator eventually vanishes, then the series is actually a polynomial. This happens, for example, when
$$
a\in\{0,-1,-2,\ldots\}.
$$
In that case the radius of convergence is infinite.

Thus the radius of convergence is always at least $1$, and it can be infinity.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Let $\alpha<1$ and let $F$ be the family of all power series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nz^n
$$
with coefficients in $\mathbb C$ such that for all $n$,
$$
|a_n|\le e^{n^\alpha}.
$$
Show that such power series converge on the unit disk $D(0,1)$ and form a normal family on the unit disk. Is the statement still true if $\alpha=1$?

#proof

Assume $\alpha<1$. Then
$$
|a_n|^{1/n}\le e^{n^{\alpha-1}}\to1.
$$
Hence every such power series has radius of convergence at least $1$, so it converges in $D(0,1)$.

To prove normality, fix $0<r<1$. For $|z|\le r$,
$$
\left|\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nz^n\right|
\le
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{n^\alpha}r^n.
$$
Since $\alpha<1$, the series on the right converges. Therefore the family is uniformly bounded on every compact subset of $D(0,1)$. By Montel's theorem, it is a normal family.

If $\alpha=1$, the statement is false. Take
$$
a_n=e^n.
$$
Then
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}a_nz^n
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(ez)^n
$$
has radius of convergence $1/e$, so it does not even converge on the whole unit disk.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $f$ be holomorphic on the disk $D(0,R)$ for some $R>1$. Show that there exists $z_0\in\mathbb C$ with $|z_0|=1$ such that
$$
\left|f(z_0)-\frac1{z_0}\right|\ge1.
$$

#proof

Suppose, toward a contradiction, that
$$
\left|f(z)-\frac1z\right|<1
$$
for every $|z|=1$. Multiplying by $|z|=1$, this is equivalent to
$$
|zf(z)-1|<1
$$
on $|z|=1$.

Let
$$
g(z)=zf(z).
$$
Then $g$ is holomorphic in a neighborhood of the closed unit disk and $g(0)=0$.

On $|z|=1$,
$$
|g(z)-1|<1.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $g$ and the constant function $1$ have the same number of zeros in $D(0,1)$. The constant function $1$ has no zeros, so $g$ should have no zeros in the unit disk. But $g(0)=0$, a contradiction.

Therefore there exists $|z_0|=1$ such that
$$
\left|f(z_0)-\frac1{z_0}\right|\ge1.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Let $f$ be an entire function with $f(0)=1$, and suppose $f$ has at least $n$ zeros in the unit disk $D(0,1)$. Show that for each $R>0$, there exists a point $z_R$ on the boundary of $D(0,R)$ such that
$$
|f(z_R)|\ge R^n.
$$

#proof

Let
$$
M(R)=\max_{|z|=R}|f(z)|.
$$
If $0<R<1$, then by the maximum modulus principle,
$$
M(R)\ge |f(0)|=1>R^n.
$$
Thus the result holds for $0<R<1$.

Now assume $R\ge1$. Let $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ be $n$ zeros of $f$ in $D(0,1)$, counted with multiplicity. Then $|a_j|<1\le R$. Jensen's formula gives
$$
\log|f(0)|+\sum_{|a_j|<R}\log\frac{R}{|a_j|}
\le \log M(R).
$$
Since $f(0)=1$, this implies
$$
\log M(R)
\ge \sum_{j=1}^n\log\frac{R}{|a_j|}
> n\log R.
$$
Therefore
$$
M(R)>R^n.
$$
Hence there exists $z_R$ with $|z_R|=R$ such that
$$
|f(z_R)|=M(R)\ge R^n.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a bounded sequence of holomorphic functions on the unit disk $D=D(0,1)$ and let $\{z_m\}$ be a sequence in $D$ that converges in $D$. Assume that
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(z_m)
$$
exists for all $m$. Show that $\{f_n\}$ converges uniformly on compact subsets of $D$.

#proof

We use Vitali's theorem. The family $\{f_n\}$ is bounded on $D$, so it is locally bounded. By Montel's theorem, every subsequence has a further subsequence converging uniformly on compact subsets of $D$ to a holomorphic limit.

The pointwise limits
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(z_m)
$$
exist for all $m$, and the sequence $\{z_m\}$ has a limit point in $D$. Interpreting the hypothesis in the standard way, the points $z_m$ give a set with an accumulation point in $D$. Therefore any two subsequential holomorphic limits must agree on a set with an accumulation point. By the identity theorem, the two limits are identical.

Thus every subsequence of $\{f_n\}$ has a further subsequence converging to the same holomorphic limit. It follows that the whole sequence $\{f_n\}$ converges uniformly on compact subsets of $D$.
