# 2018 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Let $f$ be analytic on $D(z_0,r)\setminus\{z_0\}$, with $f(z)\ne0$ there. Let
$$
g(z)=\frac1{f(z)}.
$$
Prove that $f$ has an essential singularity at $z_0$ if and only if $g$ has an essential singularity at $z_0$.

#proof

An isolated singularity is removable, a pole, or essential.

If $f$ has a removable singularity at $z_0$, then either the removable value is nonzero, in which case $g=1/f$ is also removable, or the removable value is $0$, in which case $g$ has a pole.

If $f$ has a pole at $z_0$, then $1/f$ tends to $0$, so $g$ has a removable singularity.

Thus taking reciprocals interchanges poles and removable singularities, except for nonzero removable singularities which remain removable. Therefore the only remaining possibility is essential. Hence $f$ is essential at $z_0$ if and only if $g$ is essential at $z_0$.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Let $f$ and $g$ be entire functions. Suppose:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $g(z)\ne0$ for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** $|f(z)|\le |z^7g(z)|$ for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

Prove that there exists $\alpha\in\mathbb C$ with $|\alpha|\le1$ such that
$$
f(z)=\alpha z^7g(z)
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

#proof

Since $g$ has no zeros, the only possible zero of $z^7g(z)$ is at $z=0$, of order $7$. The inequality forces $f$ to vanish at $0$ to order at least $7$. Therefore
$$
h(z)=\frac{f(z)}{z^7g(z)}
$$
extends to an entire function.

For $z\ne0$,
$$
|h(z)|\le1.
$$
By removability this also holds at $0$. Hence $h$ is a bounded entire function. By Liouville's theorem, $h$ is constant:
$$
h\equiv\alpha,
\qquad |\alpha|\le1.
$$
Thus
$$
f(z)=\alpha z^7g(z).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Let $\{f_n\}$ be a uniformly bounded sequence of analytic functions on $D(0,1)$. Suppose
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(1/k)
$$
exists for $k=1,2,\ldots$. Prove that there exists an analytic function $f$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
f_n\to f
$$
uniformly on compact subsets of $D(0,1)$.

#proof

The family $\{f_n\}$ is uniformly bounded, hence locally bounded. By Montel's theorem, every subsequence has a further subsequence converging uniformly on compact subsets to a holomorphic function.

The set
$$
\{1/k:k=1,2,\ldots\}
$$
has an accumulation point at $0\in D(0,1)$. Since the limits $f_n(1/k)$ exist for every $k$, any two subsequential limits must agree at every point $1/k$. By the identity theorem, the two subsequential limits are the same holomorphic function.

Therefore all convergent subsequences have the same limit, and the whole sequence converges uniformly on compact subsets to that holomorphic function.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Find the number of solutions, counted with multiplicity, of
$$
e^z=7z^9
$$
in the open unit disk.

#proof

Consider
$$
F(z)=e^z-7z^9.
$$
On $|z|=1$,
$$
|e^z|\le e<7=|7z^9|.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $F(z)$ and $-7z^9$ have the same number of zeros in $D(0,1)$, counted with multiplicity. The function $-7z^9$ has exactly $9$ zeros there, counting multiplicity.

Thus the equation has
$$
9
$$
solutions in the open unit disk.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

Evaluate
$$
\int_\gamma \frac{1+z}{1-\cos z}\,dz
$$
when:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $\gamma$ is the circle of radius $5$ around $0$, counterclockwise.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** $\gamma$ is the circle of radius $7$ around $0$, counterclockwise.

#proof

The poles occur where
$$
1-\cos z=0,
$$
that is,
$$
z=2\pi k,\qquad k\in\mathbb Z.
$$
Each is a double pole. Near $a=2\pi k$,
$$
1-\cos z=\frac{(z-a)^2}{2}+O((z-a)^4),
$$
so
$$
\frac1{1-\cos z}=\frac{2}{(z-a)^2}+O(1).
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac{1+z}{1-\cos z}
=\frac{2(1+a)}{(z-a)^2}+\frac{2}{z-a}+O(1),
$$
so the residue at every pole $a=2\pi k$ is $2$.

For radius $5$, the only enclosed pole is $0$. Hence
$$
\int_\gamma \frac{1+z}{1-\cos z}\,dz
=2\pi i\cdot2
=4\pi i.
$$

For radius $7$, the enclosed poles are
$$
-2\pi,\quad 0,\quad 2\pi.
$$
The sum of residues is $6$, so
$$
\int_\gamma \frac{1+z}{1-\cos z}\,dz
=2\pi i\cdot6
=12\pi i.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Find a surjective holomorphic map $\phi$ from the open unit disk $D$ to the punctured disk $D^*=D\setminus\{0\}$, with
$$
\phi'(z)\ne0
$$
for every $z\in D$.

#proof

The map
$$
w=\frac{1+z}{1-z}
$$
maps $D$ conformally onto the right half-plane. Define
$$
\phi(z)=\exp\left(-\frac{1+z}{1-z}\right).
$$
Since $\operatorname{Re}w>0$, we have
$$
|\phi(z)|=e^{-\operatorname{Re}w}<1,
$$
and $\phi(z)\ne0$. The exponential maps the right half-plane, after the negative sign, onto the punctured unit disk, so $\phi$ is surjective onto $D^*$.

Also,
$$
\phi'(z)=\phi(z)\left(-\frac{2}{(1-z)^2}\right),
$$
which never vanishes in $D$. Thus this $\phi$ has the required properties.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $f$ be analytic on $\{z:|z|>0\}$ and suppose
$$
\lim_{z\to\infty}f(z)=0.
$$
Show that for $|z|>1$,
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\eta|=1}\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}\,d\eta
=-f(z).
$$

#proof

Fix $z$ with $|z|>1$. Let $R>|z|$. Apply the residue theorem to
$$
\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}
$$
on the annulus $1<|\eta|<R$. The boundary consists of $|\eta|=R$ positively oriented and $|\eta|=1$ negatively oriented. Thus
$$
\int_{|\eta|=R}\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}\,d\eta
-\int_{|\eta|=1}\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}\,d\eta
=2\pi i f(z).
$$
As $R\to\infty$, the outer integral tends to $0$ because $f(\eta)\to0$ and the denominator is of size $R$ on a circle of length $2\pi R$.

Therefore
$$
-\int_{|\eta|=1}\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}\,d\eta
=2\pi i f(z),
$$
or
$$
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|\eta|=1}\frac{f(\eta)}{\eta-z}\,d\eta
=-f(z).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Suppose $f:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ is holomorphic and
$$
f(0)=\frac15.
$$
Give an upper bound for $|f'(0)|$, and characterize the functions for which equality holds.

#proof

By Schwarz-Pick,
$$
|f'(0)|\le 1-|f(0)|^2.
$$
Since $f(0)=1/5$,
$$
|f'(0)|\le 1-\frac1{25}
=\frac{24}{25}.
$$
Equality holds exactly for disk automorphisms. Thus equality holds precisely when
$$
f(z)=\phi_{1/5}^{-1}(e^{i\theta}z),
\qquad \theta\in\mathbb R,
$$
where
$$
\phi_{1/5}(w)=\frac{w-1/5}{1-w/5}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem

Let
$$
u=\log(x^2+y^2).
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Find all harmonic conjugates of $u$ in an open ball of radius $1$ centered at $1$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Prove that there is no harmonic conjugate of $u$ in $U\setminus\{0\}$, where $U$ is any open set containing $0$.

#proof

Since
$$
u(z)=\log|z|^2=2\log|z|,
$$
a harmonic conjugate locally is
$$
v(z)=2\arg z+C.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The disk centered at $1$ of radius $1$ does not wind around $0$, so a single-valued branch of $\arg z$ exists there. Hence all harmonic conjugates are
$$

v(z)=2\operatorname{Arg}z+C,
\qquad C\in\mathbb R,

$$
where $\operatorname{Arg}$ is any chosen branch on that disk.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** If a harmonic conjugate existed on $U\setminus\{0\}$, then
$$
u+iv
$$
would be a holomorphic branch of
$$
2\log z
$$
there. But no single-valued branch of $\arg z$ exists on a punctured neighborhood of $0$: going once around $0$ changes $2\arg z$ by $4\pi$. Therefore no global harmonic conjugate exists on $U\setminus\{0\}$.
::
