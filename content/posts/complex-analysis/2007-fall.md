# 2007 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Prove Jordan's lemma. Let $f(z)$ be continuous in
$$
D=\{z\in\mathbb C: |z|\geq R_0,\ \operatorname{Im}z\geq 0\}
$$
and suppose $f(z)\to 0$ uniformly in $D$ as $z\to\infty$. Then for every $a>0$,
$$
\lim_{R\to\infty}\int_{\Gamma_R}e^{iaz}f(z)\,dz=0,
$$
where $\Gamma_R$ is the upper semicircle $|z|=R$.

#proof
Parametrize the upper semicircle by $z=Re^{i\theta}$, $0\leq\theta\leq\pi$. Then
$$
|e^{iaz}|=e^{-aR\sin\theta}.
$$
Hence
$$
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R}e^{iaz}f(z)\,dz\right|
\leq R\sup_{\Gamma_R}|f|\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin\theta}\,d\theta.
$$
Using $\sin\theta\geq 2\theta/\pi$ on $[0,\pi/2]$ and symmetry,
$$
\int_0^\pi e^{-aR\sin\theta}\,d\theta
\leq 2\int_0^{\pi/2}e^{-2aR\theta/\pi}\,d\theta
\leq \frac{\pi}{aR}.
$$
Therefore
$$
\left|\int_{\Gamma_R}e^{iaz}f(z)\,dz\right|
\leq \frac{\pi}{a}\sup_{\Gamma_R}|f|.
$$
Since $\sup_{\Gamma_R}|f|\to 0$, the integral tends to $0$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $f$ be holomorphic in the closed unit disk. Prove that, for $z\in D(0,1)$,
$$
f(z)=\frac1\pi\int_{D(0,1)}\frac{f(w)}{(1-z\overline w)^2}\,dA(w).
$$

#proof
Write
$$
f(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nw^n.
$$
Also,
$$
\frac{1}{(1-z\overline w)^2}
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}(n+1)z^n\overline w^{\,n}.
$$
Using orthogonality on the disk,
$$
\int_D w^m\overline w^{\,n}\,dA(w)
=
\begin{cases}
\displaystyle \frac{\pi}{n+1}, & m=n,\\
0, & m\neq n.
\end{cases}
$$
Therefore
$$
\frac1\pi\int_D\frac{f(w)}{(1-z\overline w)^2}\,dA(w)
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n
=f(z).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $\alpha,\beta,\gamma>0$. Find the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)\cdots(\alpha+n-1)\beta(\beta+1)\cdots(\beta+n-1)}
{n!\gamma(\gamma+1)\cdots(\gamma+n-1)}
z^n.
$$

#proof
Let
$$
c_n=\frac{(\alpha)_n(\beta)_n}{n!(\gamma)_n}.
$$
Then
$$
\frac{c_{n+1}}{c_n}
=\frac{(\alpha+n)(\beta+n)}{(n+1)(\gamma+n)}\to 1.
$$
By the ratio test, the radius of convergence is
$$
1.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Show that
$$
F(z)=\int_0^1\frac{e^{tz}}{1+t}\,dt
$$
is holomorphic in $\mathbb C$.

#proof
For each fixed $t\in[0,1]$, the function
$$
z\mapsto \frac{e^{tz}}{1+t}
$$
is entire. On every compact set $|z|\leq R$,
$$
\left|\frac{\partial}{\partial z}\frac{e^{tz}}{1+t}\right|
=\left|\frac{t e^{tz}}{1+t}\right|
\leq e^R.
$$
Thus differentiation under the integral sign is justified on compact sets. Hence
$$
F'(z)=\int_0^1\frac{t e^{tz}}{1+t}\,dt,
$$
and similarly all higher derivatives exist. Therefore $F$ is entire.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $f:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ be holomorphic. Prove that
$$
\left|\frac{f(z)-f(w)}{1-f(z)\overline{f(w)}}\right|
\leq
\left|\frac{z-w}{1-z\overline w}\right|,
\qquad z,w\in D(0,1).
$$

#proof
This is the Schwarz-Pick lemma. For fixed $w$, let
$$
\phi_w(z)=\frac{z-w}{1-\overline w z}
$$
and
$$
\phi_{f(w)}(\zeta)=\frac{\zeta-f(w)}{1-\overline{f(w)}\zeta}.
$$
Then
$$
g=\phi_{f(w)}\circ f\circ \phi_w^{-1}
$$
maps the unit disk into itself and satisfies $g(0)=0$. By Schwarz's lemma,
$$
|g(\xi)|\leq |\xi|.
$$
Taking $\xi=\phi_w(z)$ gives the desired inequality.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $\Omega\neq\mathbb C$ be a simply connected domain in $\mathbb C$. Let $f:\Omega\to\Omega$ be holomorphic and suppose $f$ fixes two distinct points $p,q\in\Omega$. Prove that
$$
f(z)\equiv z
$$
on $\Omega$.

#proof
By the Riemann mapping theorem, choose a conformal map
$$
\Phi:\Omega\to D(0,1).
$$
Then
$$
g=\Phi\circ f\circ \Phi^{-1}
$$
is a holomorphic self-map of the unit disk fixing the two distinct points $\Phi(p)$ and $\Phi(q)$.

An automorphism or self-map of the unit disk with two fixed points in the disk must be the identity. Indeed, conjugate one fixed point to $0$ and apply Schwarz's lemma; equality at another nonzero fixed point forces a rotation, and the second fixed point forces that rotation to be $1$.

Hence $g$ is the identity, so $f$ is the identity on $\Omega$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $a$ be real. Evaluate
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin ax}{\sinh x}\,dx.
$$

#proof
Use
$$
\frac1{\sinh x}=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}e^{-(2n+1)x},
\qquad x>0.
$$
Then
$$
\int_0^\infty e^{-bx}\sin(ax)\,dx
=\frac{a}{a^2+b^2}.
$$
Thus
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin ax}{\sinh x}\,dx
=2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a}{a^2+(2n+1)^2}.
$$
The standard partial fraction expansion for $\tanh$ gives
$$
2\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}\frac{a}{a^2+(2n+1)^2}
=\frac{\pi}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\pi a}{2}\right).
$$
Therefore
$$
\displaystyle
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin ax}{\sinh x}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2}\tanh\left(\frac{\pi a}{2}\right).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $f$ be analytic on $\mathbb C\setminus\{1\}$ and have a simple pole at $z=1$ with residue $\lambda$. Prove that for every $R>0$,
$$
\lim_{n\to\infty}
R^n\left|(-1)^n\frac{f^{(n)}(2)}{n!}-\lambda\right|=0.
$$

#proof
Write
$$
f(z)=\frac{\lambda}{z-1}+g(z),
$$
where $g$ is entire. Since
$$
\frac{d^n}{dz^n}\frac{\lambda}{z-1}
=\lambda(-1)^n\frac{n!}{(z-1)^{n+1}},
$$
we get at $z=2$:
$$
(-1)^n\frac{f^{(n)}(2)}{n!}
=\lambda+(-1)^n\frac{g^{(n)}(2)}{n!}.
$$
Thus it remains to show
$$
R^n\left|\frac{g^{(n)}(2)}{n!}\right|\to 0.
$$
Since $g$ is entire, Cauchy's estimates on the circle $|\zeta-2|=\rho$ give
$$
\left|\frac{g^{(n)}(2)}{n!}\right|
\leq \frac{M_\rho}{\rho^n}.
$$
Choose $\rho>R$. Then
$$
R^n\left|\frac{g^{(n)}(2)}{n!}\right|
\leq M_\rho\left(\frac{R}{\rho}\right)^n\to 0.
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=9}
#problem
Suppose $f$ is entire and
$$
|f(z)|\leq Be^{A|z|}
$$
for some positive constants $A,B$. Let $\omega_1,\omega_2,\ldots$ be the zeros of $f$, listed with multiplicity. Prove that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+|\omega_n|)^{-\alpha}<\infty
$$
for all $\alpha>1$.

#proof
By Jensen's formula, the number $N(r)$ of zeros of $f$ in $|z|\leq r$, counted with multiplicity, satisfies
$$
N(r)\leq C r
$$
for all sufficiently large $r$. This follows from the growth bound
$$
\log |f(z)|\leq \log B+A|z|.
$$
Thus the zero-counting function grows at most linearly.

Group the zeros into dyadic annuli:
$$
2^k\leq |\omega_n|<2^{k+1}.
$$
The number of zeros in this annulus is $O(2^k)$. Therefore
$$
\sum_{2^k\leq |\omega_n|<2^{k+1}}(1+|\omega_n|)^{-\alpha}
\leq C2^k(2^k)^{-\alpha}
=C2^{k(1-\alpha)}.
$$
Since $\alpha>1$,
$$
\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}2^{k(1-\alpha)}
$$
converges. Hence
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1+|\omega_n|)^{-\alpha}<\infty.
$$
::
