# 2006 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Show that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{z^2+n^2}
$$
is a meromorphic function on $\mathbb C$.

#proof
The possible poles are at
$$
z=\pm in,\qquad n=1,2,\ldots.
$$
On every compact set avoiding these points, for all sufficiently large $n$,
$$
|z^2+n^2|\geq \frac12 n^2.
$$
Hence
$$
\left|\frac1{z^2+n^2}\right|\leq \frac2{n^2},
$$
and the series converges uniformly by the Weierstrass $M$-test. Thus the sum is holomorphic away from the points $\pm in$. At each such point, only one summand has a simple pole. Therefore the sum is meromorphic on $\mathbb C$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Show that for $a>0$,
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos ax}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(1+a)}{4e^a}.
$$

#proof
Consider
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iaz}}{(1+z^2)^2}\,dz.
$$
For $a>0$, close the contour in the upper half-plane. There is a double pole at $z=i$. Since
$$
(1+z^2)^2=(z-i)^2(z+i)^2,
$$
the residue is
$$
\left.\frac{d}{dz}\left(\frac{e^{iaz}}{(z+i)^2}\right)\right|_{z=i}
=-\frac{i(a+1)e^{-a}}4.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iax}}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=2\pi i\left(-\frac{i(a+1)e^{-a}}4\right)
=\frac{\pi(a+1)e^{-a}}2.
$$
Taking real parts and using evenness gives
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos ax}{(1+x^2)^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi(a+1)e^{-a}}4
=\frac{\pi(1+a)}{4e^a}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Let $P(z)$ be a polynomial. Assume
$$
P(z)\neq 0
$$
for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$. Show that
$$
P'(z)\neq 0
$$
for $\operatorname{Re}z>0$.

#proof
For a nonconstant polynomial, all zeros of $P$ lie in the closed left half-plane. By the Gauss-Lucas theorem, the zeros of $P'$ lie in the convex hull of the zeros of $P$. Since the closed left half-plane is convex, that convex hull is still contained in
$$
\{\operatorname{Re}z\leq 0\}.
$$
Thus $P'$ has no zeros in the right half-plane.

If $P$ is constant, the statement needs to be interpreted separately, since then $P'\equiv 0$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
Let $z_1,\ldots,z_n$ be distinct points in $D(0,R)$, let $f$ be analytic in $\overline{D(0,R)}$, and let
$$
Q(z)=(z-z_1)\cdots(z-z_n).
$$
Prove that
$$
P(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}f(\zeta)
\frac{1-\frac{Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)}}{\zeta-z}\,d\zeta
$$
is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$ with
$$
P(z_j)=f(z_j).
$$

#proof
For fixed $\zeta$,
$$
\frac{1-\frac{Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)}}{\zeta-z}
=\frac{Q(\zeta)-Q(z)}{Q(\zeta)(\zeta-z)}
$$
is a polynomial in $z$ of degree at most $n-1$, because $Q(\zeta)-Q(z)$ is divisible by $\zeta-z$. Hence $P$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$.

If $z=z_j$, then $Q(z_j)=0$, so
$$
P(z_j)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\int_{|\zeta|=R}\frac{f(\zeta)}{\zeta-z_j}\,d\zeta
=f(z_j)
$$
by Cauchy's integral formula.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $f$ be analytic in $D(0,1)$ and suppose
$$
|f(z)-z|\leq 1
$$
on $|z|=1$. Show that
$$
|f'(1/2)|\leq \frac73.
$$

#proof
Let
$$
g(z)=f(z)-z.
$$
By the maximum modulus principle,
$$
|g(z)|\leq 1
$$
in the unit disk. The Schwarz-Pick derivative estimate for $g:D\to\overline D$ gives
$$
|g'(a)|\leq \frac{1}{1-|a|^2}.
$$
At $a=1/2$,
$$
|g'(1/2)|\leq \frac{1}{1-1/4}=\frac43.
$$
Since
$$
f'(z)=1+g'(z),
$$
we get
$$
|f'(1/2)|\leq 1+\frac43=\frac73.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let $a>1$ be real. Prove that
$$
ze^{a-z}=1
$$
has a single solution in $\overline{D(0,1)}$, and that this solution is real and positive.

#proof
Rewrite the equation as
$$
z-e^{z-a}=0.
$$
On $|z|=1$,
$$
|e^{z-a}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}z-a}\leq e^{1-a}<1=|z|.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $z-e^{z-a}$ and $z$ have the same number of zeros in $D(0,1)$, namely one.

For $x\in[0,1]$, the function
$$
h(x)=xe^{a-x}
$$
is strictly increasing because
$$
h'(x)=e^{a-x}(1-x)\geq 0
$$
and is positive on $[0,1)$. Also
$$
h(0)=0,
\qquad
h(1)=e^{a-1}>1.
$$
Thus there is a unique $x\in(0,1)$ with $xe^{a-x}=1$. Since the disk zero is unique, it is this real positive solution.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $\Omega$ be a bounded domain in $\mathbb C$, and let $\{f_j\}$ be analytic on $\Omega$ with
$$
\int_\Omega |f_j(z)|^2\,dA(z)\leq 1.
$$
Prove that $\{f_j\}$ is a normal family in $\Omega$.

#proof
Fix a compact set $K\subset\Omega$. Choose $\rho>0$ such that $D(z,\rho)\subset\Omega$ for every $z\in K$. Since $|f_j|^2$ is subharmonic,
$$
|f_j(z)|^2\leq \frac{1}{\pi\rho^2}\int_{D(z,\rho)}|f_j(w)|^2\,dA(w)
\leq \frac{1}{\pi\rho^2}.
$$
Thus the family is uniformly bounded on $K$. Since $K$ was arbitrary, the family is locally uniformly bounded. By Montel's theorem, it is normal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $f:D(0,1)\to\mathbb C$ be bounded and analytic. Let $a_n$ be the nonzero zeros of $f$ in $D(0,1)$, counted with multiplicity. Prove that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}(1-|a_n|)<\infty.
$$

#proof
This is the Blaschke condition. If $f$ has no zeros, there is nothing to prove. Otherwise apply Jensen's formula to $f$:
$$
\log |f(0)|+\sum_{|a_n|<r}\log\frac{r}{|a_n|}
=\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\log|f(re^{i\theta})|\,d\theta.
$$
Since $f$ is bounded, the right-hand side is bounded above uniformly in $r<1$. Letting $r\to 1^-$ gives
$$
\sum_n \log\frac1{|a_n|}<\infty.
$$
For $0<x<1$,
$$
1-x\leq \log\frac1x.
$$
Therefore
$$
\sum_n (1-|a_n|)<\infty.
$$
::
