::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Evaluate
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{1+x^5}\,dx.
$$

#proof

Use the standard formula
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x^b}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{b}\csc\left(\frac{a\pi}{b}\right),
\qquad 0<a<b.
$$
Here $a=2$ and $b=5$, so
$$

\int_0^\infty \frac{x}{1+x^5}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{5}\csc\left(\frac{2\pi}{5}\right).

$$

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Suppose $f$ is analytic in the annulus
$$
A=\{z\in\mathbb C:1/2<|z|<2\},
$$
and there exists a sequence of polynomials $p_n$ converging to $f$ uniformly on the unit circle $|z|=1$. Show that $f$ can be extended analytically to $D(0,2)$.

#proof

Write the Laurent expansion of $f$ in the annulus:
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty}a_kz^k.
$$
For $j\ge1$,
$$
a_{-j}
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=1} f(z)z^{j-1}\,dz.
$$
Since $p_n\to f$ uniformly on $|z|=1$,
$$
a_{-j}
=\lim_{n\to\infty}
\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|z|=1} p_n(z)z^{j-1}\,dz.
$$
But $p_n(z)z^{j-1}$ is holomorphic in the unit disk, so each integral is zero. Hence
$$
a_{-j}=0
$$
for all $j\ge1$.

Thus the Laurent expansion has no negative powers, and $f$ extends holomorphically to $D(0,2)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Prove or disprove: there is a nonzero holomorphic function $f$ on $\mathbb C$ such that
$$
|f(z)|^2\le |\cos z|
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

#proof

No such nonzero function exists.

If the inequality holds, then at every zero of $\cos z$, the function $f$ must vanish. Consider
$$
h(z)=\frac{f(z)^2}{\cos z}.
$$
The zeros of $\cos z$ are simple. Since $f^2$ has zeros of even order, $h$ has removable singularities at the zeros of $\cos z$, so $h$ is entire. The inequality gives
$$
|h(z)|\le1
$$
away from the zeros, and hence everywhere by removability.

By Liouville's theorem, $h$ is constant:
$$
h\equiv c.
$$
Thus
$$
f^2=c\cos z.
$$
If $c\ne0$, then the right side has simple zeros, while the left side has zeros of even order, impossible. Therefore $c=0$, so $f\equiv0$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Let $f$ be meromorphic in $\mathbb C$ such that
$$
|f(z)|=1
$$
on $|z|=1$. Prove that $f$ is rational.

#proof

Define the reflected meromorphic function
$$
f^*(z)=\frac{1}{\overline{f(1/\overline z)}}.
$$
On $|z|=1$, the condition $|f(z)|=1$ gives
$$
f^*(z)=f(z).
$$
By the identity theorem for meromorphic functions,
$$
f(z)=f^*(z)
$$
where both are defined.

This identity shows that the behavior of $f$ at infinity is the reflection of its behavior at $0$. Since $f$ is meromorphic at $0$, it is also meromorphic at infinity. Therefore $f$ is meromorphic on the Riemann sphere.

Every meromorphic function on the Riemann sphere is rational. Hence $f$ is rational.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

Find the radius of convergence of
$$
\sin\left(\frac{2}{(z-2i+2)(z-3+i)}\right)
=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n.
$$

#proof

The sine function is entire, so the only singularities come from the poles of
$$
\frac{2}{(z-2i+2)(z-3+i)}.
$$
These occur at
$$
z=-2+2i
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
z=3-i.
$$
Their moduli are
$$
|-2+2i|=2\sqrt2,
\qquad
|3-i|=\sqrt{10}.
$$
The nearest singularity to $0$ is $-2+2i$, so the radius of convergence is
$$
2\sqrt2.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Prove or disprove: there is a holomorphic function $f$ on $\mathbb C\setminus D(0,3)$ such that
$$
f'(z)=\frac{z^2+1}{z(z-1)(z-2)}.
$$

#proof

Such an $f$ exists if and only if the integral of the proposed derivative around a generator of the exterior domain is zero.

For $|z|=4$,
$$
\int_{|z|=4}\frac{z^2+1}{z(z-1)(z-2)}\,dz
=2\pi i\sum \text{residues at }0,1,2.
$$
The rational function behaves like $1/z$ at infinity, so the sum of the finite residues is $1$. Hence the integral is
$$
2\pi i\ne0.
$$
Therefore the function has no primitive on $\mathbb C\setminus D(0,3)$. No such holomorphic $f$ exists.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $f$ be analytic on the upper half-plane and satisfy $|f(z)|<1$. Suppose
$$
f(2+i)=0.
$$
Give an upper bound for $|f'(2+i)|$ and state which functions realize the extremum.

#proof

Use the Schwarz-Pick lemma on the upper half-plane. The automorphism
$$
\phi(z)=\frac{z-(2+i)}{z-(2-i)}
$$
maps the upper half-plane to the unit disk and sends $2+i$ to $0$. Since $f(2+i)=0$, Schwarz's lemma applied to $f\circ\phi^{-1}$ gives
$$
|f'(2+i)|\le \frac12.
$$
Equality occurs exactly for rotations:
$$

f(z)=e^{i\theta}\frac{z-(2+i)}{z-(2-i)},
\qquad \theta\in\mathbb R.

$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Let $u$ be real-valued and harmonic in $D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ such that
$$
\lim_{z\to0}\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}=0.
$$
Show that there is a harmonic function $U$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
u(z)=U(z)
$$
for all $z\in D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$.

#proof

An isolated singularity of a harmonic function has the form
$$
u(z)=a\log|z|+\operatorname{Re}\left(\sum_{k=1}^{N}c_kz^{-k}\right)+h(z),
$$
where $h$ is harmonic near $0$, unless the singularity is removable.

The hypothesis
$$
\frac{u(z)}{\log|z|}\to0
$$
forces $a=0$ and rules out every principal part term, since those grow faster than $|\log|z||$ along suitable directions.

Thus the singular part vanishes, and $u$ has a removable singularity at $0$. The resulting extension $U$ is harmonic on $D(0,1)$.
::
