# 2015 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Prove that the series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n^2}
$$
converges uniformly in $|z|\leq 1$. Does the term-by-term derivative converge uniformly in the unit disk?

#proof
For $|z|\leq 1$,
$$
\left|\frac{z^n}{n^2}\right|\leq \frac{1}{n^2}.
$$
Since $\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}1/n^2$ converges, the Weierstrass $M$-test shows that
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^n}{n^2}
$$
converges uniformly on the closed unit disk $|z|\leq 1$.

The term-by-term derivative is
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{z^{n-1}}{n}.
$$
This series does not converge uniformly on the closed unit disk, because at $z=1$ it becomes the harmonic series
$$
\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\frac{1}{n},
$$
which diverges. Thus uniform convergence on $|z|\leq 1$ is impossible.

On every smaller disk $|z|\leq r<1$, however,
$$
\left|\frac{z^{n-1}}{n}\right|\leq r^{n-1},
$$
and $\sum r^{n-1}$ converges. Therefore the derivative series converges uniformly on compact subsets of the open unit disk, but not uniformly on the whole closed unit disk.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $f$ be an entire function. Suppose there exists a bounded sequence $\{a_n\}$ of real numbers such that $f(a_n)$ is real for all $n$. Prove that $f(x)$ is real for all real $x$.

#proof
There is a small hypothesis issue in the statement as written: if the bounded sequence is eventually constant, the conclusion need not follow. The intended argument requires the sequence to have a real accumulation point with infinitely many distinct terms.

Under that intended interpretation, since $\{a_n\}$ is bounded, it has a convergent subsequence. Passing to a subsequence of distinct terms, write
$$
a_{n_k}\to a\in \mathbb R.
$$
Define
$$
g(z)=f(z)-\overline{f(\overline z)}.
$$
Because $f$ is entire, the function $z\mapsto \overline{f(\overline z)}$ is also entire. Hence $g$ is entire.

For each $k$, $a_{n_k}$ is real, so
$$
g(a_{n_k})=f(a_{n_k})-\overline{f(a_{n_k})}=0,
$$
because $f(a_{n_k})$ is real. Thus the zeros of the entire function $g$ have an accumulation point $a$ in the complex plane. By the identity theorem,
$$
g\equiv 0.
$$
Therefore, for every real $x$,
$$
0=g(x)=f(x)-\overline{f(x)}.
$$
So $f(x)=\overline{f(x)}$, which means $f(x)$ is real for all real $x$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Evaluate
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^2+b^2)}\,dx,
$$
where $a>0$ and $b>0$.

#proof
Let
$$
I(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^2+b^2)}\,dx.
$$
Differentiate with respect to $a$:
$$
I'(a)=\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos(ax)}{x^2+b^2}\,dx.
$$
The standard residue computation gives, for $a>0$,
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{e^{iax}}{x^2+b^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{b}e^{-ab}.
$$
Taking real parts and using evenness,
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{\cos(ax)}{x^2+b^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2b}e^{-ab}.
$$
Therefore
$$
I'(a)=\frac{\pi}{2b}e^{-ab}.
$$
Also $I(0)=0$. Hence
$$
I(a)=\int_0^a I'(t)\,dt
=\frac{\pi}{2b}\int_0^a e^{-bt}\,dt
=\frac{\pi}{2b^2}(1-e^{-ab}).
$$
Thus
$$
\displaystyle \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(ax)}{x(x^2+b^2)}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{2b^2}(1-e^{-ab}).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
How many roots does
$$
z+e^{-z}=a,
$$
where $a\in \mathbb R$ and $a>1$, have in the right half-plane?

#proof
Write
$$
F(z)=z+e^{-z}-a=(z-a)+e^{-z}.
$$
We count zeros in the right half-plane by Rouche's theorem on the large right half-disk
$$
D_R=\{z:\operatorname{Re} z>0,\ |z|<R\}.
$$
On the imaginary-axis part of the boundary, $z=iy$, so
$$
|z-a|=|iy-a|=\sqrt{a^2+y^2}>1=|e^{-iy}|,
$$
because $a>1$.

On the semicircular part $|z|=R$, $\operatorname{Re}z\geq 0$, we have
$$
|e^{-z}|=e^{-\operatorname{Re}z}\leq 1,
$$
while $|z-a|\geq R-a$. For $R>a+1$,
$$
|z-a|>|e^{-z}|.
$$
Thus, on the boundary of $D_R$,
$$
|e^{-z}|<|z-a|.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $F(z)$ and $z-a$ have the same number of zeros in $D_R$.

The function $z-a$ has exactly one zero, namely $z=a$, and since $a>1$, this zero lies in the right half-plane. Therefore
$$
z+e^{-z}=a
$$
has exactly one root in the right half-plane.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Find a real-valued function $u$, continuous on $|z|\leq R$ and harmonic in $|z|<R$, such that
$$
u(Re^{i\theta})=\frac12(1+\cos 3\theta).
$$

#proof
The boundary data have Fourier expansion
$$
\frac12(1+\cos 3\theta)=\frac12+\frac12\cos 3\theta.
$$
For the disk of radius $R$, the harmonic function with boundary term $\cos n\theta$ is
$$
\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^n\cos n\theta.
$$
Therefore
$$
u(re^{i\theta})
=\frac12+\frac12\left(\frac{r}{R}\right)^3\cos 3\theta.
$$
This function is continuous on $|z|\leq R$, harmonic for $|z|<R$, real-valued, and has the required boundary values.

Equivalently, since $z=re^{i\theta}$,
$$
u(z)=\frac12+\frac12\operatorname{Re}\left(\frac{z^3}{R^3}\right).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Let
$$
P(z)=a_nz^n+a_{n-1}z^{n-1}+\cdots+a_1z+a_0,
$$
where $a_0a_n\neq 0$. Put
$$
B=\max_{0\leq j\leq n-1}|a_j|,
\qquad
C=\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|a_j|.
$$
Prove that all roots of $P$ lie in the annulus
$$
\frac{1}{1+C/|a_0|}\leq |z|\leq 1+\frac{B}{|a_n|}.
$$

#proof
First prove the upper bound. Suppose $|z|>1+B/|a_n|$. Then $|z|>1$, and
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|a_j||z|^j
\leq B\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|z|^j
=B\frac{|z|^n-1}{|z|-1}
<\frac{B|z|^n}{|z|-1}.
$$
Since $|z|-1>B/|a_n|$, we have
$$
\frac{B}{|z|-1}<|a_n|.
$$
Thus
$$
\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}|a_j||z|^j<|a_n||z|^n.
$$
So the leading term cannot be cancelled by the remaining terms, and $P(z)\neq 0$. Hence every root satisfies
$$
|z|\leq 1+\frac{B}{|a_n|}.
$$

For the lower bound, apply the upper-bound result to the reciprocal polynomial
$$
Q(w)=w^nP(1/w)=a_0w^n+a_1w^{n-1}+\cdots+a_{n-1}w+a_n.
$$
The leading coefficient of $Q$ is $a_0$, and the maximum of the absolute values of its lower coefficients is
$$
\max_{1\leq j\leq n}|a_j|=C.
$$
Therefore every zero $w$ of $Q$ satisfies
$$
|w|\leq 1+\frac{C}{|a_0|}.
$$
If $z$ is a zero of $P$, then $z\neq 0$ and $w=1/z$ is a zero of $Q$. Hence
$$
\frac1{|z|}\leq 1+\frac{C}{|a_0|},
$$
so
$$
|z|\geq \frac{1}{1+C/|a_0|}.
$$
Combining the two inequalities gives the stated annulus.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
True or false: the family of holomorphic functions in the unit disk with power series
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n
$$
satisfying
$$
|a_n|\leq n^{2015}
$$
is a normal family.

#proof
The statement is true.

Fix $0<r<1$. For $|z|\leq r$,
$$
|a_nz^n|\leq n^{2015}r^n.
$$
The numerical series
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty} n^{2015}r^n
$$
converges for every $r<1$. Therefore the family is uniformly bounded on each compact disk $|z|\leq r<1$.

By Montel's theorem, every locally bounded family of holomorphic functions on the unit disk is normal. Hence this family is normal.

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let
$$
U_1=\{z:1<|z|<2\},
\qquad
U_2=\{z:0<|z|<1\}.
$$
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Show that
$$
f(re^{i\theta})=(r-1)e^{i\theta}
$$
is a homeomorphism from $U_1$ to $U_2$ but is not conformal.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Does there exist a conformal mapping from $U_1$ to $U_2$?

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The map sends $1<r<2$ to $0<r-1<1$ and preserves the angle $\theta$, so it is a one-to-one continuous map from $U_1$ onto $U_2$. Its inverse is
$$
\rho e^{i\theta}\mapsto (1+\rho)e^{i\theta},
\qquad 0<\rho<1,
$$
which is also continuous. Thus $f$ is a homeomorphism.

It is not conformal. In polar coordinates, a conformal radial map preserving angle has the form
$$
re^{i\theta}\mapsto \phi(r)e^{i\theta}
$$
with radial and angular scale factors equal:
$$
|\phi'(r)|=\frac{\phi(r)}{r}.
$$
Here $\phi(r)=r-1$, so $\phi'(r)=1$, while
$$
\frac{\phi(r)}{r}=\frac{r-1}{r}.
$$
These are not equal for $1<r<2$. Hence the map does not preserve infinitesimal angles and is not conformal.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** No. The annulus $U_1=\{1<|z|<2\}$ has finite conformal modulus
$$
\operatorname{mod}(U_1)=\frac{1}{2\pi}\log 2.
$$
The punctured unit disk $U_2=\{0<|z|<1\}$ has infinite modulus; it is conformally equivalent to a punctured disk with the inner radius tending to $0$.

Conformal modulus is invariant under conformal maps between doubly connected domains. Since the moduli are different, no conformal mapping $U_1\to U_2$ exists.
::
