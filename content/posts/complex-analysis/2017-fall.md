# 2017 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Let $u$ be a real-valued continuous function on $\mathbb C$ such that $e^{u(z)}$ is harmonic in $\mathbb C$. Prove that $u$ is constant.

#proof

The function
$$
v(z)=e^{u(z)}
$$
is positive and harmonic on all of $\mathbb C$. A positive harmonic function on the plane is constant. Indeed, by Harnack's inequality applied on disks of radius $R$ and then letting $R\to\infty$, one obtains
$$
v(z)=v(0)
$$
for every $z\in\mathbb C$.

Thus $e^{u(z)}$ is constant. Since the exponential is injective on real numbers, $u$ is constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Prove or disprove: there is a holomorphic function $f$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
f\left(\frac1{2n}\right)
=
f\left(\frac1{2n+1}\right)
=\frac1n
$$
for all positive integers $n$.

#proof

No such function exists.

If such an $f$ existed, then for the sequence $z_n=1/(2n)$,
$$
f(z_n)=\frac1n=2z_n.
$$
Thus the holomorphic function
$$
h(z)=f(z)-2z
$$
has zeros at $z_n=1/(2n)$, and these zeros accumulate at $0\in D(0,1)$. By the identity theorem,
$$
h\equiv0,
$$
so
$$
f(z)=2z.
$$
But then
$$
f\left(\frac1{2n+1}\right)=\frac{2}{2n+1}\ne\frac1n.
$$
This contradiction proves that no such holomorphic function exists.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Let $f$ be entire and suppose $f(x)$ and $f(ix)$ are real for $x\in(1,2)$. Prove there is an entire function $g$ such that
$$
f(z)=g(z^2)
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

#proof

Since $f(x)$ is real for $x\in(1,2)$, the Schwarz reflection identity gives
$$
f(\overline z)=\overline{f(z)}
$$
for all $z\in\mathbb C$.

For $x\in(1,2)$, the number $f(ix)$ is real. Hence
$$
f(-ix)=f(\overline{ix})=\overline{f(ix)}=f(ix).
$$
Thus the entire function
$$
h(z)=f(z)-f(-z)
$$
vanishes on the segment $\{ix:1<x<2\}$. By the identity theorem,
$$
h\equiv0.
$$
So $f$ is even.

Write the Taylor series
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n.
$$
Since $f$ is even, all odd coefficients vanish. Therefore
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{2n}z^{2n}.
$$
Define
$$
g(w)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_{2n}w^n.
$$
This is entire, and
$$
f(z)=g(z^2).
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Let $z_1,\ldots,z_n\in D(0,R)$ and
$$
Q(z)=(z-z_1)\cdots(z-z_n).
$$
Let $f$ be holomorphic on $D(0,R)$. Prove that
$$
P(z)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=R}
\frac{Q(w)-Q(z)}{(w-z)Q(w)}f(w)\,dw
$$
is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$ such that
$$
P(z_j)=f(z_j)
$$
for $1\le j\le n$.

#proof

For fixed $w$, the expression
$$
\frac{Q(w)-Q(z)}{w-z}
$$
is a polynomial in $z$ of degree at most $n-1$. Therefore $P(z)$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n-1$.

Now evaluate at $z=z_j$. Since $Q(z_j)=0$,
$$
P(z_j)=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=R}
\frac{Q(w)}{(w-z_j)Q(w)}f(w)\,dw
=\frac1{2\pi i}\int_{|w|=R}\frac{f(w)}{w-z_j}\,dw.
$$
By Cauchy's integral formula,
$$
P(z_j)=f(z_j).
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

For $a>1$, prove that
$$
ze^{a-z}=1
$$
has a unique solution in $|z|\le1$, and that this solution is real and positive.

#proof

The equation is equivalent to
$$
z=e^{z-a}.
$$
On $|z|=1$,
$$
|e^{z-a}|=e^{\operatorname{Re}z-a}\le e^{1-a}<1=|z|.
$$
By Rouche's theorem, $z-e^{z-a}$ and $z$ have the same number of zeros in $|z|<1$. Hence there is exactly one solution in the unit disk.

Now consider the real function
$$
\phi(x)=xe^{a-x}
$$
on $[0,1]$. We have
$$
\phi(0)=0,\qquad \phi(1)=e^{a-1}>1.
$$
Also,
$$
\phi'(x)=e^{a-x}(1-x)>0
$$
for $0<x<1$. Therefore there is a unique $x\in(0,1)$ such that
$$
xe^{a-x}=1.
$$
This real positive solution must be the unique solution in $|z|\le1$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Prove that
$$
\int_0^\infty\frac{(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx
=\frac{\pi^3}{8}.
$$

#proof

For $0<s<2$, let
$$
I(s)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
The beta-integral gives
$$
I(s)=\frac{\pi}{2}\csc\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right).
$$
Differentiating twice under the integral sign,
$$
I''(s)=\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{s-1}(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
At $s=1$,
$$
I''(1)=\int_0^\infty\frac{(\log x)^2}{1+x^2}\,dx.
$$
Since
$$
\left.\frac{d^2}{ds^2}\left[\frac{\pi}{2}\csc\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\right]\right|_{s=1}
=\frac{\pi^3}{8},
$$
the desired integral is
$$
\frac{\pi^3}{8}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $F$ be a family of holomorphic functions $f$ on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
|f(0)|^2+\int_{D(0,1)}|f'(z)|^2\,dA\le1.
$$
Prove that $F$ is a normal family.

#proof

We prove local boundedness. Fix $0<r<1$. For $|z|\le r$,
$$
f(z)=f(0)+\int_0^1 f'(tz)z\,dt.
$$
Using Cauchy-Schwarz along the segment and then the area $L^2$ bound locally gives
$$
|f(z)|\le |f(0)|+C_r\left(\int_{D(0,1)}|f'(w)|^2\,dA(w)\right)^{1/2}.
$$
Since both terms are bounded by the hypothesis, there is a constant $M_r$ such that
$$
|f(z)|\le M_r
$$
for all $|z|\le r$ and all $f\in F$.

Thus $F$ is locally bounded. By Montel's theorem, $F$ is normal.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Let $\{f_n\}_{n=1}^{\infty}$ be holomorphic on $D(0,1)$ such that
$$
F(z)=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_n(z)|
$$
defines a continuous function in $D(0,1)$ and
$$
F(0)\ge F(z)
$$
for all $z\in D(0,1)$. Prove that every $f_n$ is constant.

#proof

For $0<r<1$, the mean-value inequality gives
$$
|f_n(0)|\le \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f_n(re^{it})|\,dt.
$$
Summing over $n$ and using monotone convergence,
$$
F(0)
=\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_n(0)|
\le
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}|f_n(re^{it})|\,dt
=
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}F(re^{it})\,dt.
$$
But $F(z)\le F(0)$ everywhere, so the average on the right is at most $F(0)$. Hence equality holds throughout.

Therefore, for each $n$,
$$
|f_n(0)|=
\frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}|f_n(re^{it})|\,dt.
$$
Equality in the mean-value inequality for $|f_n|$ implies that $|f_n|$ is harmonic and constant on $D(0,r)$. Thus $f_n$ is constant on $D(0,r)$.

Since $r<1$ was arbitrary, every $f_n$ is constant on $D(0,1)$.
::
