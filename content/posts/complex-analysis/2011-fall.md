# 2011 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem
Describe all entire holomorphic functions $f$ and $g$ such that:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** $f(1/n)=f(-1/n)=1/n^2$ for all positive integers $n$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** $g(1/n)=g(-1/n)=1/n^3$ for all positive integers $n$.

#proof
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** The function
$$
f(z)=z^2
$$
has the required values. If another entire function has the same values, then $f(z)-z^2$ vanishes at $z=\pm 1/n$ for every positive integer $n$. These zeros accumulate at $0$, so by the identity theorem,
$$
f(z)\equiv z^2.
$$
Thus the only possibility is
$$
f(z)=z^2.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** No such entire function exists.

Indeed, the condition
$$
g(1/n)=g(-1/n)
$$
for all $n$ implies by the identity theorem that $g$ is even:
$$
g(z)=g(-z).
$$
Hence there is an entire function $h$ such that
$$
g(z)=h(z^2).
$$
Then
$$
h(1/n^2)=1/n^3.
$$
Let $w_n=1/n^2$. Then $w_n\to 0$ and
$$
h(w_n)=w_n^{3/2}.
$$
This gives $h(0)=0$ and
$$
\frac{h(w_n)}{w_n}=\frac1n\to 0,
$$
so $h'(0)=0$. Hence $h(w)=O(w^2)$ near $0$. But
$$
\frac{h(w_n)}{w_n^2}=n\to\infty,
$$
contradicting $h(w)=O(w^2)$. Therefore no entire $g$ satisfies the stated condition.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem
Let $f(z)$ be an entire holomorphic function such that
$$
\lim_{z\to\infty}\frac{|f(z)|}{|z|}=0.
$$
Prove that $f$ is constant.

#proof
Write
$$
f(z)=\sum_{k=0}^{\infty}a_kz^k.
$$
Fix $\varepsilon>0$. For all sufficiently large $R$,
$$
|f(z)|\leq \varepsilon |z|
$$
on $|z|=R$. By Cauchy's estimate,
$$
|a_k|\leq \frac{\max_{|z|=R}|f(z)|}{R^k}
\leq \varepsilon R^{1-k}.
$$
For $k=1$, this gives $|a_1|\leq\varepsilon$, so $a_1=0$. For $k\geq 2$, letting $R\to\infty$ gives $a_k=0$.

Thus all nonconstant Taylor coefficients vanish, and $f$ is constant.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem
Evaluate
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{x^{1/3}(1+x)}.
$$

#proof
Use the standard formula
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{x^{a-1}}{1+x}\,dx
=\frac{\pi}{\sin \pi a},
\qquad 0<a<1.
$$
Here
$$
x^{-1/3}=x^{2/3-1},
$$
so $a=2/3$. Therefore
$$
\int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{x^{1/3}(1+x)}
=\frac{\pi}{\sin(2\pi/3)}
=\frac{\pi}{\sqrt3/2}
=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}.
$$
Thus
$$
\displaystyle \int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{x^{1/3}(1+x)}
=\frac{2\pi}{\sqrt3}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem
<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** Show that there is an analytic function defined in
$$
\Omega=\{z\in\mathbb C:|z|>4\}
$$
whose derivative is
$$
\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}.
$$

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** Does there exist an analytic function in $\Omega$ with derivative
$$
\frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)}?
$$

#proof
On the exterior domain $\Omega$, a holomorphic function has a primitive if and only if its integral around one large positively oriented circle is $0$. Equivalently, the coefficient of $1/z$ in its Laurent expansion at infinity must be $0$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(a)** For
$$
F(z)=\frac{z}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)},
$$
we have
$$
F(z)=O(z^{-2})
\qquad (z\to\infty).
$$
Thus the Laurent expansion at infinity has no $1/z$ term. Therefore the integral of $F$ around every closed curve in $\Omega$ is $0$, and $F$ has a primitive in $\Omega$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(b)** For
$$
G(z)=\frac{z^2}{(z-1)(z-2)(z-3)},
$$
we have
$$
G(z)=\frac1z+O(z^{-2})
\qquad (z\to\infty).
$$
Therefore the coefficient of $1/z$ is $1$, so
$$
\int_{|z|=R}G(z)\,dz=2\pi i
$$
for $R>4$. Hence $G$ does not have a primitive on $\Omega$.

So the answer to part **(b)** is no.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem
Let $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb C$ be continuous. Define
$$
F(z)=\int_0^1 \frac{f(t)}{t-z}\,dt,
\qquad z\in\mathbb C\setminus[0,1].
$$
Prove that $F$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$.

#proof
Let $K$ be a compact subset of $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$. Then there is $\delta>0$ such that
$$
|t-z|\geq \delta
$$
for all $t\in[0,1]$ and $z\in K$.

For each fixed $t$, the function
$$
z\mapsto \frac{f(t)}{t-z}
$$
is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$. On $K$, its derivative with respect to $z$ is
$$
\frac{f(t)}{(t-z)^2},
$$
and this is uniformly bounded by
$$
\frac{\|f\|_\infty}{\delta^2}.
$$
Thus differentiation under the integral sign is justified on compact subsets of $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$.

Therefore
$$
F'(z)=\int_0^1 \frac{f(t)}{(t-z)^2}\,dt,
$$
and $F$ is holomorphic on $\mathbb C\setminus[0,1]$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem
Prove the Schwarz-Pick lemma: if $f:D(0,1)\to D(0,1)$ is holomorphic, then
$$
\left|\frac{f(z)-f(a)}{1-\overline{f(a)}f(z)}\right|
\leq
\left|\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}\right|,
\qquad a,z\in D(0,1).
$$

#proof
For $a\in D(0,1)$, define the disk automorphism
$$
\phi_a(z)=\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}.
$$
Also define
$$
\phi_{f(a)}(w)=\frac{w-f(a)}{1-\overline{f(a)}w}.
$$
Then the function
$$
g=\phi_{f(a)}\circ f\circ \phi_a^{-1}
$$
maps $D(0,1)$ holomorphically into itself and satisfies
$$
g(0)=0.
$$
By the Schwarz lemma,
$$
|g(\zeta)|\leq |\zeta|
$$
for all $\zeta\in D(0,1)$.

Now take
$$
\zeta=\phi_a(z).
$$
Then
$$
g(\phi_a(z))=\phi_{f(a)}(f(z)).
$$
Thus
$$
|\phi_{f(a)}(f(z))|\leq |\phi_a(z)|,
$$
which is exactly
$$
\left|\frac{f(z)-f(a)}{1-\overline{f(a)}f(z)}\right|
\leq
\left|\frac{z-a}{1-\overline a z}\right|.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem
Let $f$ be holomorphic in $D(0,1)$ and let
$$
M(r,f)=\int_0^{2\pi}|f(re^{i\theta})|^2\,d\theta.
$$
Prove that $M(r,f)$ is an increasing convex function of $r$ on $[0,1)$.

#proof
Write the Taylor expansion
$$
f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n.
$$
For $0\leq r<1$, Parseval's identity gives
$$
M(r,f)=\int_0^{2\pi}\left|\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nr^ne^{in\theta}\right|^2\,d\theta
=2\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_n|^2r^{2n}.
$$
Each function $r^{2n}$ is increasing on $[0,1)$, and for $n\geq 1$ it is convex there. The $n=0$ term is constant.

Since the series has nonnegative coefficients and converges locally uniformly in $r$, the sum
$$
M(r,f)=2\pi\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}|a_n|^2r^{2n}
$$
is increasing and convex on $[0,1)$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem
Let $f$ be meromorphic in $D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}$ such that
$$
\int_{D(0,1)\setminus\{0\}} |f(z)|^3\,dA(z)\leq 1.
$$
Prove that $z=0$ is a removable singularity of $f$.

#proof
Since $f$ is meromorphic near $0$, the isolated singularity is either removable or a pole. Suppose it is a pole of order $m\geq 1$. Then near $0$,
$$
|f(z)|\sim C|z|^{-m}
$$
for some $C>0$. Hence
$$
|f(z)|^3\sim C^3|z|^{-3m}.
$$
In polar coordinates,
$$
\int_{|z|<\varepsilon}|f(z)|^3\,dA(z)
$$
has the same convergence behavior as
$$
\int_0^\varepsilon r^{1-3m}\,dr.
$$
This integral diverges for every integer $m\geq 1$.

Therefore $0$ cannot be a pole. The only remaining possibility is that $0$ is removable.
::
