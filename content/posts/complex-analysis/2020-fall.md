# 2020 Fall Qualifying Exam in Complex Analysis

::ProblemBlock{number=1}
#problem

Prove that there exists no holomorphic function $f$ such that
$$
f(z)=u(x,y)+(2+3i)y^2,\qquad z=x+iy,
$$
where $u(x,y)$ is real-valued.

#proof

If such an $f$ existed, then
$$
f=(u+2y^2)+i(3y^2).
$$
Thus the imaginary part would be
$$
v(x,y)=3y^2.
$$
The Cauchy-Riemann equations give
$$
(u+2y^2)_x=v_y=6y
$$
and
$$
(u+2y^2)_y=-v_x=0.
$$
The first equation says
$$
u_x=6y,
$$
while the second says
$$
u_y=-4y.
$$
Then
$$
u_{xy}=6,
\qquad
u_{yx}=\frac{\partial}{\partial x}(-4y)=0,
$$
which is impossible for a $C^2$ function. Therefore no such holomorphic function exists.

::ProblemBlock{number=2}
#problem

Let $f$ be entire and suppose
$$
|f(z)|\ge |z|^\alpha
$$
for all $|z|\le1$, where
$$
\alpha=\frac{2019}{2020}.
$$
Prove that
$$
|f(0)|\ge1.
$$

#proof

First, $f$ has no zeros in $D(0,1)$. Indeed, if $f$ had a zero of order $m\ge1$ at some point in the disk, and in particular if $f(0)=0$, the lower bound near that zero would be impossible. At $0$, for example, a zero of order $m$ would give
$$
|f(z)|\le C|z|^m,
$$
but $m\ge1>\alpha$, so this contradicts
$$
|f(z)|\ge |z|^\alpha
$$
for small $z$.

Thus $1/f$ is holomorphic in $D(0,1)$. On $|z|=1$,
$$
|f(z)|\ge1,
$$
so
$$
\left|\frac1{f(z)}\right|\le1.
$$
By the maximum modulus principle,
$$
\left|\frac1{f(0)}\right|\le1.
$$
Therefore
$$
|f(0)|\ge1.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=3}
#problem

Prove that for any $a\in\mathbb C$ and $n\ge2$, the polynomial
$$
z^n+az+a
$$
has at least one root in the disk
$$
\{|z|\le2\}.
$$

#proof

If $a=0$, then $z=0$ is a root. Assume $a\ne0$.

Let the roots be $\alpha_1,\ldots,\alpha_n$. Suppose, toward a contradiction, that
$$
|\alpha_j|>2
$$
for every $j$. Put
$$
\beta_j=\frac1{\alpha_j}.
$$
Then $|\beta_j|<1/2$.

The reciprocal polynomial is
$$
1+az^{n-1}+az^n.
$$
After dividing by $a$, its roots are $\beta_j$, and it has the form
$$
z^n+z^{n-1}+\frac1a.
$$
Thus the elementary symmetric functions satisfy
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n\beta_j=-1
$$
and
$$
e_2(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)=\cdots=e_{n-1}(\beta_1,\ldots,\beta_n)=0.
$$
Newton's identities then imply
$$
\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j^{\,n-1}=(-1)^{n-1}.
$$
But
$$
\left|\sum_{j=1}^n \beta_j^{\,n-1}\right|
\le n\left(\frac12\right)^{n-1}<1
$$
for $n\ge3$, a contradiction. For $n=2$, the condition
$$
\beta_1+\beta_2=-1
$$
is already impossible with both $|\beta_j|<1/2$.

Therefore at least one root satisfies $|\alpha_j|\le2$.
::

::ProblemBlock{number=4}
#problem

Evaluate
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x(x^2-\pi^2)}\,dx.
$$

#proof

Use the partial fraction decomposition
$$
\frac1{x(x^2-\pi^2)}
=-\frac1{\pi^2x}
\frac1{2\pi^2}\left(\frac1{x-\pi}+\frac1{x+\pi}\right).
$$
The apparent singularities are removable in the original integrand. We use
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x}\,dx=\pi
$$
and, in the principal-value sense,
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x-a}\,dx=\pi\cos a.
$$
Therefore
$$
\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}\frac{\sin x}{x(x^2-\pi^2)}\,dx
=-\frac1{\pi^2}\pi
\frac1{2\pi^2}\bigl(\pi\cos\pi+\pi\cos(-\pi)\bigr).
$$
Since $\cos\pi=\cos(-\pi)=-1$, this becomes
$$
-\frac1\pi-\frac1\pi
=-\frac2\pi.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=5}
#problem

Suppose the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}a_nz^n
$$
is equal to $2$. Find the radius of convergence of
$$
\sum_{n=0}^{\infty}
\sqrt[2n]{|a_{2n}|+|a_{2n+1}|}\,z^{3n}.
$$

#proof

Since the original radius of convergence is $2$,
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}|a_n|^{1/n}=\frac12.
$$
It follows that
$$
\limsup_{n\to\infty}
\left(|a_{2n}|+|a_{2n+1}|\right)^{1/(2n)}
=\frac12.
$$
Let
$$
b_n=\sqrt[2n]{|a_{2n}|+|a_{2n+1}|}.
$$
Then
$$
\limsup b_n=\frac12.
$$
The new series is
$$
\sum b_n(z^3)^n.
$$
As a power series in $w=z^3$, it has radius
$$
\frac1{\limsup b_n}=2.
$$
Therefore it converges when
$$
|z|^3<2.
$$
The radius of convergence in $z$ is
$$
\sqrt[3]{2}.
$$
::

::ProblemBlock{number=6}
#problem

Find explicitly a conformal mapping of
$$
\{|z|<1,\ \operatorname{Re}z>0,\ z\notin[0,1/2]\}
$$
onto the unit disk.

#proof

One explicit construction is as follows. First square:
$$
w=z^2.
$$
This maps the right half of the unit disk with the slit $[0,1/2]$ removed onto
$$
D(0,1)\setminus[0,1/4].
$$
Next use the disk automorphism
$$
\eta=\frac{w-\frac14}{1-\frac14w},
$$
which sends the slit $[0,1/4]$ to a slit on the negative real axis. Choose the branch of the square root on this slit disk with positive imaginary part on the upper side of the slit:
$$
\xi=\sqrt{\eta}.
$$
This opens the slit and maps the domain conformally to a half-disk. A final Mobius transformation maps that half-disk to the unit disk. Thus one explicit map is
$$

\Phi(z)=
\frac{
\left(\sqrt{\frac{z^2-\frac14}{1-\frac14z^2}}\right)-i
}{
\left(\sqrt{\frac{z^2-\frac14}{1-\frac14z^2}}\right)+i
},

$$
with the square-root branch chosen as above. This is a composition of conformal maps and hence is conformal onto the unit disk.

::ProblemBlock{number=7}
#problem

Let $F_i$, $i=1,2,3$, be families of holomorphic functions on $D=\{|z|<1\}$:
$$
F_1=\{f:D\to\mathbb C: f \text{ is a polynomial whose coefficients are bounded by }1\},
$$
$$
F_2=\{f:D\to\mathbb C: f \text{ is a polynomial of degree at most }2020\},
$$
and
$$
F_3=F_1\cap F_2.
$$
Which of $F_1,F_2,F_3$ are normal?

#proof

The family $F_1$ is normal. Indeed, if
$$
f(z)=\sum a_nz^n
$$
with $|a_n|\le1$, then for $|z|\le r<1$,
$$
|f(z)|\le \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}r^n=\frac1{1-r}.
$$
Thus $F_1$ is locally bounded, hence normal by Montel's theorem.

The family $F_2$ is not normal. For example, the constant polynomials
$$
f_m(z)=m
$$
belong to $F_2$, but they are not locally bounded, so the family is not normal as a family of holomorphic functions into $\mathbb C$.

The family $F_3$ is normal because $F_3\subset F_1$, and every subfamily of a normal family is normal.

Thus
$$
F_1\text{ and }F_3\text{ are normal, while }F_2\text{ is not.}
$$

::ProblemBlock{number=8}
#problem

Let $U\subsetneq\mathbb C$ be simply connected and open. Suppose $f:U\to U$ is holomorphic and not identically equal to $z$. Prove or disprove:

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(1)** There exists at most one fixed point of $f$ in $U$.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(2)** There exists exactly one fixed point of $f$ in $U$.

#proof

Since $U$ is simply connected and proper, by the Riemann mapping theorem there is a conformal map
$$
\phi:U\to D.
$$
Then
$$
g=\phi\circ f\circ \phi^{-1}
$$
is a holomorphic self-map of $D$ and is not the identity.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(1)** This is true. If $g$ had two distinct fixed points in $D$, then by the Schwarz-Pick lemma $g$ would have to be the identity. Hence $f$ would be the identity, contrary to the assumption. Therefore $f$ has at most one fixed point.

<span style="display:inline-block; width:1em;"></span> **(2)** This is false. For example, on $D$ take
$$
g(z)=\frac{z+1}{2}.
$$
This maps $D$ into $D$, but its only fixed point is $z=1$, which lies on the boundary and not in $D$. Thus it has no fixed point in $D$.

Conjugating this example by a Riemann map gives a counterexample on any simply connected proper domain $U$.
::
